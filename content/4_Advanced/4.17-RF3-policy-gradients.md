## Overview

Q-learning finds a policy indirectly: learn $Q$, then act greedily. That works well when actions are few and discrete, but it has trouble otherwise. If the actions are **continuous**, such as how far to turn a steering wheel, finding $\arg\max_a Q(s,a)$ is its own optimization problem at every step. And a greedy policy is always deterministic, which is no good when the best play is random, as in rock-paper-scissors or any game where being predictable gets punished.

**Policy gradient** methods skip the middleman. Parameterize the policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ directly, a neural network whose output is a distribution over actions, and do gradient *ascent* on expected return.

This also happens to be how language models are fine-tuned from human feedback (RLHF): the language model *is* $\pi_\theta$, tokens are actions, and PPO, described below, is one optimizer used for it.

## The objective, and the trick that makes it differentiable

Write a trajectory as $\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots)$ with return $R(\tau)$. The objective is expected return:

$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ R(\tau) \right]
= \int P(\tau; \theta)\, R(\tau)\, d\tau
$$

The difficulty is immediate: $\theta$ sits inside the *distribution* we are averaging over, not inside the function being averaged. We cannot simply push $\nabla_\theta$ onto $R$, which may not be differentiable at all.

The **log-derivative trick** resolves it. From $\nabla \log P = \nabla P / P$ we get $\nabla P = P \nabla \log P$, so:

$$
\nabla_\theta J = \int \nabla_\theta P(\tau;\theta) R(\tau) d\tau
= \int P(\tau;\theta)\, \nabla_\theta \log P(\tau;\theta)\, R(\tau)\, d\tau
$$

$$
= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}
\left[ \nabla_\theta \log P(\tau;\theta)\, R(\tau) \right]
$$

An integral we could not compute has become an **expectation we can sample**.

### Why the dynamics disappear

Now expand the trajectory probability:

$$
P(\tau;\theta) = p(s_0) \prod_{t} \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,
p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)
$$

Take logs, turning the product into a sum:

$$
\log P(\tau;\theta) = \log p(s_0) + \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)
+ \sum_t \log p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)
$$

and differentiate. The initial-state term and **every transition term** are constants with respect to $\theta$, because the environment's dynamics do not depend on the agent's parameters. So their gradients vanish:

$$
\nabla_\theta \log P(\tau;\theta)
= \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)
$$

So the gradient of the objective depends only on the gradient of the **policy**, which we chose and can differentiate. No transition model appears anywhere, which is what makes the method model-free:

$$
\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\left[
\left( \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \right) R(\tau)
\right]
$$

Sampling trajectories and averaging gives **REINFORCE**. The update has an intuitive reading: $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ is the direction that makes action $a_t$ more likely, and it is scaled by the return. Good trajectories reinforce their actions. Bad ones suppress them.

### Only future rewards

One correction follows from causality. As written, every action is weighted by the return of the *whole* trajectory, including reward collected **before** that action was taken. An action cannot influence the past, so those terms only add noise. Replacing $R(\tau)$ with the **reward-to-go** $G_t = \sum_{k \ge t} \gamma^{k-t} r_k$ leaves the estimator unbiased and lowers its variance:

$$
\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\left[
\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, G_t \right]
$$

## Baselines

REINFORCE is unbiased but notoriously noisy, and there is a deeper problem than mere noise. If every reward in a task is positive, say returns between 10 and 12, then every sampled update pushes up the log-probability of the action it sampled, scaled by a return of order 10. Only the *differences* between those pushes carry signal, so a small useful gradient rides on a large common scale, and that scale is what inflates the variance.

Subtracting a **baseline** $b(s)$ removes the offset, and it adds no bias as long as $b$ does not depend on the action:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,
b(s) \right]
= b(s) \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)
$$

Apply the log-derivative identity in reverse, $\pi \nabla \log \pi = \nabla \pi$:

$$
= b(s) \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s)
= b(s)\, \nabla_\theta \underbrace{\sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}_{= \, 1}
= b(s) \cdot \nabla_\theta 1 = 0
$$

The term has **expectation exactly zero**. We may subtract any state-dependent baseline we like, change the variance drastically, and leave the gradient's mean untouched. The sum-to-one constraint on a probability distribution is doing all the work.

### Advantage and actor-critic

The most common choice of baseline is $V^\pi(s)$, the average return from this state, and using it gives the **advantage**:

$$
A^\pi(s, a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)
$$

which asks the sharper question: not *was this good*, but **was this better than what I normally do here?** Actions above average are reinforced, below average suppressed, regardless of whether raw rewards are all large and positive.

Since $V^\pi$ is unknown, learn it too. That is **actor-critic**:

* the **actor** $\pi_\theta$ chooses actions,
* the **critic** $V_\phi$ estimates state value and supplies the baseline.

With a one-step estimate $Q(s_t,a_t) \approx r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})$, the advantage becomes

$$
A_t \approx r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)
$$

which is exactly the **TD error** from [reinforcement learning](4.16-RF2-reinforcement-learning.md). The same number that drove value learning now weights the policy gradient, so a single scalar per step trains both the actor and the critic.

At a step that ends the episode there is no next state to bootstrap from, so the target is the reward alone: $A_t \approx r_t - V_\phi(s_t)$. Code usually writes this as $A_t \approx r_t + \gamma (1 - \text{done}) V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)$, since the value the critic outputs for the state an episode ends in stands for rewards that episode will never collect.

## Why PPO exists

Policy gradients are **on-policy**: the expectation is over trajectories from $\pi_\theta$, so the moment $\theta$ is updated, the collected data is stale and must be thrown away. That is badly sample-inefficient, and taking larger steps to compensate is exactly what destroys these methods. One oversized update can collapse the policy into a degenerate distribution it never recovers from, because the data it collects afterward is drawn from that broken policy.

**PPO** (proximal policy optimization) addresses both. It reuses each batch for several passes. To correct for the data having been collected by an older policy, it weights each sample by an **importance ratio**: how much more or less likely the current policy is to take that action than the old one was.

$$
r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
(a_t \mid s_t)}
$$

Then it refuses to trust that correction too far:

$$
L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min\left( r_t(\theta) A_t,\;
\operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_t \right) \right]
$$

Taking the minimum with a clipped copy is what makes PPO work. Once the ratio moves outside $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$, typically $\epsilon = 0.2$, in the direction that would *improve* the objective, the clipped branch is smaller, the $\min$ selects it, and the gradient flattens to zero. So there is no incentive to push further, and the policy stays close to the one that gathered the data, where the importance-weighted estimate can be trusted. That neighborhood is the **trust region** of TRPO, and PPO approximates it with nothing but a clip.

<ProblemCard id="pg-samples-that-still-pass-a-gradient" />

This is one algorithm behind RLHF. $\pi_\theta$ is the language model, and $A_t$ is built from the scores of a reward model trained on human preference comparisons. The clip, together with a penalty for moving too far from the original model, keeps the model from drifting into degenerate text that games the reward.

## From scratch

Both claims about baselines, that they add no bias and that they cut the variance, can be checked directly. One state, three actions, rewards 10/11/12, a softmax policy. Small enough that the exact gradient can be computed in closed form and compared against the sampled estimator.

```python
import numpy as np

REWARDS = np.array([10.0, 11.0, 12.0])        # one state, three actions

def policy(theta):
    e = np.exp(theta - theta.max())
    return e / e.sum()

def grad_log_pi(a, pi):
    """d/dtheta log pi(a) for a softmax policy: one-hot minus the policy."""
    g = -pi.copy()
    g[a] += 1.0
    return g

def true_gradient(theta):
    """Exact: sum_a pi(a) * grad_log_pi(a) * r(a). Only possible here."""
    pi = policy(theta)
    return sum(pi[a] * grad_log_pi(a, pi) * REWARDS[a] for a in range(len(pi)))

def sample_gradients(theta, n, baseline, rng):
    pi = policy(theta)
    acts = rng.choice(len(pi), size=n, p=pi)
    return np.stack([grad_log_pi(a, pi) * (REWARDS[a] - baseline) for a in acts])

theta = np.zeros(3)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
exact = true_gradient(theta)
print("exact gradient:      ", np.round(exact, 4))

for name, b in [("no baseline", 0.0), ("baseline b=11", 11.0)]:
    G = sample_gradients(theta, 200_000, b, rng)
    est, var = G.mean(axis=0), G.var(axis=0).sum()
    print(f"\n{name}")
    print("  estimate:          ", np.round(est, 4))
    print("  max error vs exact: ", f"{np.abs(est - exact).max():.4f}")
    print("  total variance:    ", f"{var:.4f}")
```

```output
exact gradient:       [-0.3333  0.      0.3333]

no baseline
  estimate:           [-0.3363  0.0105  0.3257]
  max error vs exact:  0.0105
  total variance:     80.8862

baseline b=11
  estimate:           [-0.3336 -0.0007  0.3343]
  max error vs exact:  0.0009
  total variance:     0.2222
```

Both estimators land on the exact gradient: the proof above says they must. But the variance falls from 80.89 to 0.22, a **364-fold reduction**, purely from subtracting a constant that provably cannot bias the result.

That ratio is the whole case for baselines in one number. Since the standard error of a Monte Carlo estimate scales as $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}/n}$, a 364× variance reduction is worth 364× the samples. To match what the baselined estimator gets from 300 episodes, the unbaselined one would need about 110,000.

Training with it converges as expected:

```python
theta = np.zeros(3)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
alpha, steps, batch = 0.05, 400, 32

for t in range(steps):
    pi = policy(theta)
    acts = rng.choice(3, size=batch, p=pi)
    r = REWARDS[acts]
    b = r.mean()                              # batch mean baseline: built from these sampled actions, so the update is shrunk by (1 - 1/batch)
    g = np.mean([grad_log_pi(a, pi) * (ri - b) for a, ri in zip(acts, r)], axis=0)
    theta += alpha * g
    if t % 100 == 0:
        print(f"  step {t:3d}: pi = {np.round(policy(theta), 3)}")

pi = policy(theta)
print(f"  final:    pi = {np.round(pi, 3)}")
print(f"  expected reward: {pi @ REWARDS:.4f}  (best possible {REWARDS.max():.1f})")
```

```output
  step   0: pi = [0.327 0.334 0.339]
  step 100: pi = [0.063 0.115 0.822]
  step 200: pi = [0.026 0.05  0.923]
  step 300: pi = [0.016 0.03  0.954]
  final:    pi = [0.011 0.021 0.968]
  expected reward: 11.9571  (best possible 12.0)
```

Note the policy never fully commits. It converges *toward* a one-hot distribution without reaching it, because the softmax needs infinite logits to get there. That residual stochasticity is exploration the method never completely abandons, and it is why policy gradients need no explicit $\varepsilon$-greedy mechanism.
