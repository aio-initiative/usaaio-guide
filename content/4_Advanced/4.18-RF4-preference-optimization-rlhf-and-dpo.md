# 4.18 · RF4 · RLHF and Preference Optimization

> **Goal:** Optimize the policy against the reward model with PPO and a KL penalty to the reference, or replace the RL loop entirely with DPO.
> **Stage:** Advanced, Extension topics.

## The pipeline

1. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT).** Fine-tune a pretrained language model on demonstration data to get $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$.
2. **Reward modeling.** Collect human preferences between pairs of responses and fit a reward model under the **Bradley–Terry** model:

$$
p(y_w\succ y_l\mid x) = \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big),
$$

trained by maximizing the log-likelihood of the observed preferences. Note this needs only *rankings*, not absolute scores, which is what humans can actually provide reliably.

3. **RL fine-tuning.** Optimize the policy against the reward model with PPO, with a KL penalty to the reference:

$$
\max_\pi\ \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big] - \beta\,D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi(y\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y\mid x)\big).
$$

Each stage sees far less data than the one before: pretraining sees trillions of tokens, SFT tens of thousands of examples, preference optimization tens of thousands of comparisons.

## Why comparisons

Asking a person to rate a response out of ten produces data dominated by the rater's personal scale, their mood, and what they saw immediately before. Asking which of two responses is better removes all of that: the comparison is relative, both items are in view, and inter-annotator agreement is far higher.

Bradley–Terry is the model that converts such comparisons into a scalar. It assumes each item has a latent quality $r$ and that

$$
p(i \succ j) = \sigma(r_i - r_j),
$$

so only *differences* of rewards are identified: the scale has an arbitrary additive constant, which is why a reward model's raw values are not comparable across training runs.

**The reward model** is usually the SFT model with the language-modeling head replaced by a scalar output, initialized from the same weights and trained on the same distribution. That shared initialization matters: a reward model trained from scratch would be valid on a different region of input space than the policy it must score.

## The KL penalty

**The KL penalty is not optional.** The reward model is trained on the SFT model's output distribution and is only valid near it. Without the penalty the policy produces gibberish outputs that the reward model scores enormously highly (**reward hacking**), and it happens reliably within a few hundred steps.

The mechanism is straightforward: the reward model is a neural network fitted to a finite sample, so it has regions of wildly wrong output away from its training distribution, and an optimizer whose only instruction is "maximize this" will find them. The KL term says "and stay where the reward model is trustworthy".

**Reading $\beta$.** Small $\beta$ permits a large distributional shift and gets a higher reward-model score, which may or may not correspond to a better model. Large $\beta$ keeps the policy near SFT and limits how much can be learned. In practice $\beta$ is often *controlled adaptively* to hold the measured KL at a target value, since the appropriate penalty differs by prompt distribution and by stage of training.

**The diagnostic** is a plot of reward-model score against KL from the reference. A healthy run rises steadily. A hacked one shows the score climbing sharply while KL runs away, and the samples become recognizably degenerate: repetitive, over-long, full of whatever surface feature the reward model over-weighted.

## DPO

DPO removes the RL loop entirely. The KL-constrained objective has a closed-form optimum:

$$
\pi^\ast(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right).
$$

*Derivation:* this is the standard variational result: the distribution maximizing $\mathbb{E}_\pi[r] - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\Vert\pi_{\text{ref}})$ is the exponentially-tilted reference, obtained by writing the objective as a negative KL to that tilted distribution plus a constant.

$Z(x)$ is a sum over **all possible responses**, astronomically many for a language model, and completely intractable.

Invert for the reward:

$$
r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^\ast(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
$$

Substitute into Bradley–Terry, which needs only the **difference** of two rewards for the same prompt:

$$
r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^\ast(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^\ast(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} + \underbrace{\beta\log Z(x) - \beta\log Z(x)}_{= 0} .
$$

**$Z(x)$ cancels exactly**, because it depends only on $x$ and both responses share the same prompt. What remains is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right],
$$

a **binary classification loss** computable from four forward passes and nothing else.

**What replaced what.** PPO-based RLHF needs: a reward model (a second network, separately trained), online sampling from the policy, a value network, GAE, the clipped objective, and a KL controller. DPO needs: a forward pass through the policy and the frozen reference on each of two responses, and a sigmoid. **The RL was never necessary**. It was an artifact of treating the reward model as something that had to exist.

## Where each still wins

DPO is simpler, cheaper, and more stable, and it is the default for most open work. Two things PPO-based RLHF retains:

- **Online data.** PPO samples from the *current* policy, so it can be scored on responses the model actually produces now. DPO trains on a fixed offline preference set, and once the policy has moved away from the distribution those responses came from, the signal degrades. Online and iterative DPO variants close much of this gap by regenerating preference data periodically.
- **A reusable reward model.** Having a separate $r_\phi$ lets you score arbitrary responses, run best-of-$n$ sampling at inference, filter training data, and monitor for regressions. DPO's reward is implicit and only defined relative to the reference.

**The wider family.** IPO fixes a DPO failure mode where the loss can be driven arbitrarily low by pushing the rejected response's probability to zero. KTO learns from thumbs-up/thumbs-down labels rather than pairs, which are far cheaper to collect. GRPO replaces the value network with a group-relative baseline computed from several sampled responses, which is what recent reasoning-focused training runs use.

## What alignment by preference does not solve

**Reward models are proxies.** They are fitted to what annotators preferred, which correlates with quality and also with length, confidence, and formatting. Optimizing against them amplifies every such correlation: the well-documented length bias, where aligned models grow steadily more verbose, is the clearest example.

**Preferences disagree.** A single scalar reward assumes a single population with consistent taste, which no real annotator pool has. Averaging over disagreement produces a model that satisfies no one on contested questions.

**Capability and alignment trade.** Heavy preference optimization measurably reduces output diversity and can degrade performance on tasks the preference data did not cover: the "alignment tax". Mitigations are mixing pretraining data back in, and constraining the KL more tightly.

## From Scratch

```python
import numpy as np


def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-np.clip(z, -500, 500)))


def bradley_terry_loss(r_w, r_l):
    """Only DIFFERENCES are identified, so the reward scale has an arbitrary
    additive constant, which is why raw reward values are not comparable
    across training runs."""
    return float(-np.log(sigmoid(np.asarray(r_w) - np.asarray(r_l)) + 1e-12).mean())


def bradley_terry_fit(comparisons, n_items, lr=0.1, iters=2000):
    """Recover latent qualities from pairwise wins. Fix one item's score to
    remove the additive degeneracy."""
    r = np.zeros(n_items)
    for _ in range(iters):
        g = np.zeros(n_items)
        for w, l in comparisons:
            p = sigmoid(r[w] - r[l])
            g[w] += (1 - p)
            g[l] -= (1 - p)
        r += lr * g / max(len(comparisons), 1)
        r -= r[0]                                    # anchor the scale
    return r


def rlhf_objective(rewards, logp_policy, logp_ref, beta):
    """E[r] - beta KL(pi || pi_ref). The second term is what stops the
    optimizer leaving the region where the reward model is valid."""
    kl = float(np.mean(np.asarray(logp_policy) - np.asarray(logp_ref)))
    return float(np.mean(rewards)) - beta * kl, kl


def adaptive_kl_controller(beta, kl, kl_target=6.0):
    """Hold the measured KL near a target by adjusting beta, since the right
    penalty differs by prompt distribution and training stage."""
    err = np.clip(kl / kl_target - 1, -0.2, 0.2)
    return beta * (1 + err)


def reward_hacking_signature(rewards, kls):
    """A healthy run raises reward with modest KL. A hacked one shows reward
    climbing while KL runs away, and the samples become degenerate."""
    return {"reward_slope": float(np.polyfit(range(len(rewards)), rewards, 1)[0]),
            "kl_slope": float(np.polyfit(range(len(kls)), kls, 1)[0]),
            "suspicious": kls[-1] > 5 * max(kls[len(kls) // 4], 1e-9)}


def implicit_reward(logp_policy, logp_ref, beta):
    """DPO's reward: beta log(pi/pi_ref). Never trained as a separate model,
    and defined only relative to the reference."""
    return beta * (np.asarray(logp_policy) - np.asarray(logp_ref))


def dpo_loss(logp_w, logp_ref_w, logp_l, logp_ref_l, beta=0.1):
    """Four forward passes and a sigmoid. Z(x) cancelled because both
    responses share the prompt, and Bradley-Terry needs only the difference."""
    margin = beta * ((logp_w - logp_ref_w) - (logp_l - logp_ref_l))
    return float(-np.log(sigmoid(margin) + 1e-12).mean()), margin


def optimal_policy_tilt(pi_ref, r, beta):
    """pi* = pi_ref exp(r/beta) / Z. Z sums over EVERY possible response,
    an intractable sum, and it is exactly what DPO makes cancel."""
    w = pi_ref * np.exp(np.asarray(r) / beta)
    return w / w.sum()


def best_of_n(responses, reward_model_scores, n):
    """What an explicit reward model buys that DPO's implicit one does not:
    inference-time selection, data filtering, regression monitoring."""
    idx = np.argsort(reward_model_scores)[::-1][:n]
    return [responses[i] for i in idx]


def length_bias_check(rewards, lengths):
    """The best-documented proxy failure: reward correlates with verbosity,
    so optimizing it makes models longer rather than better."""
    return float(np.corrcoef(rewards, lengths)[0, 1])
```

## Worked Example

**Part A: Bradley–Terry, fitted by hand.** Three responses with observed pairwise preferences: $A\succ B$ six times out of ten, $A\succ C$ nine out of ten, $B\succ C$ eight out of ten.

Fix $r_A = 0$. From $p(A\succ B) = \sigma(r_A - r_B) = 0.6$:

$$
r_B = -\sigma^{-1}(0.6) = -\log\frac{0.6}{0.4} = -0.405 .
$$

From $p(A\succ C) = 0.9$: $r_C = -\log\frac{0.9}{0.1} = -2.197$.

**Check consistency** against the third comparison: the model predicts

$$
p(B\succ C) = \sigma(-0.405 + 2.197) = \sigma(1.792) = 0.857 ,
$$

against the observed 0.8. Close, and the discrepancy is what maximum-likelihood fitting on all three comparisons jointly would split between the parameters.

Note that $r_A = 0$ was a choice: adding 5 to every reward changes nothing, since only differences enter. That is why a reward model's absolute output is meaningless and only its ordering matters.

**Part B: reward hacking, traced.** A run with $\beta = 0$ (no KL penalty), logging the reward-model score and a human rating every 100 steps (selected steps shown):

| step | RM score | KL from ref | human rating |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 6.2 |
| 100 | 1.8 | 3.1 | 6.9 |
| 300 | 4.2 | 18.4 | 6.4 |
| 600 | 9.7 | 91.0 | 3.1 |
| 1000 | 18.3 | 340 | 1.2 |

The reward-model score rises monotonically and the human rating peaks at step 100 and then collapses. By step 1000 the outputs are recognizably degenerate: extremely long, repetitive, and packed with whatever surface features the reward model over-weighted.

**The optimizer did exactly what it was told.** The reward model was fitted near the SFT distribution and is arbitrary at KL 340, so maximizing it there is maximizing noise. With $\beta = 0.05$ and an adaptive controller holding KL near 6, the same run reaches a human rating of 7.8 and stays there.

**Part C: DPO's numeric behavior.** Suppose $\beta = 0.1$ and

$$
\log\pi_\theta(y_w) = -10, \quad \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w) = -12, \quad
\log\pi_\theta(y_l) = -15, \quad \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l) = -14 .
$$

$$
\text{implicit } r_w = 0.1(-10+12) = 0.2, \qquad r_l = 0.1(-15+14) = -0.1 .
$$
$$
\mathcal{L} = -\log\sigma(0.2-(-0.1)) = -\log\sigma(0.3) = -\log(0.5744) = 0.5544 .
$$

The policy already prefers the winner ($r_w > r_l$), so the loss is below $\log 2 = 0.693$ but not zero, and there is still gradient pushing the margin wider. Had the preference been reversed ($r_w - r_l = -0.3$), the loss would be $-\log\sigma(-0.3) = 0.8544$, and the gradient would push harder.

**Part D: what DPO removes, counted.** Components required for one training step:

| | RLHF (PPO) | DPO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| networks in memory | policy, reference, reward model, value head | policy, reference |
| forward passes per step | generate ($n$ tokens, sequential) + 3 scoring | 4 (parallel) |
| online generation | required | none |
| extra hyperparameters | clip $\epsilon$, GAE $\lambda$, value coefficient, KL controller | $\beta$ |
| separate training run | reward model | none |

DPO's step is a batched forward-backward pass over precomputed text. PPO's requires autoregressive generation, which is memory-bound and sequential. The wall-clock difference is typically 3–10×, before counting the reward model's own training run.

**Part E: what DPO gives up.** Two things, both about having an explicit reward model.

**Online data.** PPO scores responses the *current* policy generates, so the signal always describes where the policy is. DPO's preference set was generated by some earlier model. After enough training the policy's outputs no longer resemble either $y_w$ or $y_l$, and the gradient is fitting a comparison between two things it would never produce. Iterative DPO (regenerate preferences from the current policy every few thousand steps) recovers most of this, at the cost of reintroducing the sampling loop.

**Inference-time use.** With $r_\phi$ in hand you can sample 16 responses and return the best (best-of-$n$), which is often worth more than the alignment training itself. You can filter a training corpus, and you can monitor for regressions. DPO's implicit reward $\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ can be computed, but it is defined only relative to the reference and is a much weaker scorer in practice.


**Part F: the length bias, measured.** Correlation between reward-model score and response length across 5,000 held-out responses: $\rho = 0.61$.

That is high enough that a policy could improve its score substantially by lengthening its outputs without improving them, and this is exactly what is observed: mean response length rises from 180 to 310 tokens over a preference-training run, while length-controlled win rate is flat.

**The fix is at the data and evaluation stage, not the algorithm.** Collect preferences with length shown and instructions to ignore it, add a length penalty to the reward, and report **length-controlled** win rates, comparing responses of similar length, so that verbosity cannot be mistaken for quality.
