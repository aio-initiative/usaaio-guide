### Sparse patterns

Restrict which positions may attend to which. Unlike FlashAttention, this **does** change the result.

**Sliding window** (local): each position attends to $w$ neighbors. Cost $O(nw)$ instead of $O(n^2)$.

The key property: **stacking windows grows the receptive field linearly in depth**, exactly like convolutions ([CNN Fundamentals](/module/?id=convolutional-neural-networks)). After $L$ layers with window $w$ (a symmetric window attends $(w-1)/2$ positions on each side, so take $w$ odd), the effective field is

$$
r = 1 + L(w-1) \quad\text{for a symmetric window, or } L\cdot(w-1) \text{ for a causal one.}
$$

Mistral 7B uses $w = 4096$ with $L = 32$, giving a theoretical receptive field of $131{,}040$ tokens from purely local attention.

**Global tokens.** Designate a few positions (a `[CLS]`, or the first $k$ tokens) that attend to everything and are attended by everything. This gives an information bottleneck through which any position can reach any other in two hops, at a cost of $O(nk)$.

**Dilated attention** attends to every $d$-th position, growing the field geometrically, the attention analog of dilated convolutions.

**Longformer** combines sliding window plus global tokens, and **BigBird** adds random connections and proves the combination is a universal approximator of full attention. The proofs are reassuring but loose, and the practical justification is empirical.

**The honest caveat.** A theoretical receptive field is not an effective one. Each hop through a window is a lossy weighted average, so information from 100k tokens back arrives heavily attenuated after 32 hops. Sliding-window models score well on perplexity and poorly on precise long-range retrieval, which is the same pattern seen throughout this area.

### Attention sinks

**The observation.** In trained language models, a large fraction of attention mass goes to the **first token** (often a `<bos>` or a period) regardless of its content and regardless of what the query is. Removing it destroys the model.

**The explanation.** Softmax rows must sum to 1. Sometimes a head has nothing it needs to retrieve, but it cannot output "nothing", so it must put its mass somewhere. So the model learns a **default dump**, and the first token is the natural choice because it is visible to every position under a causal mask. The sink token's *value* vector is typically near zero, so attending to it contributes almost nothing to the output, which is exactly the point.

**Consequences:**

1. **StreamingLLM.** When evicting cache, keep the first few tokens. Without the sink, the softmax mass that used to go there is redistributed onto genuine content, corrupting every head that wanted to do nothing. Four sink tokens plus a sliding window is enough to keep quality nearly intact over millions of tokens.
2. **Quantization outliers.** The sink token's key and value have unusually large magnitudes, producing activation outliers that break naive per-tensor quantization. Handling them separately (as in LLM.int8() and SmoothQuant) is necessary.
3. **A principled fix**: add a learnable "no-op" logit to the softmax denominator, so a head can attend to nothing without hijacking a real token. Some recent architectures do this explicitly.

### Cache eviction

Bounding memory means dropping cache entries, and *which* entries determine whether the model survives.

- **Evict oldest (naive window).** Catastrophic, for the sink reason above.
- **Sinks plus window (StreamingLLM).** Keep the first 4 tokens and the most recent $w$. Quality holds over millions of tokens, and memory is constant. The model cannot recall anything outside the window, so this buys *endurance*, not long context.
- **Heavy-hitter eviction (H2O).** Keep the tokens that have accumulated the most attention so far, on the theory that they will continue to matter. Better retention of distant content at some bookkeeping cost.
- **Quantized cache.** Orthogonal to all of the above, and it halves or quarters memory at small quality cost.

### What heads do

**Induction heads** are the most important interpretability result for this syllabus. An induction head implements the rule: *given the pattern `[A][B] ... [A]`, predict `[B]`*: copy what followed this token last time.

It requires two heads working together across layers:

1. A **previous-token head** in an early layer writes information about token $t-1$ into position $t$'s residual stream.
2. An **induction head** in a later layer, at the second `[A]`, forms a query matching that written information, finds the earlier position holding "preceded by A", and copies its value.

**Why it matters.** Induction heads form abruptly during training, at a specific point visible as a bump in the loss curve, and **in-context learning ability appears at exactly the same moment**. Olsson et al. give substantial evidence that induction heads are the primary mechanism behind in-context learning in small models, which is one of the few mechanistic explanations of a large-scale emergent capability that has held up.

**Other identified head types:** previous-token heads, positional heads (attend to a fixed offset), syntactic heads (attend to a dependency parent), duplicate-token heads, name-mover heads (in the indirect-object-identification circuit).

### Head redundancy and diagnostics

**Attention entropy** is a useful diagnostic:

$$
H(\alpha_i) = -\sum_j\alpha_{ij}\log\alpha_{ij} .
$$

Low entropy means a sharply focused head, and high entropy near $\log n$ means a uniform, near-useless one. Many heads in a trained model are prunable: 20–40% can typically be removed with little loss, which is evidence that multi-head attention is over-parameterized in a way that helps optimization rather than expressiveness.

**A caution.** Attention weights show where a head looked, not why the output is what it is. The "attention is not explanation" line of work shows the weights can often be substantially perturbed without changing the prediction. Circuit-level analysis (tracing paths through the residual stream, with ablations) is the more reliable method.


### From Scratch

```python
import numpy as np


def sliding_window_mask(n, w, causal=True):
    """Cost O(nw). Receptive field grows LINEARLY with depth, like a CNN."""
    i = np.arange(n)[:, None]
    j = np.arange(n)[None, :]
    if causal:
        return (j <= i) & (j > i - w)
    return np.abs(i - j) <= (w - 1) // 2


def global_token_mask(n, n_global):
    """The first n_global positions attend to everything and are attended by
    everything: any pair of tokens is now two hops apart."""
    m = np.zeros((n, n), dtype=bool)
    m[:n_global, :] = True
    m[:, :n_global] = True
    return m


def dilated_mask(n, dilation, w):
    """Every d-th position: the field grows GEOMETRICALLY with stacked layers."""
    i = np.arange(n)[:, None]
    j = np.arange(n)[None, :]
    return ((i - j) % dilation == 0) & (j <= i) & (j > i - w * dilation)


def longformer_mask(n, w, n_global):
    return sliding_window_mask(n, w) | global_token_mask(n, n_global)


def receptive_field(L, w, causal=True):
    """Theoretical reach. An UPPER BOUND -- each hop is a lossy average, so the
    effective reach is much shorter. Measure it, do not compute it."""
    return L * (w - 1) if causal else 1 + L * (w - 1)


def sparse_cost(n, w, n_global, n_random=0):
    dense = n * n
    sparse = n * (w + n_global + n_random) + n_global * n
    return {"dense": dense, "sparse": sparse, "ratio": dense / sparse}


def attention_entropy(alpha, eps=1e-12):
    """Low = focused head; near log(n) = uniform and probably prunable."""
    a = np.clip(np.asarray(alpha), eps, 1.0)
    return float(-(a * np.log(a)).sum(-1).mean())


def sink_mass(alpha, n_sink=4):
    """Fraction of attention going to the first few tokens, averaged over
    queries. In trained models this is routinely 30-60% for many heads."""
    return float(np.asarray(alpha)[..., :n_sink].sum(-1).mean())


def streaming_cache_indices(t, window, n_sink=4):
    """StreamingLLM: keep the sinks AND the recent window. Dropping the sinks
    redistributes their mass onto real content and destroys every head that
    wanted to attend to nothing."""
    recent = list(range(max(n_sink, t - window + 1), t + 1))
    return list(range(min(n_sink, t + 1))) + recent


def h2o_eviction(accumulated_attention, budget, n_sink=4):
    """Heavy hitters: keep the tokens that have received the most attention so
    far, plus the sinks. Retains distant content better than a plain window."""
    scores = np.asarray(accumulated_attention).copy()
    scores[:n_sink] = np.inf
    return np.sort(np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:budget])


def induction_score(model_fn, vocab, seq_len=50, rng=None):
    """The standard probe: a random repeated sequence. A model with induction
    heads predicts the second copy almost perfectly; one without cannot."""
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng(0)
    half = rng.integers(0, vocab, seq_len)
    seq = np.concatenate([half, half])
    logits = model_fn(seq)
    preds = logits[seq_len:-1].argmax(-1)
    return float((preds == seq[seq_len + 1:]).mean())
```

`induction_score` is the cheapest interpretability experiment there is: feed a randomly generated sequence twice and measure how well the model predicts the second copy. A model with induction heads scores near 1, while one without scores at chance, and the transition happens over a few hundred training steps.

### Worked Example

**Part A: the receptive field of stacked windows.** Mistral 7B: $w = 4096$, $L = 32$, causal.

$$
r = L\cdot (w-1) = 32\times4095 = 131{,}040 \text{ tokens}.
$$

The mechanism, layer by layer: after layer 1, position $t$ has seen $[t-4095, t]$. After layer 2, it has seen positions that *those* positions saw, i.e. $[t-8190, t]$. Each layer adds another window's worth, exactly as a stack of $3\times3$ convolutions grows its field by 2 per layer.

**Part B: the cost saving.** At $n = 32{,}768$ with $w = 4096$:

$$
\text{dense: } n^2 = 1.07\times10^9 \text{ score entries.}
$$
$$
\text{window: } nw = 1.34\times10^8 \quad (8\times \text{ fewer}).
$$

At $n = 131{,}072$ the ratio is 32×, and it grows linearly in $n$, which is the point of a sub-quadratic pattern. But note that at $n = 4096$ the ratio is 1: the window only helps once the sequence exceeds it, and below that it is pure overhead.

**Part C: theoretical versus effective reach.** Plant a fact at position 1000 of a 100,000-token document and ask for it at the end. The theoretical receptive field covers it comfortably (131k > 100k). Empirically, sliding-window models retrieve such facts at rates far below full attention, and the rate falls with distance.

The reason is that each of the 32 hops is a softmax-weighted average over 4096 positions. Even if every hop gave the relevant position a generous 10% of its mass (far more than typical), the signal after 32 hops is $0.1^{32}$ of what it started as. Information does propagate, but as a diffuse influence rather than a retrievable value.

That is why Mistral pairs its window with a large context and full attention over the recent span, and why the honest description of a window is "cheap local processing with weak global influence".

**Part D: attention sinks, measured.** Take a trained 7B model and average the attention mass on the first four tokens across all heads in layer 10:

| head | sink mass | entropy | reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 0.71 | 1.2 | mostly parked, a "do nothing" head |
| 7 | 0.08 | 4.8 | broadly distributed |
| 12 | 0.44 | 2.1 | partially parked |
| 19 | 0.02 | 3.3 | genuinely retrieving |

Head 3 sends 71% of its mass to the first four tokens. Its value vectors there are near zero, so its contribution to the residual stream is small. It is, most of the time, choosing to do nothing, and the sink is how it expresses that.

**Part E: what naive eviction does.** Evict the oldest tokens to keep a 1024-token window, dropping the sinks. Head 3's 71% of mass now has nowhere to go, and softmax redistributes it proportionally over the remaining 1024 positions, so every genuine content token receives roughly $0.71/1024 = 0.07\%$ of *extra* spurious mass, and the head that meant to contribute nothing now contributes a weighted average of arbitrary recent content.

Across dozens of such heads and 32 layers, this compounds: perplexity rises by an order of magnitude within a few hundred tokens of the first eviction. Keeping four sink tokens (4 out of 1028 cache slots, 0.4% of the memory) restores it almost completely. It is one of the highest-leverage one-line fixes in the area.

**Part F: the induction-head probe.** Generate 50 random token IDs, repeat them, and feed the 100-token sequence.

Positions 52–100 are exactly predictable from positions 1–50 by the rule "what followed this token last time". A model **before** induction heads form predicts them at chance, roughly $1/|V|$. A model **after** predicts them at over 90%.

The transition happens over a few hundred training steps and coincides with a small visible bump in the training loss curve, and, strikingly, with the appearance of in-context learning on real tasks. That coincidence is the main evidence that induction heads are the mechanism behind in-context learning in small models, and it is reproducible on a model small enough to train in an afternoon.
