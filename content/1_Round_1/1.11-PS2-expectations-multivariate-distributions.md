## Overview

You average the predictions of five models that saw similar data, so their mistakes overlap. How much does averaging still help, and is there a floor that no number of models can get below? To answer this you need three numbers for a random quantity: its average (expectation), its spread (variance), and how it moves with another one (covariance). The USAAIO syllabus lists probability and statistics, and later modules use these three numbers throughout: a loss is an expected error, an ensemble lowers variance, and PCA starts from a covariance matrix.

## Example: how much does an ensemble help?

Five models each make an error on a test example. Each error has mean 0 and variance 4. Any two errors have correlation 0.5, because the models were trained on similar data. (Correlation measures how closely two quantities move together, on a scale from $-1$ to 1. It is defined below.) You average the five predictions. The averaged prediction minus the truth is the average of the five errors.

1. What is the variance of the averaged error?
2. What would it be if the errors were independent? What does it approach as you average more and more models with correlation 0.5?

## Explanation

### Random variables and their distributions

Each model's error is a number you do not know until you pick a test example. A number that depends on chance like this is a **random variable**. A die roll and the number of heads in 10 flips are two more. Its **distribution** says which values it takes and how likely each one is.

A **discrete** random variable, like a die roll, takes separate values. Its **probability mass function** (PMF) $p(x) = P(X = x)$ gives the probability of each value, and these add up to 1. A **continuous** random variable, like a height, can take any value in a range. It has a **probability density** $p(x)$, and probabilities are areas under its curve. So $P(X = x)$ is 0 for any single value $x$.

### Expectation

Roll a fair die many times and average the results. Each face shows up about 1/6 of the time, so the average approaches $(1 + 2 + \dots + 6)/6 = 3.5$. This probability-weighted average of the values is the **expectation** (or **mean**) of $X$, written $\mathbb{E}[X]$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x) \qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\,p(x)\,dx .
$$

The sum runs over every value $x$ that $X$ can take, and the integral is the version for a continuous variable. The mean need not be a possible value, since no face shows 3.5. To average a function $g(X)$, weight the function's values: $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\,p(x)$. For the die, $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36)/6 = 91/6$.

Expectation is **linear**. You can split it across a sum and pull constants out. For any constants $a$, $b$ and $c$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[aX + bY + c] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y] + c .
$$

This holds even when $X$ and $Y$ are dependent. So you can find the mean of a complicated count by splitting it into simple pieces.

For example, shuffle $n$ labeled cards. How many land in their original position, on average? Let $I_k$ be 1 if card $k$ lands in its original spot and 0 otherwise. A variable that is 1 when an event happens and 0 otherwise is an **indicator**, and its mean is the probability of the event. Card $k$ is equally likely to land in any of the $n$ spots, so $\mathbb{E}[I_k] = 1/n$. The count is $I_1 + \dots + I_n$, so its mean is $n \cdot \tfrac1n = 1$ for every $n$. The $I_k$ are dependent (if $n - 1$ cards are in place, the last one is too), but linearity does not need independence.

### Variance

Two models can both have mean error 0 and still differ. One is usually off by 0.1 and the other by 2. The **variance** measures this spread. It is the average squared distance from the mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2 .
$$

The second form comes from expanding the square and using linearity: $\mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X] + \mu^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2$. It is quicker by hand. For the die, $\operatorname{Var}(X) = 91/6 - 3.5^2 = 35/12 \approx 2.92$.

The **standard deviation** $\sigma$ is the square root of the variance, so it is in the original units. In the focus problem each error has variance $\sigma^2 = 4$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 2$.

Variance is not linear. Adding $b$ to $X$ moves the mean by $b$ too, so every distance $X - \mu$ stays the same and the variance does not change. Multiplying $X$ by $a$ multiplies every distance by $a$ and every squared distance by $a^2$. So $\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$. Measuring in centimeters instead of meters multiplies the variance by $100^2 = 10{,}000$.

### Distributions to know

| Distribution | Models | Mean | Variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bernoulli$(p)$ | one yes-or-no trial | $p$ | $p(1-p)$ |
| Binomial$(n, p)$ | successes in $n$ independent trials | $np$ | $np(1-p)$ |
| Geometric$(p)$ | trials up to and including the first success | $1/p$ | $(1-p)/p^2$ |
| Poisson$(\lambda)$ | counts of rare events in a fixed window | $\lambda$ | $\lambda$ |
| Uniform$(a, b)$ | any value in $[a, b]$ equally likely | $(a+b)/2$ | $(b-a)^2/12$ |
| Exponential$(\lambda)$ | waiting time between Poisson events | $1/\lambda$ | $1/\lambda^2$ |
| Normal $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | sums of many small effects | $\mu$ | $\sigma^2$ |

Here $p$ is the success probability of one trial (a number, not the PMF), $n$ is the number of trials, $\lambda$ is the average count per window, and $[a, b]$ is the interval. The normal distribution is also called the **Gaussian**. Its density is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$.

The binomial is a sum of $n$ independent Bernoullis. Its mean $np$ follows from linearity. Its variance $np(1-p)$ uses the fact, shown below, that variances of independent variables add. A binary classifier outputs a probability $p$, which defines a Bernoulli distribution over the two labels. A classifier with $C$ classes outputs a **categorical** distribution, which extends the Bernoulli to $C$ outcomes.

### CDFs and maxima

How good is the best of several random tries? Roll two fair 4-sided dice and let $M$ be the larger. To find its distribution, use the **cumulative distribution function** (CDF), $F(x) = P(X \le x)$, which climbs from 0 to 1. The larger of two dice is at most $k$ if and only if both dice are at most $k$. So for independent variables,

$$
P(\max(X_1, \dots, X_n) \le k) = P(X_1 \le k)\cdots P(X_n \le k) .
$$

To turn that into an expectation, use the **tail-sum formula**. For a variable taking values $1, 2, 3, \dots$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{k \ge 1} P(X \ge k) = P(X \ge 1) + P(X \ge 2) + P(X \ge 3) + \cdots .
$$

The outcome $X = 3$ is included in the first three terms, so it is counted 3 times. That matches its share $3\,P(X = 3)$ of $\mathbb{E}[X]$. Every value $x$ is counted $x$ times in the same way.

**Worked example.** For the two dice, $P(M \le k) = (k/4)^2$. The event $M \ge k$ is the opposite of $M \le k - 1$, so $P(M \ge k) = 1 - ((k-1)/4)^2$. Then

$$
\mathbb{E}[M] = 1 + \tfrac{15}{16} + \tfrac{12}{16} + \tfrac{7}{16} = \tfrac{50}{16} = \tfrac{25}{8} = 3.125 .
$$

For a variable between 0 and 1, the sum becomes an integral, $\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^1 P(X > x)\,dx$. For the maximum of $n$ independent Uniform(0, 1) values, $P(\max \le x) = x^n$, so

$$
\mathbb{E}[\max] = \int_0^1 (1 - x^n)\,dx = 1 - \frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{n}{n+1} .
$$

With 5 tries the expected best is $5/6 \approx 0.83$, and with 99 tries it is 0.99.

### Joint distributions

Two random variables together have a **joint distribution**. For discrete ones it is a table of $P(X = x, Y = y)$:

| | $Y = 0$ | $Y = 1$ | $Y = 2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $X = 0$ | 0.10 | 0.20 | 0.10 |
| $X = 1$ | 0.20 | 0.30 | 0.10 |

- The **marginal distribution** of $X$ comes from adding across each row, which forgets $Y$: $P(X = 0) = 0.4$ and $P(X = 1) = 0.6$. Adding down the columns gives $P(Y = y) = 0.3$, $0.5$, $0.2$.
- The **conditional distribution** of $Y$ given $X = 1$ is that row divided by its total: $(0.2, 0.3, 0.1)/0.6 = (\tfrac13, \tfrac12, \tfrac16)$.
- $X$ and $Y$ are independent when every cell equals the product of its marginals. Here $P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 0.10$, but $0.4 \times 0.3 = 0.12$, so they are dependent.

### Covariance and correlation

Do large values of $X$ come with large values of $Y$? Look at the product of the two distances from the means, $(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)$, where $\mu_X = \mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\mu_Y = \mathbb{E}[Y]$. It is positive when both are on the same side of their means and negative otherwise. Its average is the **covariance**:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\big] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mu_X\mu_Y .
$$

The second form comes from expanding, as for the variance, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$. In the table, any cell with $x = 0$ or $y = 0$ adds 0 to $\mathbb{E}[XY]$, so only two cells count: $\mathbb{E}[XY] = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 0.3 + 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 0.1 = 0.5$. With $\mu_X = 0.6$ and $\mu_Y = 0 \cdot 0.3 + 1 \cdot 0.5 + 2 \cdot 0.2 = 0.9$, the covariance is $0.5 - 0.54 = -0.04$. Large $X$ comes with slightly smaller $Y$.

**Variance of a sum.** The mean of $aX + bY$ is $a\mu_X + b\mu_Y$, so its distance from the mean is $a(X - \mu_X) + b(Y - \mu_Y)$. Squaring gives $a^2(X - \mu_X)^2 + b^2(Y - \mu_Y)^2 + 2ab(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)$, and taking the expectation of each term gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X) + b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) .
$$

If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, every cell of their table is a product $P(X = x)P(Y = y)$. Then $\sum xy\,P(X = x)P(Y = y)$ factors into $\mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$, so the covariance is 0 and the variances add.

**Correlation.** Covariance is in units of $X$ times units of $Y$, so its size alone means little. Dividing by both standard deviations removes the units:

$$
\rho = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y} .
$$

The **correlation** $\rho$ is always between $-1$ and 1. It is $\pm 1$ only when $Y = aX + b$ for constants $a \ne 0$ and $b$. The closer $|\rho|$ is to 1, the closer the $(X, Y)$ pairs lie to a straight line. It misses curved relationships. Let $X$ be **standard normal**, meaning $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, and let $Y = X^2$. Then $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]$. Since $x$ and $-x$ are equally likely, $\mathbb{E}[X^3]$ and $\mathbb{E}[X]$ are both 0. So the correlation is 0, yet $X$ determines $Y$. Zero correlation does not imply independence.

Back to the ensemble. Each error has $\sigma = 2$, and two errors have correlation $0.5$, so their covariance is $\rho\sigma\sigma = 0.5 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 2$.

**The covariance matrix.** For a random vector $\mathbf{x}$ with $d$ entries, written as a column, the **covariance matrix** $\Sigma$ has entry $\Sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(x_i, x_j)$. Its diagonal holds the variances, and it is symmetric. For a weight vector $\mathbf{w}$, the combination $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} = w_1x_1 + \dots + w_dx_d$ has variance

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} w_i w_j \Sigma_{ij} = \mathbf{w}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{w} .
$$

With $d = 2$ and $\mathbf{w} = (a, b)$ this is the variance-of-a-sum formula above. A variance is never negative, so $\mathbf{w}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{w} \ge 0$ for every $\mathbf{w}$. A symmetric matrix with this property is called **positive semidefinite**. The sum of the diagonal, called the **trace**, is the total variance. [Principal Component Analysis](2.17-ML12-principal-component-analysis.md) looks for the unit vector $\mathbf{w}$ that makes this variance largest. It is the eigenvector of $\Sigma$ with the largest eigenvalue, as [Eigenvalues and Spectral Theory](1.8-MA2-linear-algebra-ii-eigenvalues-eigenvectors-spectral-theory.md) explains.

### Averages: why more data and more models help

Now average $n$ variables $X_1, \dots, X_n$, each with variance $\sigma^2$. Write $\bar{X} = (X_1 + \dots + X_n)/n$. Multiplying by $1/n$ multiplies the variance by $1/n^2$, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{1}{n^2}\operatorname{Var}(X_1 + \dots + X_n) .
$$

The variance of the sum is $\mathbf{w}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{w}$ with every weight equal to 1. That is the sum of every $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$ in an $n \times n$ grid. Suppose every pair has correlation $\rho$. Then each covariance is $\rho\sigma\sigma = \rho\sigma^2$, and for $n = 3$ the grid is

| | $X_1$ | $X_2$ | $X_3$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $X_1$ | $\sigma^2$ | $\rho\sigma^2$ | $\rho\sigma^2$ |
| $X_2$ | $\rho\sigma^2$ | $\sigma^2$ | $\rho\sigma^2$ |
| $X_3$ | $\rho\sigma^2$ | $\rho\sigma^2$ | $\sigma^2$ |

The $n$ diagonal cells are variances. The other $n^2 - n = n(n-1)$ cells are covariances. Adding the grid and dividing by $n^2$ gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{n\sigma^2 + n(n-1)\rho\sigma^2}{n^2} = \sigma^2\left(\rho + \frac{1 - \rho}{n}\right) .
$$

For independent variables $\rho = 0$, which leaves $\sigma^2/n$. The standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$, so averaging four times as many values halves the standard deviation.

**Solving the focus problem.** With $n = 5$, $\sigma^2 = 4$ and $\rho = 0.5$,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = 4\left(0.5 + \frac{0.5}{5}\right) = 4(0.6) = 2.4 .
$$

Independent errors ($\rho = 0$) would give $4 \cdot \tfrac15 = 0.8$. As $n$ grows, $(1 - \rho)/n$ goes to 0, which leaves $\rho\sigma^2 = 4(0.5) = 2$. Five correlated models cut the variance from 4 to 2.4, and no number of them gets it below 2.

Averaging cannot remove the error the models share. To lower that floor of $\rho\sigma^2$, an ensemble has to lower $\rho$. A random forest does this by training each tree on a different random resample of the training data and letting each split consider only a random subset of the features. [Ensemble Learning](1.16-ML4-tree-based-models-ensembles.md) covers how.

### Conditional expectation

The **conditional expectation** $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]$ is the average of $Y$ among the cases where $X = x$, computed from the conditional distribution. In the table,

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = 0] = \frac{0 \cdot 0.1 + 1 \cdot 0.2 + 2 \cdot 0.1}{0.4} = 1,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = 1] = \frac{0 \cdot 0.2 + 1 \cdot 0.3 + 2 \cdot 0.1}{0.6} = \frac56 .
$$

If we do not fix $x$, then $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$ is itself a random variable. It equals 1 when $X = 0$ and $5/6$ when $X = 1$.

- The **law of total expectation** says that averaging the group means, weighted by how often each group occurs, gives the overall mean: $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\big]$. Here $0.4 \cdot 1 + 0.6 \cdot \tfrac56 = 0.9$, which matches $\mu_Y$.
- The **law of total variance** splits the overall spread into the average spread within groups plus the spread of the group means: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\big)$.

**Example.** A factory makes 70% of its parts on line A (mean length 10, variance 1) and 30% on line B (mean 14, variance 4). The overall mean is $0.7 \cdot 10 + 0.3 \cdot 14 = 11.2$. The within-line part of the variance is $0.7 \cdot 1 + 0.3 \cdot 4 = 1.9$. The between-line part is $0.7(10 - 11.2)^2 + 0.3(14 - 11.2)^2 = 3.36$. The total is 5.26, and most of it comes from the two lines having different means, not from either line's noise.

### The multivariate Gaussian

A normal distribution also exists for vectors. We write $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)$, read "$\mathbf{x}$ is normally distributed with mean vector $\boldsymbol\mu$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$". In two dimensions the samples form an elliptical cloud, which is tilted when the two coordinates are correlated. Two variables are **jointly Gaussian** when the vector of both has this distribution. For jointly Gaussian variables, zero correlation does imply independence. In general it does not, as $X$ and $X^2$ showed.

**Conditioning.** Take two jointly Gaussian variables $x_1$ and $x_2$ with means $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$, standard deviations $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$, and correlation $\rho$. Observing $x_2$ gives

$$
\mathbb{E}[x_1 \mid x_2] = \mu_1 + \rho\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}(x_2 - \mu_2), \qquad
\operatorname{Var}(x_1 \mid x_2) = \sigma_1^2(1 - \rho^2) .
$$

The best guess for $x_1$ is a straight-line function of the observed $x_2$. The variance shrinks by the factor $1 - \rho^2$, whatever value you observed. For example, with $\boldsymbol\mu = (1, 0)$, $\sigma_1 = 2$, $\sigma_2 = 1$ and $\rho = 0.6$, observing $x_2 = 1.5$ gives mean $1 + 0.6 \cdot \tfrac21 \cdot 1.5 = 2.8$ and variance $4(1 - 0.36) = 2.56$.

### Entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence

How unpredictable is a distribution? Rare outcomes are more surprising, so define the **surprise** of an outcome with probability $p$ as $-\log p$. It is 0 for a sure thing and grows as $p$ shrinks. For independent events the probabilities multiply, so the surprises add. The **entropy** of a distribution $p$ is its average surprise:

$$
H(p) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) .
$$

With $\log_2$ the unit is bits. A fair coin has $H = 1$ bit. A coin that lands heads 90% of the time has $H = -0.9\log_2 0.9 - 0.1\log_2 0.1 \approx 0.137 + 0.332 = 0.469$ bits. That is less than 1 bit because this coin is easier to predict. A uniform distribution over $m$ outcomes has entropy $\log_2 m$, the largest possible for $m$ outcomes. The entropy is 0 when one outcome is certain.

If the data follow $p$ but you predict with $q$, your average surprise is the **cross-entropy** $H(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x)\log q(x)$. It is never less than $H(p)$, and the gap is the **KL divergence**:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\Vert\, q) = H(p, q) - H(p) = \sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \ge 0 ,
$$

with equality only when $q = p$. KL is not symmetric. For the 90% coin $p$ and a fair-coin model $q$, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) \approx 0.531$ bits while $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p) \approx 0.737$.

For a classifier, the true distribution $p$ puts probability 1 on the true class $y$ and 0 on the others. This is called a **one-hot** label. Every term but one is then 0, so $H(p, q) = -\log q_y$, where $q_y$ is the probability the model gives the true class. This is the **cross-entropy loss**, usually computed with the natural log, which gives a unit called nats. Minimizing it pushes $q$ toward the data. [Information Theory for AI](3.1-PS4-information-theory-core.md) goes further.

## Implementation

This code computes the marginals, a conditional distribution, the independence check, the conditional expectations and the covariance from the joint table.

```python
import numpy as np

P = np.array([[0.10, 0.20, 0.10],     # joint table: rows X = 0, 1, columns Y = 0, 1, 2
              [0.20, 0.30, 0.10]])
x_vals, y_vals = np.array([0, 1]), np.array([0, 1, 2])
pX, pY = P.sum(axis=1), P.sum(axis=0)              # marginals: sum out the other variable
print("P(X)         ", pX, "  P(Y)", pY)
print("P(Y | X = 1) ", np.round(P[1] / pX[1], 4))
print("independent? ", np.allclose(P, np.outer(pX, pY)))
EY = y_vals @ pY
EY_given_X = (P @ y_vals) / pX                     # E[Y | X = x] for each x
print("E[Y]", round(EY, 4), "  E[Y | X]", np.round(EY_given_X, 4), "  E[E[Y | X]]", round(EY_given_X @ pX, 4))
EXY = x_vals @ P @ y_vals
print("Cov(X, Y)", round(EXY - (x_vals @ pX) * EY, 4))
```

```output
independent?  False
```

A sample has a CDF too. Sorting its $n$ values and dividing the ranks $1, \dots, n$ by $n$ gives the **empirical CDF**, the fraction of the sample at or below each value, the data-side counterpart of the $F(x)$ above:

```python
def empirical_cdf(x):
    xs = np.sort(x)
    return xs, np.arange(1, x.size + 1) / x.size
```

For the `rolls` above, `empirical_cdf(rolls.max(axis=1))` pairs each simulated maximum with its rank out of $1{,}000{,}000$, so the curve jumps at 1, 2, 3, 4 and should sit close to $(k/4)^2$ at each of them.

This code solves the focus problem three ways: with the formula, with $\mathbf{w}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{w}$ for equal weights $\mathbf{w} = (1/n, \dots, 1/n)$, and by simulating 400,000 test examples.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, sigma2, rho = 5, 4.0, 0.5
Sigma = sigma2 * (rho * np.ones((n, n)) + (1 - rho) * np.eye(n))   # covariance of the 5 errors
errors = rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(n), Sigma, size=400_000)  # one row per test example
avg = errors.mean(axis=1)
w = np.full(n, 1 / n)
print(f"formula   sigma^2 (rho + (1 - rho) / n) = {sigma2 * (rho + (1 - rho) / n):.3f}")
print(f"w^T Sigma w with w = (1/n, ..., 1/n)    = {w @ Sigma @ w:.3f}")
print(f"simulated variance of the average       = {avg.var():.2f}")
```

```output
formula   sigma^2 (rho + (1 - rho) / n) = 2.400
w^T Sigma w with w = (1/n, ..., 1/n)    = 2.400
simulated variance of the average       = 2.40
```

All three give 2.4.

```output
corr(x, 2x + noise) = 0.970
corr(x, x^2)        = -0.010
mean of x^2 when |x| > 1: 2.531,  when |x| <= 1: 0.290
```

The straight-line pair has correlation 0.97. The correlation of $x$ with $x^2$ is about 0. Yet $x^2$ averages 2.53 when $|x| > 1$ and 0.29 otherwise, so it depends strongly on $x$.

This code samples a 2D Gaussian and checks the conditioning formula by keeping only the samples with $x_2$ near 1.5.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
mu = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
s1, s2, r = 2.0, 1.0, 0.6
Sigma = np.array([[s1**2, r * s1 * s2],
                  [r * s1 * s2, s2**2]])
xs = rng.multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma, size=1_000_000)
near = np.abs(xs[:, 1] - 1.5) < 0.02                # samples with x2 close to 1.5
print(f"samples with x2 near 1.5: {near.sum()}")
print(f"formula : E[x1 | x2 = 1.5] = {mu[0] + r * s1 / s2 * (1.5 - mu[1]):.2f}, Var = {s1**2 * (1 - r**2):.2f}")
print(f"samples : E[x1 | x2 = 1.5] = {xs[near, 0].mean():.2f}, Var = {xs[near, 0].var():.2f}")
```

```output
samples with x2 near 1.5: 5152
formula : E[x1 | x2 = 1.5] = 2.80, Var = 2.56
samples : E[x1 | x2 = 1.5] = 2.79, Var = 2.56
```

The sample estimates agree with the formula to within about 0.01. Only about 5,000 samples fall in the window, so they carry some noise.

This code computes the entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence for the two coins above, and one cross-entropy loss.

```python
def entropy(p):
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))

def cross_entropy(p, q):
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(q))

p, q = np.array([0.9, 0.1]), np.array([0.5, 0.5])
print(f"H(fair coin) = {entropy(q):.3f} bits,  H(p) = {entropy(p):.3f} bits")
kl_pq = cross_entropy(p, q) - entropy(p)
kl_qp = cross_entropy(q, p) - entropy(q)
print(f"H(p, q) = {cross_entropy(p, q):.3f},  KL(p || q) = {kl_pq:.3f},  KL(q || p) = {kl_qp:.3f}")
print(f"cross-entropy loss when the true class gets 0.7: {-np.log(0.7):.3f} nats")
```

```output
H(fair coin) = 1.000 bits,  H(p) = 0.469 bits
H(p, q) = 1.000,  KL(p || q) = 0.531,  KL(q || p) = 0.737
cross-entropy loss when the true class gets 0.7: 0.357 nats
```

The two directions of KL differ, 0.531 against 0.737 bits.

> **Warning.**
>
> $\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)$ needs $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$. For the five correlated models, assuming independence predicts a variance of 0.8 when the true value is 2.4, so error bars built on $\sigma^2/n$ come out too narrow. For independent variables, $\operatorname{Var}(X - Y)$ is also $\operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)$.

<Optional title="Affine maps, sampling and conditioning in many dimensions">

**Affine maps stay Gaussian.** If $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)$, then for a matrix $A$ and vector $\mathbf{c}$, $A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{c} \sim \mathcal{N}(A\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf{c},\ A\Sigma A^\top)$. Picking out some coordinates is such a map, so a marginal keeps the matching entries of $\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\Sigma$.

**Sampling.** When $\Sigma$ is positive definite ($\mathbf{w}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{w} > 0$ for every nonzero $\mathbf{w}$), it factors as $\Sigma = BB^\top$ with $B$ lower triangular. This is the **Cholesky factorization**, `np.linalg.cholesky` in NumPy. Set $\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol\mu + B\mathbf{z}$ with $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$. By the affine rule its covariance is $BIB^\top = \Sigma$. A VAE (see [Build a Generative Model: 2. VAE](3.10-RP3-vae-encoder-decoder-generation.md)) samples this way in the diagonal case, writing each coordinate as $\mu_i + \sigma_i z_i$. All the randomness is in $\mathbf{z}$, so gradients can flow back to $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$.

**Conditioning.** Split $\mathbf{x}$ into blocks $\mathbf{x}_a$ and $\mathbf{x}_b$, with $\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\Sigma$ split to match. Then $\mathbf{x}_a$ given $\mathbf{x}_b$ is Gaussian with mean and covariance

$$
\boldsymbol\mu_a + \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_b - \boldsymbol\mu_b)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab}\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}\Sigma_{ba} .
$$

With one variable in each block this is the two-variable formula above. The covariance does not depend on the observed value, and it is never larger than $\Sigma_{aa}$. Gaussian processes, Kalman filters and Bayesian linear regression all use this formula.
