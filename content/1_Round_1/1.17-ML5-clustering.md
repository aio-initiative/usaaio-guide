## Overview

k-means answers a narrow question. Given a number $k$, find $k$ centers and assign each point to the nearest one, so that points sharing a center are close together. No labels are involved. A clustering is scored by one number, the sum of squared distances from the points to their centers, and the algorithm that drives that number down is two steps repeated until nothing moves.

[2026 Round 2, Day 2](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-2-day-2-problems.pdf), Problem 5 handed out 400 points drawn from four function families at once: linear, logarithmic, exponential and trigonometric. The statement's own baseline clustered the points first and then fitted a curve to each cluster. The groups were curves, not blobs, which is one of the cases k-means handles worst, so that baseline was a floor to beat. The assumptions section below explains why.

## Example: six points, two centers

The points are $(1,1)$, $(2,1)$, $(1,2)$, $(5,5)$, $(6,5)$ and $(5,6)$, and $k = 2$. The starting centers are $c_1 = (1,1)$ and $c_2 = (2,1)$, both inside the left-hand group. Run the assign and update steps until nothing changes, and compute the final inertia. Then see how likely k-means++, the seeding rule described below, is to avoid a start like this one.

Both starting centers sit in one group, which is the kind of start that makes a bad clustering possible. The explanation runs the algorithm on it and then shows what k-means++ does to the odds.

## Explanation

### The objective

We have $n$ points $\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a number of clusters $k$. A clustering is two things: an **assignment** of each point to one of the sets $C_1, \dots, C_k$, and a **center** $\boldsymbol{\mu}_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$ per cluster. The score is the within-cluster sum of squared distances, the **inertia**:

$$
J = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in C_j} \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j \rVert^2 .
$$

Small $J$ means tight clusters. The goal is to choose both the assignment and the centers to make $J$ small. Searching over all assignments is hopeless, there are $k^n$ of them, so k-means solves two easier problems instead, each exactly, and alternates between them.

<ProblemCard id="km-spread-from-distances" />

### The two steps

**Fix the centers.** Each point's term in $J$ depends only on which center it is assigned to, so the best assignment sends every point to its nearest center.

**Fix the assignment.** Cluster $j$'s contribution is $g(\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in C_j} \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu} \rVert^2$. Its derivative with respect to $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is $-2 \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in C_j} (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu})$, which is zero when

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu}_j = \frac{1}{|C_j|} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in C_j} \mathbf{x}_i ,
$$

the mean of the cluster. $g$ is a bowl in $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, so the mean is its one lowest point. Each step minimizes $J$ over one set of variables with the other held fixed, so neither step can increase $J$.

<ProblemCard id="km-update-for-manhattan" />

### Lloyd's algorithm

1. **Initialize** $k$ centers.
2. **Assign** each point to its nearest center.
3. **Update** each center to the mean of its points.
4. **Repeat** 2 and 3 until the assignment stops changing.

$J$ never increases, $J \ge 0$, and there are finitely many assignments, so the loop stops. What it stops at is a local minimum that depends on the start. A round costs $nkd$ operations, every point against every center. `MiniBatchKMeans` updates the centers from small random batches and trades a little inertia for speed on large data.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/kmeans.svg" alt="Three panels showing k-means: points with random cluster centers, then reassigned points with moved centers, then clean converged clusters.">
Three blobs, three centers. Assign, move, repeat.
</Figure>

For the example, round 1 assigns $(1,1)$ and $(1,2)$ to $c_1 = (1,1)$ and everything else to $c_2 = (2,1)$. The point $(2,1)$ is $c_2$ itself, and the three far points are all closer to $(2,1)$ than to $(1,1)$. The inertia of that assignment is 92, almost all of it the far points' distances to $(2,1)$. The update moves $c_1$ to $(1, 1.5)$ and $c_2$ to the mean of $(2,1)$, $(5,5)$, $(6,5)$ and $(5,6)$, which is $(4.5, 4.25)$. Round 2 assigns $(2,1)$ to $c_1$, since it is now 1.25 from $c_1$ and 16.8 from $c_2$ in squared distance, and the inertia falls to 8.69. The update gives $c_1 = (4/3, 4/3)$ and $c_2 = (16/3, 16/3)$. Round 3 changes nothing. The final inertia is six squared distances to the two means, $4/3$ per cluster, so $J = 8/3 = 2.667$.

<ProblemCard id="km-where-lloyd-can-stop" />

### Seeding and restarts

Two centers that start in the same group can end there, with one group split in half and the other group handed a single center. **k-means++** makes that unlikely. It picks the first center uniformly at random. Each later one is drawn from the remaining points with probability proportional to $D(\mathbf{x})^2$, the squared distance to the nearest center already chosen, so the next seed is probably far from the seeds so far. For the example, with $(1,1)$ as the first seed, the squared distances are $0, 1, 1, 32, 41, 41$, summing to 116. The probability that the second seed is one of the three far points is $114/116 = 0.983$. With this seeding the expected inertia is provably within a factor of $\mathcal{O}(\log k)$ of the best possible, and it is the default in scikit-learn.

The other defense is restarts: run from several seedings and keep the lowest inertia, which is `n_init`. The implementation compares the two seedings on eight blobs. Twenty single runs from random seeds reach inertia between 951 and 5204, median 1518. Twenty runs with k-means++ seeding stay between 949 and 1012.

<ProblemCard id="km-third-seed" />

### Choosing k

Inertia falls as $k$ grows, all the way to 0 at $k = n$, so it cannot pick $k$ by itself. Two diagnostics are standard. The **elbow** is the $k$ where inertia stops falling fast. The **silhouette** of a point is $s = (b - a) / \max(a, b)$, with $a$ its mean distance to its own cluster and $b$ its mean distance to the nearest other cluster. It runs from −1 to 1. Near 1 the point is much closer to its own cluster than to any other, near 0 it sits on a boundary, and below 0 it is probably in the wrong cluster. The mean silhouette over the points is highest when the clusters are tight and well separated.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/kmeans-choose-k.svg" alt="Left: inertia against k from 1 to 8, falling steeply to k = 4 and flattening after. Right: mean silhouette against k from 2 to 8, highest at k = 2, dipping at 3, with a second peak at 4, then falling.">
Four blobs. The elbow is at $k = 4$. The silhouette is highest at $k = 2$, with a second peak at 4, because three of the four blobs sit close together and the fourth is far from all of them.
</Figure>

The two diagnostics disagree on the four blobs in the figure, and both are right about something. Inertia drops from 2222 at $k = 3$ to 1125 at $k = 4$ and only to 992 at $k = 5$, an elbow at 4. The silhouette is 0.704 at $k = 2$ and 0.638 at $k = 4$. The data has one blob far from three that sit close together, and splitting it into those two groups gives a cleaner separation than splitting it into four. Both are diagnostics, not answers. The number of clusters is usually decided by what the clusters are for.

<ProblemCard id="km-silhouette-on-a-line" />

### What k-means assumes

A point goes to the nearest center, so each cluster is the set of points nearer to its center than to any other: a region with straight edges and no dents. That suits round blobs of similar spread. It does not suit clusters that are elongated, nested, or very different in spread.

Distinguish an unlucky start, which restarts and k-means++ fix, from a mismatch between the objective and the grouping you wanted, which they do not.

The implementation measures the agreement with the true labels, as an adjusted Rand index, on round blobs of equal spread and on four shapes k-means was not built for:

<Figure src="assets/module-media/kmeans-assumptions.svg" alt="Four panels of k-means results: three elongated diagonal blobs cut across their long axes, three blobs of very different spread with the wide one split and the tight ones merged, three blobs of sizes 400, 60 and 20 clustered correctly, and two concentric rings cut in half by a straight line.">
k-means on shapes it was not built for. Elongated blobs are cut across, the widest of three unequal blobs is split, unequal sizes are fine, and the rings are cut in half.
</Figure>

Round blobs of equal spread score 0.944. Elongated blobs score 0.580, blobs of unequal spread 0.803, and two rings −0.001, chance, because no straight line separates an inner ring from an outer one. Blobs of unequal size, 400, 60 and 20 points, score 1.000: size by itself is not the problem, shape is.

Three more things matter:

- **Scale.** k-means is a distance method. Multiplying one column of the four blobs by 100 drops the score from 0.944 to 0.579, and standardizing restores it to 0.94, the same lesson as [kNN](1.29-ML10-decision-trees-knn.md).
- **Outliers.** A center is a mean, and a far point drags it or takes a cluster for itself, so clip or remove outliers first.
- **Dimension.** In many dimensions every distance looks alike, and reducing with [PCA](2.17-ML12-principal-component-analysis.md) before clustering helps.

<ProblemCard id="km-transform-that-changes-nothing" />

When the blobs are not round, the usual next step is a [Gaussian mixture model](1.31-ML5-clustering-methods.md), taught later in Clustering Methods as k-means with two restrictions lifted.

### Scoring a clustering against labels

Labels exist when a method is being checked on data where the answer is known. One detail then catches almost everyone: cluster IDs are arbitrary. A run may call the first class cluster 1 and the next run may call it cluster 0, so comparing IDs to labels directly, as accuracy does, can score a perfect clustering at zero. Two fixes are standard.

- **Match, then score.** Build the table of classes against clusters and pick the one-to-one pairing with the most agreements. That is an assignment problem, solved exactly by the Hungarian algorithm, in SciPy as `linear_sum_assignment`.
- **Use a score that never reads IDs.** The **adjusted Rand index** (ARI) looks at every pair of points and asks whether the two labelings agree on putting the pair together or apart, then subtracts the agreement chance alone would produce. It is 1 for identical partitions, about 0 for random ones, and can go negative.

The implementation shows both on the two rings, where k-means scores chance and spectral clustering, which splits a nearest-neighbor graph instead of the plane, scores 1.

## Implementation

### The example from scratch

Lloyd's algorithm is an `argmin` and a mean. The loop prints each round's assignment and the inertia of that assignment under the current centers:

```python
import numpy as np

X = np.array([[1, 1], [2, 1], [1, 2], [5, 5], [6, 5], [5, 6]], dtype=float)
C = np.array([[1, 1], [2, 1]], dtype=float)                 # both starting centers in the left group

def assign(X, C):                                           # index of the nearest center, per point
    return ((X[:, None, :] - C[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2).argmin(axis=1)

def update(X, labels, k):                                   # each center becomes the mean of its points
    return np.stack([X[labels == j].mean(axis=0) for j in range(k)])

def inertia(X, labels, C):
    return ((X - C[labels]) ** 2).sum()

for r in range(1, 10):
    labels = assign(X, C)
    print(f"round {r}: assignment {labels}, inertia {inertia(X, labels, C):.3f}")
    new = update(X, labels, 2)
    if np.allclose(new, C):
        break
    C = new
    print(f"         centers -> {np.round(C, 3).tolist()}")

d2 = ((X - X[0]) ** 2).sum(axis=1)                          # k-means++: D^2 after seeding at (1, 1)
print("D^2 from (1,1):", d2, " P(second seed in the far group):", round(d2[3:].sum() / d2.sum(), 3))
```

```output
round 1: assignment [0 1 0 1 1 1], inertia 92.000
         centers -> [[1.0, 1.5], [4.5, 4.25]]
round 2: assignment [0 0 0 1 1 1], inertia 8.688
         centers -> [[1.333, 1.333], [5.333, 5.333]]
round 3: assignment [0 0 0 1 1 1], inertia 2.667
D^2 from (1,1): [ 0.  1.  1. 32. 41. 41.]  P(second seed in the far group): 0.983
```

Change the points or the starting centers and watch the rounds:

<EmbeddedPyEditor packages="numpy">
import numpy as np

X = np.array([[1, 1], [2, 1], [1, 2], [5, 5], [6, 5], [5, 6]], dtype=float)   # change the points
C = np.array([[1, 1], [2, 1]], dtype=float)                                   # and the starting centers

for r in range(1, 20):
    labels = ((X[:, None, :] - C[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2).argmin(axis=1)
    inertia = ((X - C[labels]) ** 2).sum()
    print(f"round {r}: assignment {labels}  inertia {inertia:.3f}")
    new = np.stack([X[labels == j].mean(axis=0) if np.any(labels == j) else C[j] for j in range(len(C))])
    if np.allclose(new, C):
        break
    C = new
print("final centers:", np.round(C, 3).tolist())
</EmbeddedPyEditor>

The rounds, the centers $(4/3, 4/3)$ and $(16/3, 16/3)$, the inertia $8/3$ and the probability 0.983 are the hand values. The `if np.any(labels == j) else C[j]` guard is not decoration. A center that loses every point takes the mean of an empty set, which NumPy returns as `nan` after a warning rather than raising. Every distance to a `nan` center is then `nan`, and `argmin` ranks `nan` below every number, so every point piles onto the dead center, the centers flip between two states and the loop never converges, ending on `nan` centers instead of an error. It usually shows up only on one unlucky seed of a long sweep. In scikit-learn, `KMeans` from the same start reaches the same centers, and from its default k-means++ seeding with ten restarts it reaches the same inertia:

```python
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, init=np.array([[1, 1], [2, 1]], dtype=float), n_init=1).fit(X)
print("centers from the example's start:\n", np.round(km.cluster_centers_, 3), " inertia", round(km.inertia_, 4))
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
print("k-means++, ten restarts: inertia", round(km.inertia_, 4))
```

```output
centers from the example's start:
 [[1.333 1.333]
 [5.333 5.333]]  inertia 2.6667
k-means++, ten restarts: inertia 2.6667
```

### Choosing k, measured

Four blobs. Inertia and mean silhouette for $k$ from 1 to 8, and the silhouette of the true labels for comparison:

```python
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

Xb, yb = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=4, cluster_std=1.0, random_state=1)
for k in range(1, 9):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(Xb)
    sil = silhouette_score(Xb, km.labels_) if k > 1 else float("nan")
    print(f"k = {k}: inertia {km.inertia_:8.1f}   silhouette {sil:.3f}")
print("silhouette of the true labels", round(silhouette_score(Xb, yb), 3))
```

### What k-means assumes, measured

The adjusted Rand index between the true labels and k-means on the five shapes, then the four blobs with one column multiplied by 100:

```python
from sklearn.datasets import make_circles
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

Xa, ya = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=3, random_state=170)
Xa = Xa @ np.array([[0.6, -0.6], [-0.4, 0.8]])                            # stretched along a diagonal
Xv, yv = make_blobs(n_samples=600, centers=3, cluster_std=[1.0, 2.5, 0.5], random_state=170)
Xs, ys = make_blobs(n_samples=[400, 60, 20], random_state=170)
Xr, yr = make_circles(n_samples=600, factor=0.4, noise=0.05, random_state=0)
for name, Xd, yd, k in [("round, equal blobs", Xb, yb, 4), ("elongated blobs", Xa, ya, 3),
                        ("unequal spread", Xv, yv, 3), ("unequal sizes", Xs, ys, 3), ("two rings", Xr, yr, 2)]:
    pred = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xd)
    print(f"{name:<20} ARI {adjusted_rand_score(yd, pred):.3f}")

Xw = Xb.copy()
Xw[:, 0] *= 100                                                            # one column in different units
print("four blobs, x1 times 100:", round(adjusted_rand_score(yb, KMeans(4, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xw)), 3))
print("the same, standardized:  ", round(adjusted_rand_score(yb, KMeans(4, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(StandardScaler().fit_transform(Xw))), 3))
```

### Scoring against labels

Two rings, one inside the other, clustered by k-means and by spectral clustering, scored three ways:

```python
import warnings
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment
from sklearn.cluster import SpectralClustering
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix

X, y = make_circles(n_samples=600, factor=0.4, noise=0.05, random_state=0)
km = KMeans(2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(X)
with warnings.catch_warnings():
    warnings.simplefilter("ignore")       # the two rings share no edges, which is the point
    sc = SpectralClustering(2, affinity="nearest_neighbors", n_neighbors=10,
                            random_state=0).fit_predict(X)

def matched_accuracy(y, pred):
    C = confusion_matrix(y, pred)                       # rows: true classes, columns: clusters
    rows, cols = linear_sum_assignment(-C)              # the pairing with the most agreements
    return C[rows, cols].sum() / len(y)

print(f"  {'clustering':<24}{'naive acc':>10}{'matched acc':>13}{'ARI':>8}")
for name, pred in [("k-means", km), ("spectral", sc), ("spectral, IDs swapped", 1 - sc)]:
    print(f"  {name:<24}{np.mean(pred == y):>10.3f}{matched_accuracy(y, pred):>13.3f}"
          f"{adjusted_rand_score(y, pred):>8.3f}")
```

```output
  clustering               naive acc  matched acc     ARI
  k-means                      0.508        0.508  -0.001
  spectral                     1.000        1.000   1.000
  spectral, IDs swapped        0.000        1.000   1.000
```

k-means cuts the plane in half with a straight line, so each half holds pieces of both rings, and ARI reports that as chance. Spectral clustering connects each point to its nearest neighbors and splits the resulting graph, which follows the rings exactly. The last row is the same perfect clustering with its two IDs exchanged: accuracy drops to zero while the matched accuracy and ARI do not move.
