## Overview

A fraud detector catches 90% of fraud and wrongly flags only 3% of legitimate transactions. It flags a transaction. Is that transaction probably fraud? That depends on one number the two rates leave out: how common fraud is in the first place.

The USAAIO syllabus lists probability and statistics among the math foundations, with Bayes' rule as its first example. Classifiers output probabilities, and reading them correctly means knowing which way a conditional probability points. Later modules use these ideas to fit models to data and to build the losses that train classifiers.

You need fractions, percentages and exponents. The last section uses a curve over the numbers from 0 to 1, and it explains what that curve means.

## Example: is a flagged transaction fraud?

1% of transactions are fraud. A detector flags 90% of fraudulent transactions. This is its **sensitivity**. It correctly passes 97% of legitimate ones, which is its **specificity**. The other 3% of legitimate transactions get flagged by mistake. That 3% is the **false-positive rate**, and it equals 1 minus the specificity.

1. A transaction is flagged. What is the probability it is fraud?
2. A second detector with the same rates also flags it. Its mistakes are independent of the first detector's given whether the transaction is fraud. What is the probability now?

Guess each answer before you read on.

## Explanation
### Probability as counting

<Figure src="assets/module-media/fraud-flag-tree.svg" alt="A probability tree. 10,000 transactions split into 100 fraud (1%) and 9,900 legitimate (99%). The 100 fraud split into 90 flagged (90%) and 10 not flagged (10%). The 9,900 legitimate split into 297 flagged (3%) and 9,603 not flagged (97%). Arrows carry the two flagged groups into one bar of all 387 flagged transactions, drawn to scale: a highlighted block of 90 fraud above a block of 297 legitimate, labeled fraud: 90 / 387, about 0.233.">

The 10,000 transactions split first by fraud, then by the detector's flag. Stacking the two flagged groups gives all 387 flagged transactions, drawn to scale. Fraud is 90 of the 387, a share of about 0.233, because 3% of 9,900 is more than 90% of 100.

</Figure>

Picture 10,000 transactions. 1% of them, 100, are fraud, and the detector flags 90 of those. Of the other 9,900, 3% get flagged by mistake, which is 297. Put the truth in the columns and the detector's verdict in the rows:

|  | fraud | legitimate | total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| flagged | 90 | 297 | 387 |
| not flagged | 10 | 9,603 | 9,613 |
| total | 100 | 9,900 | 10,000 |

[3Blue1Brown's Bayes video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HZGCoVF3YvM) draws the same kind of table as a box of people.

Pick one of these transactions at random. Each transaction you could pick is an **outcome**, and the set of all 10,000 is the **sample space**. An **event** is a set of outcomes, such as "fraud" (100 outcomes) or "flagged" (387 outcomes). When every outcome is equally likely, the **probability** of an event $A$ is the fraction of outcomes in it:

$$
P(A) = \frac{\text{outcomes in } A}{\text{all outcomes}}, \qquad P(\text{flagged}) = \frac{387}{10{,}000} = 0.0387 .
$$

A probability is always between 0 and 1. Three rules come from counting.

- **Complement:** the outcomes not in $A$ are all the others, so $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$. Here $P(\text{not flagged}) = 1 - 0.0387 = 0.9613$.
- **Or:** to count the outcomes in $A$ or $B$, add the two counts. The outcomes in both were counted twice, so subtract them once: $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$. This is **inclusion-exclusion**. For "fraud or flagged" it gives $(100 + 387 - 90)/10{,}000 = 0.0397$.
- Events that cannot both happen, like "fraud" and "legitimate", are **disjoint**. Nothing is counted twice, so $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$.

Other books write $A \cap B$ for "$A$ and $B$", $A \cup B$ for "$A$ or $B$", and $A^c$ or $\neg A$ for "not $A$".

### Counting outcomes

The table handed us the counts. With dice and cards you count the outcomes yourself. Roll two fair dice. Each outcome is a pair, like (2, 5). The first die has 6 options and the second has 6, so there are $6 \times 6 = 36$ outcomes, all equally likely. The event "the sum is 7" holds (1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2) and (6, 1), so $P(\text{sum is } 7) = 6/36 = 1/6$.

The basic tool is the **counting principle**. A choice made in two stages, with $n_1$ options at the first stage and $n_2$ at the second, can be made in $n_1 n_2$ ways. More stages multiply in the same way. Four dice can land in $6^4 = 1296$ ways, and $5^4 = 625$ of them show no six. By the complement rule, the chance of at least one six is $1 - 625/1296 \approx 0.518$.

**Ordered choices without repeats (permutations).** Rank your top 3 of 10 features as first, second and third. There are 10 options for first place, then 9, then 8, so $10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 720$ rankings. In general, the number of ordered lists of $k$ different items taken from $n$ is

$$
\frac{n!}{(n - k)!} ,
$$

where $n! = n(n-1)\cdots 2 \cdot 1$ is read "$n$ factorial". For the features, $10!/7! = 720$.

**Unordered choices (combinations).** Now suppose you only care which 3 features you picked. Each set of 3 appears $3! = 6$ times among the 720 rankings, once per order, so there are $720/6 = 120$ sets. The number of unordered sets of $k$ items from $n$ is written $\binom{n}{k}$ and read "$n$ choose $k$":

$$
\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n - k)!} .
$$

A 5-card hand from a 52-card deck is a combination, so there are $\binom{52}{5} = 2{,}598{,}960$ hands. All five cards are hearts in $\binom{13}{5} = 1287$ of them, a probability of about 0.0005. To choose from two groups at once, multiply. A hand with exactly 2 hearts picks 2 of the 13 hearts and 3 of the 39 other cards. That gives $\binom{13}{2}\binom{39}{3} = 78 \cdot 9139 = 712{,}842$ hands, a probability of about 0.274.

### Conditional probability

Back to the table. Read down the fraud column: of the 100 fraud transactions, 90 are flagged, so the chance of a flag given fraud is $90/100 = 0.9$. That is the sensitivity we started with. Now read along the flagged row instead: of the 387 flagged transactions, 90 are fraud, so the chance of fraud given a flag is $90/387 \approx 0.233$. Learning that a transaction is flagged shrinks the sample space from 10,000 outcomes to the 387 flagged ones.

The **conditional probability** of $A$ given $B$ is the fraction of $B$'s outcomes that are also in $A$. It is written $P(A \mid B)$, and the bar is read "given". Dividing both counts by 10,000 turns them into probabilities:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flag}) = \frac{0.009}{0.0387} \approx 0.233 .
$$

The same works for dice. Given that two dice sum to 8, the sample space shrinks to (2, 6), (3, 5), (4, 4), (5, 3) and (6, 2). Two of these five contain a 3, so $P(\text{a } 3 \mid \text{sum is } 8) = 2/5$.

<ProblemCard id="pb-at-least-one-failing-test" />

Multiply both sides of the definition by $P(B)$ to get the **multiplication rule**, $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)$. We used it to build the table. The fraud-and-flagged cell is 90 of 10,000 because

$$
P(\text{fraud and flag}) = P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud})\,P(\text{fraud}) = 0.9 \times 0.01 = 0.009 .
$$

$P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ are usually different numbers. Here $P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud}) = 0.9$, but $P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flag}) \approx 0.233$.

### The law of total probability

Where do the 387 flags come from? A flagged transaction is either fraud that was caught, or legitimate and flagged by mistake. These two cases do not overlap, so their probabilities add:

$$
P(\text{flag}) = 0.90 \times 0.01 + 0.03 \times 0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0297 = 0.0387 .
$$

In general, split the possibilities into cases $A_1, \dots, A_m$ that do not overlap and together cover everything. Find the probability of $B$ inside each case, weight it by how likely the case is, and add:

$$
P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} P(B \mid A_i)\,P(A_i) .
$$

This is the **law of total probability**. With two cases, $A$ and not $A$, it reads $P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid \text{not } A)\,P(\text{not } A)$.

### Bayes' theorem

The detector's rates give $P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud})$, and we want $P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flag})$. The table did this by counting. To do it with probabilities, write $P(\text{fraud and flag})$ with the multiplication rule in both directions:

$$
P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flag})\,P(\text{flag}) = P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud})\,P(\text{fraud}) .
$$

Divide by $P(\text{flag})$, and write $A$ for the hypothesis (fraud) and $B$ for the evidence (a flag). The result is **Bayes' theorem**:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)} .
$$

The parts have names. $P(A)$ is the **prior**, what you believe before the evidence. $P(B \mid A)$ is the **likelihood**, how probable the evidence is if the hypothesis is true. $P(A \mid B)$ is the **posterior**, what you believe after. The denominator $P(B)$ comes from the law of total probability. For the detector, $0.009 / 0.0387 \approx 0.233$.

Fewer than 1 in 4 flags is fraud. Legitimate transactions outnumber fraud 99 to 1, so 3% of them (297) is more than three times all the fraud caught (90). The 1% is the **base rate**, how common fraud is before you see any evidence. A low base rate pulls the posterior down even when the detector is good.

**More than two hypotheses.** Three machines, $M_1$, $M_2$ and $M_3$, make 50%, 30% and 20% of a factory's parts. Their defect rates are 1%, 2% and 4%. A part is defective. Which machine made it? Total probability gives $P(\text{defect}) = 0.005 + 0.006 + 0.008 = 0.019$. Bayes' theorem gives each machine's posterior:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(M_1 \mid \text{defect}) &= 0.005 / 0.019 \approx 0.263, \\
P(M_2 \mid \text{defect}) &= 0.006 / 0.019 \approx 0.316, \\
P(M_3 \mid \text{defect}) &= 0.008 / 0.019 \approx 0.421 .
\end{aligned}
$$

$M_3$ makes the fewest parts but is the most likely source. A rule that predicts the hypothesis with the largest posterior, here $M_3$, is called the **Bayes classifier**.

### Bayes' theorem in odds form

The **odds** of an event compare its probability with the probability that it does not happen, $P/(1 - P)$. They are often written as a ratio $a : b$, and odds of $a : b$ mean a probability of $a/(a + b)$. The prior odds of fraud are $1 : 99$.

Write Bayes' theorem for $A$ and for not $A$, with the same evidence $B$. Divide the first by the second, and $P(B)$ cancels:

$$
\frac{P(A \mid B)}{P(\text{not } A \mid B)} = \frac{P(A)}{P(\text{not } A)} \times \frac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B \mid \text{not } A)} .
$$

In words, posterior odds equal prior odds times the **likelihood ratio**. The likelihood ratio says how many times more likely the evidence is if $A$ is true than if it is false. For the detector it is $0.90/0.03 = 30$. So the posterior odds are $1 : 99$ times 30, which is $30 : 99 = 10 : 33$, a probability of $10/43 \approx 0.233$.

Posterior odds also say when to act. Suppose blocking a legitimate transaction costs 1 dollar and letting a fraud through costs 50 dollars. If the transaction is fraud with probability $q$, blocking costs 1 dollar with probability $1 - q$, so its **expected cost**, each cost times its probability, is $1 - q$ dollars. Letting it through has expected cost $50q$ dollars. Block when blocking is cheaper in expectation, $1 - q < 50q$, which is the same as posterior odds $q/(1 - q) > 1/50$. A flagged transaction has odds $10 : 33$, far above $1 : 50$, so it is blocked.

### Independence

Two events are **independent** when knowing one does not change the chance of the other: $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$. Put this into the definition $P(A \mid B) = P(A \text{ and } B)/P(B)$ and multiply both sides by $P(B)$:

$$
P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B) .
$$

Fraud and a flag are not independent, because $P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud}) = 0.9$ while $P(\text{flag}) = 0.0387$.

Two events can be independent even when they involve the same die. With two dice, "the first die is even" has probability $1/2$ and "the sum is 7" has probability $1/6$. Both happen for (2, 5), (4, 3) and (6, 1), so $P(\text{both}) = 3/36 = 1/12 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac16$. Whatever the first die shows, one value of the second die makes 7. So learning the first die tells you nothing about whether the sum is 7.

Two events are **conditionally independent** given $C$ if, once you know whether $C$ happened, learning one tells you nothing more about the other. Two detectors that make their mistakes separately are conditionally independent given whether the transaction is fraud. Given fraud, both flag with probability

$$
P(\text{flag}_1 \text{ and } \text{flag}_2 \mid \text{fraud}) = P(\text{flag}_1 \mid \text{fraud})\,P(\text{flag}_2 \mid \text{fraud}) = 0.9^2 ,
$$

and given a legitimate transaction, with probability $0.03^2$. Overall the flags are not independent. Split on fraud with the law of total probability:

$$
P(\text{both flag}) = 0.01 \times 0.9^2 + 0.99 \times 0.03^2 = 0.0081 + 0.000891 \approx 0.0090 .
$$

If the flags were independent, this would be $0.0387^2 \approx 0.0015$, about six times smaller. One flag makes fraud more likely, and fraud makes a second flag more likely.

A **naive Bayes** classifier makes this assumption about its features. A naive Bayes spam filter finds $P(\text{words} \mid \text{spam})$ by multiplying $P(\text{word} \mid \text{spam})$ over the words in the email. Bayes' theorem then turns this into $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{words})$. The product assumes the words are conditionally independent given the class (spam or not). Real words are not ("New" is often followed by "York"), but naive Bayes filters still work well for spam in practice.

### Solving the focus problem

**Part 1.** The prior odds of fraud are $1 : 99$, and one flag has likelihood ratio 30. The posterior odds are $30 : 99 = 10 : 33$, so $P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flag}) = 10/43 \approx 0.233$. The table agrees: 90 of the 387 flagged transactions are fraud.

**Part 2.** After the first flag, the posterior odds $10 : 33$ become the prior odds for the second detector. Its flag is conditionally independent of the first given the truth, so its likelihood ratio multiplies in again:

$$
\frac{1}{99} \times 30 \times 30 = \frac{900}{99} = \frac{100}{11}, \qquad P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{two flags}) = \frac{100}{100 + 11} = \frac{100}{111} \approx 0.901 .
$$

The second step needs conditional independence. Two detectors that make the same mistakes add almost no new evidence, and multiplying by 30 again would overstate the odds.

### Bayes for an unknown probability

So far the unknown was yes or no: fraud or not. Often it is a number. An ad was clicked 7 times in 10 views. What is its click rate $p$?

The simplest estimate is the fraction of views that were clicks, $7/10 = 0.7$. ([Statistical Inference](1.12-PS3-statistics-hypothesis-testing.md) shows it is the value of $p$ that makes 7 clicks in 10 views most probable.) Bayes' theorem also lets us use a prior belief about $p$.

Since $p$ can be any number from 0 to 1, a prior for it is a curve over the interval $[0, 1]$. The higher the curve above a value, the more plausible that value. The total area under the curve is 1. The **Beta(a, b)** curve has height $p^{a-1}(1 - p)^{b-1}$ times a constant that makes the area 1. The positive numbers $a$ and $b$ set its shape. Beta(1, 1) is flat, so every rate is equally plausible. The average value of $p$ under Beta(a, b), weighting each value by the curve's height, is $a/(a + b)$. [Expectation and Joint Distributions](1.11-PS2-expectations-multivariate-distributions.md) explains averages over a curve.

Now the data. Each view is a click with probability $p$ and a non-click with probability $1 - p$, independently of the other views. So one particular sequence of $k$ clicks and $n - k$ non-clicks has probability $p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}$. This is the likelihood. Bayes' theorem says the posterior is prior times likelihood divided by $P(\text{data})$. That denominator is the same number for every $p$, so the posterior curve has the shape of prior times likelihood:

$$
p^{a-1}(1 - p)^{b-1} \cdot p^k(1 - p)^{n-k} = p^{a+k-1}(1 - p)^{b+n-k-1} .
$$

This is the shape of Beta$(a + k,\ b + n - k)$. So the posterior is again a Beta curve: add the clicks to $a$ and the non-clicks to $b$. You can read $a$ and $b$ as imaginary clicks and non-clicks seen before the data.

From a flat prior, 7 clicks in 10 views give Beta(8, 4), with average $8/12 \approx 0.667$. That is a little below 0.7, pulled toward $\tfrac12$ by the prior. In general, $k$ clicks in $n$ views turn the flat prior into Beta$(k + 1,\ n - k + 1)$, whose average is

$$
\frac{k + 1}{n + 2},
$$

which is **Laplace smoothing**. It acts as if you saw one extra click and one extra non-click. With 0 clicks in 3 views it gives $1/5$ instead of 0. With 0 in 1,000 it gives $1/1002$, almost 0, so smoothing matters only when data is scarce. A naive Bayes spam filter needs it. The filter multiplies one probability per word, so a word never seen in the training spam would make the whole product 0. Smoothing prevents that. [Statistical Inference](1.12-PS3-statistics-hypothesis-testing.md) uses the peak of a posterior like this one as an estimate, and Linear and Logistic Regression compares naive Bayes with logistic regression.

## Implementation

This code computes the focus problem from the formulas: total probability, then Bayes' theorem, then the odds form for two flags.

```python
import numpy as np

prior, sens, spec = 0.01, 0.90, 0.97
p_flag = sens * prior + (1 - spec) * (1 - prior)        # total probability
post1 = sens * prior / p_flag                            # Bayes
odds = prior / (1 - prior) * (sens / (1 - spec)) ** 2    # two flags, odds form
post2 = odds / (1 + odds)
print(f"P(flag)                 = {p_flag:.4f}")
print(f"P(fraud | one flag)     = {post1:.4f}")
print(f"P(fraud | two flags)    = {post2:.4f}")
```

```output
P(flag)                 = 0.0387
P(fraud | one flag)     = 0.2326
P(fraud | two flags)    = 0.9009
```

These match $90/387$ and $100/111$ from the hand calculation.

The next block simulates a million transactions. Each detector's mistakes are drawn separately given the truth, so the two detectors are conditionally independent.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 1_000_000
fraud = rng.random(n) < prior

def flag(is_fraud):
    u = rng.random(len(is_fraud))
    return np.where(is_fraud, u < sens, u < 1 - spec)    # independent given the truth

f1, f2 = flag(fraud), flag(fraud)
print(f"simulated P(fraud | one flag)  = {fraud[f1].mean():.3f}")
print(f"simulated P(fraud | two flags) = {fraud[f1 & f2].mean():.3f}")
print(f"flags are independent overall? P(both) = {np.mean(f1 & f2):.4f}, P(f1) P(f2) = {f1.mean() * f2.mean():.4f}")
```

```output
simulated P(fraud | one flag)  = 0.232
simulated P(fraud | two flags) = 0.904
flags are independent overall? P(both) = 0.0089, P(f1) P(f2) = 0.0015
```

The simulated posteriors, 0.232 and 0.904, sit close to the exact 0.233 and 0.901. Both detectors flag 0.0089 of transactions, about six times the 0.0015 that independent flags would give.

This block checks the counting answers with `math.comb` and `math.perm`, and the dice answers by listing all 36 outcomes of two dice. `Fraction` keeps the dice answers exact.

```python
import math, itertools
from fractions import Fraction

print("C(10, 3) =", math.comb(10, 3), "  perm(10, 3) =", math.perm(10, 3))
print(f"five hearts: C(13,5)/C(52,5) = {math.comb(13, 5) / math.comb(52, 5):.6f}")
print(f"two hearts: C(13,2) C(39,3)/C(52,5) = {math.comb(13, 2) * math.comb(39, 3) / math.comb(52, 5):.4f}")

rolls = list(itertools.product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))           # all 36 outcomes

def P(event, given=lambda r: True):
    space = [r for r in rolls if given(r)]
    return Fraction(sum(event(r) for r in space), len(space))

print("P(a 3 | sum is 8)        =", P(lambda r: 3 in r, given=lambda r: sum(r) == 8))
A = lambda r: r[0] % 2 == 0          # first die even
B = lambda r: sum(r) == 7            # sum is 7
print("P(A and B) =", P(lambda r: A(r) and B(r)), "  P(A) P(B) =", P(A) * P(B))
```

```output
C(10, 3) = 120   perm(10, 3) = 720
five hearts: C(13,5)/C(52,5) = 0.000495
two hearts: C(13,2) C(39,3)/C(52,5) = 0.2743
P(a 3 | sum is 8)        = 2/5
P(A and B) = 1/12   P(A) P(B) = 1/12
```

The counts and $2/5$ match the text. $P(A \text{ and } B)$ equals $P(A)\,P(B)$, so "first die even" and "sum is 7" are independent.

The last block checks the Beta update against a brute-force posterior. It multiplies prior and likelihood at 100,001 values of $p$, scales the results to sum to 1, and averages $p$ with those weights.

```python
k, n = 7, 10                          # 7 clicks in 10 views
a, b = 1, 1                           # flat prior, Beta(1, 1)
p = np.linspace(0, 1, 100001)
post = p ** (a - 1 + k) * (1 - p) ** (b - 1 + n - k)   # prior times likelihood
post /= post.sum()
print(f"grid posterior mean   {np.sum(p * post):.4f}")
print(f"Beta(a+k, b+n-k) mean {(a + k) / (a + b + n):.4f}")
print(f"k/n (no prior)        {k / n:.4f}")
```

```output
grid posterior mean   0.6667
Beta(a+k, b+n-k) mean 0.6667
k/n (no prior)        0.7000
```

The grid and the Beta formula agree to four decimals. Both sit below 0.7 because the flat prior pulls the estimate toward $\tfrac12$.

> **Warning.**
>
> A detector that catches 90% of fraud does not make a flagged transaction 90% likely to be fraud. Before quoting any conditional probability, say which event is given, and check whether the base rate has been used.

<Optional title="The birthday problem">

How many people must be in a room before two probably share a birthday? Counting the complement, the chance that $n$ people all have different birthdays (ignoring leap years) is

$$
\frac{365}{365}\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdots\frac{365 - n + 1}{365} .
$$

This drops below $\tfrac12$ at $n = 23$. It happens this early because 23 people form $\binom{23}{2} = 253$ pairs, and each pair is a chance to match.
