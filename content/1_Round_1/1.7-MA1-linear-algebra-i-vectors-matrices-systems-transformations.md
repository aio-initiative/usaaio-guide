## Overview

The 2025 paper asked for an affine map $W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ and its shapes, and the 2026 paper asked for a projection and its residual, a linear map split into outer products, and cosine similarity between word vectors.

## Example: fit a line through three points

You measure the points $(0, 1)$, $(1, 3)$ and $(2, 4)$ and want a line $y = c + m\,t$ through them, with intercept $c$ and slope $m$. The slope from the first point to the second is 2, and from the second to the third it is 1, so no line passes through all three. Which line comes closest, and what should "closest" mean?

Guess the slope before reading on.

## Explanation

### Vectors, lengths and unit vectors

A **vector** is a list of numbers, like $(3, 2)$, drawn as an arrow from the origin to that point. A **word embedding** is a vector a model learns for each word, so that words with similar meanings point in similar directions.

You add two vectors entry by entry, $(3, 2) + (1, 1) = (4, 3)$, and multiply a vector by a number to stretch it, $2 \cdot (3, 2) = (6, 4)$.

The **length** (or **norm**) of a vector comes from Pythagoras:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2} .
$$

For $\mathbf{v} = (2, 1, 2)$, $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = 3$. Dividing a vector by its length gives a **unit vector**, length 1, pointing the same way: $\hat{\mathbf{e}} = \mathbf{v} / \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \tfrac13 (2, 1, 2)$.

### The dot product measures agreement

The **dot product** multiplies matching entries and adds them up:

$$
\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + \dots + u_n v_n .
$$

Vectors here are columns, even when written inline as $(3, 2)$. The **transpose** $\mathbf{u}^\top$ lays a column on its side as a row, and a row times a column is the sum above, so the dot product is also written $\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}$.

- The dot product of a vector with itself is its squared length, $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2$.
- Geometrically, $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the arrows. So it is positive when the arrows point roughly the same way, negative when they point apart, and **0 when they are perpendicular**. Perpendicular vectors are also called **orthogonal**.

Dividing by both lengths gives **cosine similarity**:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert\,\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert} .
$$

It ranges from $-1$ (opposite) to 1 (same direction) and ignores length, so it is a common way to compare word embeddings.

### Projection: the shadow of one vector on another

How much of $\mathbf{x} = (3, 0, 3)$ points along $\hat{\mathbf{e}} = \tfrac13(2, 1, 2)$? Shine a light straight down onto the line through $\hat{\mathbf{e}}$.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/vector-projection.svg" alt="The flat plane that contains x and e-hat. A dashed horizontal line through the origin carries a short unit arrow, e-hat. A long arrow, x = (3, 0, 3), rises from the origin to the upper right. A colored arrow along the line, the shadow 4 e-hat = (8/3, 4/3, 8/3) of length 4, ends directly below the tip of x. A vertical arrow, the residual r = (1/3, -4/3, 1/3) of length square root of 2, joins the end of the shadow to the tip of x, with a right-angle mark where it meets the line.">
The residual meets the line at a right angle, so the shadow $4\hat{\mathbf{e}}$ is the closest point on the line to $\mathbf{x}$. Least squares, below, uses the same right angle.
</Figure>

Because $\hat{\mathbf{e}}$ has length 1, the signed length of the shadow of $\mathbf{x}$ is the dot product:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{e}} \cdot \mathbf{x} = \tfrac13(6 + 0 + 6) = 4 .
$$

The shadow itself is the vector $4\hat{\mathbf{e}} = (\tfrac83, \tfrac43, \tfrac83)$, called the **projection** of $\mathbf{x}$ onto $\hat{\mathbf{e}}$. What is left over is the **residual**:

$$
\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{x} - (\hat{\mathbf{e}} \cdot \mathbf{x})\,\hat{\mathbf{e}}
= (3, 0, 3) - (\tfrac83, \tfrac43, \tfrac83) = (\tfrac13, -\tfrac43, \tfrac13) .
$$

Check it: $\mathbf{r} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{e}} = \tfrac19(2 - 4 + 2) = 0$, so the residual is perpendicular to the line. From any other point on the line, the path to $\mathbf{x}$ is a step along the line plus the residual. By Pythagoras that path is longer than the residual, so the shadow is the closest point on the line to $\mathbf{x}$.

[Principal Component Analysis](2.17-ML12-principal-component-analysis.md) shrinks data by keeping only $\hat{\mathbf{e}} \cdot \mathbf{x}$ for the direction of greatest spread.

If $\mathbf{v}$ is not a unit vector, use $\hat{\mathbf{e}} = \mathbf{v}/\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert$:

$$
(\hat{\mathbf{e}} \cdot \mathbf{x})\,\hat{\mathbf{e}}
= \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{x}}{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}\,\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}
= \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf{v} .
$$

### A matrix moves every vector

Take the matrix

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

A matrix moves a column vector $\mathbf{x}$ to $A\mathbf{x}$. Where the basic arrows $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ go decides where every vector goes. **The first column of $A$ is where $(1, 0)$ lands, and the second column is where $(0, 1)$ lands.**

A map $T$ is **linear** if adding or stretching before the map gives the same result as after it. In symbols, $T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v})$ and $T(c\,\mathbf{u}) = c\,T(\mathbf{u})$. Multiplying by a matrix is linear. Since $(3, 2)$ is 3 copies of $(1, 0)$ plus 2 copies of $(0, 1)$, it lands on 3 copies of the first column plus 2 copies of the second:

$$
3 \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 2 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

A sum of multiples of vectors, like this one, is a **linear combination** of them. The **matrix-vector product** $A\mathbf{x}$ combines the columns of $A$ with the entries of $\mathbf{x}$ as weights. If $\mathbf{a}_j$ is the $j$-th column of $A$,

$$
A\mathbf{x} = x_1 \mathbf{a}_1 + x_2 \mathbf{a}_2 + \dots + x_n \mathbf{a}_n .
$$

By hand, most people go row by row: entry $i$ of $A\mathbf{x}$ is row $i$ dotted with $\mathbf{x}$. Here $2 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 2 = 8$ and $0 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 2 = 2$.

**Writing down the matrix of a map.** Its columns are where the basic arrows go. Rotating the plane $90^\circ$ counterclockwise sends $(1, 0)$ to $(0, 1)$ and $(0, 1)$ to $(-1, 0)$, so its matrix is $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

A matrix with $m$ rows and $n$ columns, shape $m \times n$ (rows first), takes $n$ numbers to $m$ numbers.

### Affine maps: a neural-network layer

A layer in a neural network multiplies by a matrix and then adds a vector:

$$
\mathbf{y} = W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} .
$$

This is an **affine map**. $W$ is the **weight matrix** and $\mathbf{b}$ the **bias**. For example, take

$$
W = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Row 1 of $W$ dotted with $\mathbf{x}$ is $1 + 0 + 2 = 3$, and row 2 gives $-1 + 6 + 1 = 6$. So $\mathbf{y} = (3, 6) + (1, 0) = (4, 6)$. $W$ is $2 \times 3$, so the map takes 3 numbers in and gives 2 out.

A layer from 784 inputs (a $28 \times 28$ image, flattened) to 128 outputs has $W$ of shape $128 \times 784$, $\mathbf{b}$ of length 128, and $128 \cdot 784 + 128 = 100{,}480$ parameters.

**Stacking affine maps gives another affine map.** Two layers with nothing in between compute

$$
W_2(W_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2 = (W_2 W_1)\mathbf{x} + (W_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2) ,
$$

which is one affine map with weight $W_2W_1$. Extra layers add nothing unless a nonlinear function sits between them, as [The Multilayer Perceptron](1.18-DL1-neural-networks-perceptrons-mlps-activations.md) shows.

### Multiplying matrices: one map after another

What single matrix does the same thing as "apply $B$, then apply $A$"? Call it $AB$. Its first column is where $(1, 0)$ ends up: $B$ sends it to the first column of $B$, and then $A$ moves that. So **column $j$ of $AB$ is $A$ times column $j$ of $B$**. Reading that product row by row, entry $(i, j)$ of $AB$ is row $i$ of $A$ dotted with column $j$ of $B$.

The shapes must chain: $(m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p$, with matching inner sizes.

**Cost.** Multiplying an $(m\times n)$ by an $(n\times p)$ matrix takes $mnp$ multiply-adds, one dot product of length $n$ for each of the $mp$ entries.

Take the **shear** $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which slides each point sideways by twice its height, and the rotation $B$ from before:

$$
SB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
BS = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

**Order matters**: rotating then shearing is not shearing then rotating. Three rules:

- $(AB)C = A(BC)$, so you can drop the brackets.
- $AB \ne BA$ in general.
- $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$, where the transpose $A^\top$ swaps rows and columns. Transposing a product reverses the order.

For $X$ of shape $100 \times 5$, $X^\top X$ is $5 \times 5$ and $XX^\top$ is $100 \times 100$. A matrix with $A^\top = A$ is **symmetric**. $X^\top X$ is symmetric, because $(X^\top X)^\top = X^\top (X^\top)^\top = X^\top X$.

Stack unit-length word vectors as the rows of $U$. Then entry $(i, j)$ of $UU^\top$ is the cosine of words $i$ and $j$, so one product gives every cosine similarity at once.

### Inverses and determinants

The **identity** matrix $I$ has 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere. It leaves every vector alone. The **inverse** $A^{-1}$ undoes $A$: $A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I$. Only square matrices can have one. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix,

$$
\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1}
= \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} .
$$

For $N = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, $ad - bc = 6 - 5 = 1$ and $N^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Multiplying $N\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)$ on the left by $N^{-1}$ gives $\mathbf{x} = N^{-1}(1, 2) = (3 - 2,\ -5 + 4) = (1, -1)$.

The number $ad - bc$ is the **determinant**. The matrix sends the unit square to a parallelogram whose sides are its two columns, and **the determinant is the area of that parallelogram**, with a minus sign if the matrix flips the plane over. For $A$ from earlier, the area is $2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0 = 2$.

When the determinant is 0, the matrix squashes the plane onto a line (or a point). Different inputs land on the same output, so nothing can undo it and there is no inverse. Two more rules: $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$, and $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$. To undo "$B$, then $A$", undo $A$ first.

### Independence, rank and the null space

The columns of a matrix are **linearly independent** if none of them is a linear combination of the others. In

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

the third column is the first plus the second, so the columns are dependent. Every output $M\mathbf{x}$ is a combination of the columns, and the third adds nothing new, so the outputs fill only a plane. The set of all outputs is the **column space**. A set of independent vectors that reaches all of it is a **basis**, and the number of vectors in a basis is the **dimension**.

The **rank** of a matrix is the number of independent columns, the dimension of its column space. $M$ has rank 2. Row rank always equals column rank, so the rank of an $m \times n$ matrix is at most $\min(m, n)$. A matrix whose columns are all independent has **full column rank**.

The rank of a product is at most the rank of each factor: $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \operatorname{rank}(A)$ and $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \operatorname{rank}(B)$.

The dependency gives an input that $M$ sends to zero: column 1 plus column 2 minus column 3 is $\mathbf{0}$, so $M(1, 1, -1) = \mathbf{0}$. All such inputs form the **null space**, written $\mathcal{N}(M)$. The **rank-nullity theorem** counts them: for an $m \times n$ matrix $A$,

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A) + \dim \mathcal{N}(A) = n .
$$

For $M$, $2 + 1 = 3$. Null vectors also count the solutions of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. If $\mathbf{b}$ is not in the column space, there are none. If $A\mathbf{x}_0 = \mathbf{b}$ and $A\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}$, then $A(\mathbf{x}_0 + \mathbf{z}) = \mathbf{b}$ too. So a solvable system has one solution when the null space holds only $\mathbf{0}$, and infinitely many otherwise.

A square matrix has an inverse when its rank is full, and only then. Full rank means the determinant is nonzero and the null space holds only $\mathbf{0}$.

To find a rank by hand, look for columns that are multiples or sums of earlier ones. If none jump out, use **elimination** (Strang's lecture 2): subtract multiples of rows until each row starts further right than the row above, then count the nonzero rows.

### Outer products and low rank

The dot product $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{v}$ turns two vectors into one number. Flip the order and you get the **outer product** $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top$, a whole matrix. For $\mathbf{u}$ of length $m$ and $\mathbf{v}$ of length $n$ it is $m \times n$, with entry $(i, j)$ equal to $u_i v_j$:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 4 & -2 \\ 6 & -3 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Every column is a multiple of $\mathbf{u}$, so an outer product of nonzero vectors has **rank 1**. The reverse also holds: every rank-1 matrix is an outer product. To split one, take any nonzero column as $\mathbf{u}$, and let $v_j$ be the number you multiply $\mathbf{u}$ by to get column $j$.

A rank-$r$ matrix is a sum of $r$ outer products. Put $r$ independent columns of the matrix side by side in $C$. Column $j$ of $R$ holds the weights that build column $j$ of the matrix from the columns of $C$. For $M$ above, take its first two columns as $C$. Column 3 of $M$ is $1 \cdot$(column 1) $+ 1 \cdot$(column 2), so column 3 of $R$ is $(1, 1)$:

$$
M = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}_{C}
\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}_{R}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\!\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
+ \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\!\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The last step works for any $C$ and $R$: $CR$ equals the sum of (column $k$ of $C$) times (row $k$ of $R$).

The fewest outer products that add up to a matrix is its rank.

### Least squares: projecting onto a column space

Back to the three points. A line $y = c + m\,t$ through all three would need

$$
\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}}_{A}
\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} c \\ m \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}}
= \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}} .
$$

Here $\mathbf{b}$ holds the measured $y$ values, not a bias. The errors are the vertical gaps $1 - c$, $3 - (c + m)$ and $4 - (c + 2m)$, the entries of $\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}$. We call a line best when the sum of the squared errors, $\lVert \mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x} \rVert^2$, is as small as possible. This is **least squares**.

Read $A\mathbf{x}$ by columns: every output is $c\,(1, 1, 1) + m\,(0, 1, 2)$. As $c$ and $m$ vary, these outputs fill a plane through the origin in 3D, the column space of $A$. The point $\mathbf{b}$ is not on that plane, because no line fits all three points. Since $\lVert \mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x} \rVert$ is the distance from $\mathbf{b}$ to $A\mathbf{x}$, we want the point on the plane closest to $\mathbf{b}$.

Write $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ ("x-hat") for the best $(c, m)$. Here the hat means "estimated", not "unit length". As with a line, the closest point leaves a residual $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ perpendicular to the plane. It is enough for $\mathbf{r}$ to be perpendicular to both columns of $A$, since then it is perpendicular to every combination of them. Those two dot products are the entries of $A^\top\mathbf{r}$, so

$$
A^\top(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A^\top A\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top \mathbf{b} .
$$

These are the **normal equations**.

### Solving the focus problem

Each entry of $A^\top A$ is a dot product of two columns of $A$: $(1, 1, 1) \cdot (1, 1, 1) = 3$, $(1, 1, 1) \cdot (0, 1, 2) = 3$ and $(0, 1, 2) \cdot (0, 1, 2) = 5$. Likewise $A^\top\mathbf{b} = (1 + 3 + 4,\ 0 + 3 + 8) = (8, 11)$. So the normal equations are

$$
3c + 3m = 8, \qquad 3c + 5m = 11 .
$$

Subtracting the first from the second gives $2m = 3$, so $m = 1.5$. Then $3c = 8 - 4.5 = 3.5$, so $c = 7/6$. The best line is $y = \tfrac{7}{6} + 1.5\,t$. Its slope sits halfway between the two slopes, 2 and 1.

### The projection matrix and the trace

When $A$ has full column rank, $A^\top A$ is invertible and

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf{b}, \qquad
P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top .
$$

$P$ is the **projection matrix**: $P\mathbf{b}$ is the closest point to $\mathbf{b}$ in the column space. $P$ satisfies $P^2 = P$, since projecting twice changes nothing. It is also symmetric, $P^\top = P$.

The **trace** $\operatorname{tr}(A)$ of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries. For $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ it is $1 + 4 = 5$. If $A$ is $m \times n$ and $B$ is $n \times m$, then $\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)$.

For the projection matrix the trace rule gives

$$
\operatorname{tr}(P) = \operatorname{tr}\big(A \cdot (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\big)
= \operatorname{tr}\big((A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top A\big) = \operatorname{tr}(I_n) = n .
$$

Here $n$ is the number of columns of $A$, which equals its rank because the columns are independent.

This is the formula behind [Linear & Logistic Regression](1.27-ML8-linear-logistic-regression.md). [Calculus and Gradients](1.9-MA3-calculus-gradients.md) gets the same normal equations by setting the gradient of $\lVert A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\rVert^2$ to zero.

<Optional title="Orthogonal matrices and Gram-Schmidt">

A square matrix $Q$ is **orthogonal** when its columns are perpendicular unit vectors. Entry $(i, j)$ of $Q^\top Q$ is column $i$ dotted with column $j$, so perpendicular unit columns mean $Q^\top Q = I$, and the inverse of $Q$ is its transpose. Multiplying by $Q$ keeps every dot product, because $(Q\mathbf{x}) \cdot (Q\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{x}^\top Q^\top Q\,\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y}$. So it keeps all lengths and angles too. Geometrically $Q$ is a rotation, possibly combined with a **reflection**, which flips space over like a mirror. Its columns are the sides of a unit square (in 3D, a unit cube), so $\det Q = \pm 1$, and the sign tells you which: $+1$ for a pure rotation and $-1$ when a reflection is involved. Because $Q$ keeps lengths, it never magnifies a rounding error. Its condition number is 1 (the QR box under Implementation defines the condition number), and algorithms built from orthogonal matrices stay accurate for this reason.

**Gram-Schmidt** turns independent columns $\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n$ into perpendicular unit vectors $\mathbf{q}_1, \dots, \mathbf{q}_n$ with the same column space. Take the columns in turn. From each one, subtract its projections onto the directions already found, then divide what is left by its length:

$$
\mathbf{v}_j = \mathbf{a}_j - \sum_{i<j} (\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{a}_j)\,\mathbf{q}_i,
\qquad
\mathbf{q}_j = \frac{\mathbf{v}_j}{\lVert\mathbf{v}_j\rVert} .
$$

Each $\mathbf{v}_j$ is a residual, as in the projection section, so it is perpendicular to every earlier $\mathbf{q}_i$. It cannot be $\mathbf{0}$, because then $\mathbf{a}_j$ would be a combination of the earlier columns.

The coefficients form the $R$ of the QR decomposition $A = QR$ that the QR box uses. Put $r_{ij} = \mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{a}_j$ above the diagonal of $R$, $r_{jj} = \lVert\mathbf{v}_j\rVert$ on it and zeros below it, so $R$ is upper triangular. Rearranged, the formula says $\mathbf{a}_j = r_{1j}\mathbf{q}_1 + \dots + r_{jj}\mathbf{q}_j$, which is $Q$ times column $j$ of $R$. So $A = QR$.

The code runs the recursion on a $3 \times 3$ matrix, then on the badly conditioned polynomial basis from the QR box.

```python
import numpy as np

def gram_schmidt(A, modified=False):
    """Q has perpendicular unit columns, R is upper triangular, and A = QR."""
    m, n = A.shape
    Q, R = np.zeros((m, n)), np.zeros((n, n))
    for j in range(n):
        v = A[:, j].copy()
        for i in range(j):
            R[i, j] = Q[:, i] @ (v if modified else A[:, j])   # modified: project what is left
            v -= R[i, j] * Q[:, i]                              # remove the part along q_i
        R[j, j] = np.linalg.norm(v)
        Q[:, j] = v / R[j, j]
    return Q, R

A = np.array([[1.0, 1.0, 0.0],
              [1.0, 0.0, 1.0],
              [0.0, 1.0, 1.0]])
Q, R = gram_schmidt(A)
print("Q =\n", np.round(Q, 4))
print("R =\n", np.round(R, 4))
print(f"Q^T Q = I: {np.allclose(Q.T @ Q, np.eye(3))},  QR = A: {np.allclose(Q @ R, A)},  det Q = {np.linalg.det(Q):+.4f}")
x, y = np.array([3.0, -1.0, 2.0]), np.array([0.5, 2.0, -1.0])
print(f"lengths and angles kept: {np.isclose(np.linalg.norm(Q @ x), np.linalg.norm(x))}, {np.isclose((Q @ x) @ (Q @ y), x @ y)}")

V = np.vander(np.linspace(0, 1, 30), 12, increasing=True)   # the polynomial basis from the QR box
for name, (Qv, _) in [("classical Gram-Schmidt", gram_schmidt(V)),
                      ("modified Gram-Schmidt ", gram_schmidt(V, modified=True)),
                      ("Householder (np.qr)   ", np.linalg.qr(V))]:
    err = np.linalg.norm(Qv.T @ Qv - np.eye(12))
    print(f"{name}  orthogonal to 1e-6: {str(err < 1e-6):5}  to 1e-12: {err < 1e-12}")
```

The $3 \times 3$ example checks every claim, and $\det Q = -1$ says these particular columns include a reflection. The last three lines show why `np.linalg.qr` does not run this loop. Classical and modified Gram-Schmidt are the same on paper and differ in one line. The modified version projects what is *left* of the column after the earlier steps, not the original column. So each step also removes any part along $\mathbf{q}_i$ that rounding in the earlier steps let in. The polynomial basis has condition number about $10^8$. On it the classical version's $Q^\top Q$ is off from $I$ by an amount of order 1, so its columns are not perpendicular at all. The modified version is off by an amount of order $10^{-9}$. `np.linalg.qr` uses Householder's method instead, which builds $Q$ as a product of reflections, and its columns are perpendicular to machine precision.

</Optional>

<Optional title="Why the matrix rules hold">

**Transpose of a product.** Entry $(i, j)$ of $(AB)^\top$ is entry $(j, i)$ of $AB$, which is row $j$ of $A$ dotted with column $i$ of $B$. That is row $i$ of $B^\top$ dotted with column $j$ of $A^\top$, which is entry $(i, j)$ of $B^\top A^\top$.

**Inverse of a product.** $(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}B = I$.

**Rank of a product.** Every column of $AB$ lies in the column space of $A$, so $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \operatorname{rank}(A)$. Row rank equals column rank, so $\operatorname{rank}(AB) = \operatorname{rank}(B^\top A^\top) \le \operatorname{rank}(B^\top) = \operatorname{rank}(B)$.

**A product as a sum of outer products.** Entry $(i, j)$ of $\sum_k (\text{column } k \text{ of } C)(\text{row } k \text{ of } R)$ is $\sum_k C_{ik}R_{kj}$, which is entry $(i, j)$ of $CR$.

**The fewest outer products is the rank.** A rank-$r$ matrix splits into $r$ pieces with the $CR$ construction. It cannot use fewer: in a sum of $k$ outer products $\mathbf{u}_1\mathbf{v}_1^\top + \dots + \mathbf{u}_k\mathbf{v}_k^\top$, every column is a linear combination of $\mathbf{u}_1, \dots, \mathbf{u}_k$, so the rank is at most $k$.

**The trace rule.** For $A$ of shape $m \times n$ and $B$ of shape $n \times m$, both $\operatorname{tr}(AB)$ and $\operatorname{tr}(BA)$ equal $\sum_i \sum_j A_{ij}B_{ji}$.

**The Frobenius norm.** Each diagonal entry of $A^\top A$ is a column of $A$ dotted with itself, so $\operatorname{tr}(A^\top A)$ is the sum of all squared entries of $A$. Its square root is the **Frobenius norm** $\lVert A \rVert_F$, the length of $A$ treated as one long vector.

</Optional>

<Optional title="The four fundamental subspaces">

Every $m \times n$ matrix of rank $r$ carries four subspaces:

| Subspace | Lives in | Dimension | What it holds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| column space $C(A)$ | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | $r$ | everything $A$ can output |
| null space $\mathcal{N}(A)$ | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $n - r$ | inputs $A$ sends to zero |
| row space $C(A^\top)$ | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $r$ | combinations of the rows of $A$ |
| left null space $\mathcal{N}(A^\top)$ | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | $m - r$ | vectors perpendicular to every output |

The pairs are perpendicular, $\mathcal{N}(A) \perp C(A^\top)$ and $\mathcal{N}(A^\top) \perp C(A)$. Each pair splits its space: every vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a row-space piece plus a null-space piece, and every vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$ is a column-space piece plus a left-null-space piece.

Least squares fits this picture. The residual must be perpendicular to $C(A)$, and the vectors perpendicular to $C(A)$ are the ones in $\mathcal{N}(A^\top)$. Writing "the residual lies in $\mathcal{N}(A^\top)$" in coordinates gives $A^\top(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0}$, the normal equations.

</Optional>

<Optional title="QR, and why lstsq beats the normal equations">

The **QR decomposition** factors $A = QR$ with perpendicular unit columns in $Q$ and an upper-triangular $R$. Substituting into the normal equations and using $Q^\top Q = I$ gives $R^\top R\hat{\mathbf{x}} = R^\top Q^\top \mathbf{b}$. When the columns of $A$ are independent, $R^\top$ is invertible and cancels, which leaves the small square system $R\hat{\mathbf{x}} = Q^\top \mathbf{b}$, without ever forming $A^\top A$. Because $R$ is triangular, back-substitution solves it one unknown at a time.

That matters because of the **condition number** $\kappa(A)$, which bounds how much a small relative error in the input can grow in the output. For square, invertible $A$, if $Ax = b$ is perturbed to $A(x+\delta x) = b+\delta b$, then $$\frac{\lVert\delta x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert} \le \kappa(A)\,\frac{\lVert\delta b\rVert}{\lVert b\rVert}.$$ *Proof.* $\delta x = A^{-1}\delta b$, so $\lVert\delta x\rVert \le \lVert A^{-1}\rVert\lVert\delta b\rVert$. Also $\lVert b\rVert = \lVert Ax\rVert \le \lVert A\rVert\lVert x\rVert$, so $1/\lVert x\rVert \le \lVert A\rVert/\lVert b\rVert$. Multiply. Forming $A^\top A$ squares it. Double precision carries about 16 digits, so $\kappa(A) = 10^8$ leaves about half of them and $\kappa(A^\top A) = 10^{16}$ leaves none. A condition number of $10^k$ costs roughly $k$ digits of accuracy. Fitting $e^t$ with a degree-11 polynomial is enough to see it:

```python
t = np.linspace(0, 1, 30)
A = np.vander(t, 12, increasing=True)   # degree-11 polynomial basis
b = np.exp(t)

kA = np.linalg.cond(A)
print(f"cond(A)     = {kA:.2e}")
print(f"cond(A^T A) = {np.linalg.cond(A.T @ A):.2e}     and cond(A)^2 = {kA ** 2:.2e}")

x_normal = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ b)     # form A^T A, then solve
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(A)
x_qr = np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ b)               # never forms A^T A (R is triangular)
x_lstsq, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)  # SVD underneath

for name, x in (("normal equations", x_normal), ("QR", x_qr), ("lstsq (SVD)", x_lstsq)):
    print(f"  {name:17} residual {np.linalg.norm(A @ x - b):.2e}")
```

```output
cond(A)     = 1.23e+08
cond(A^T A) = 1.43e+16     and cond(A)^2 = 1.52e+16
  normal equations  residual 1.63e-08
  QR                residual 2.02e-15
  lstsq (SVD)       residual 2.71e-15
```

In exact arithmetic all three routes give the same answer. In floating point the normal equations leave a residual millions of times larger. So use `np.linalg.lstsq`, even though `np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ b)` looks more like the formula. That rule has one exception. Ridge adds $\lambda I$ to $A^\top A$, shifting every eigenvalue up by $\lambda$, so $\kappa(A^\top A + \lambda I) = (\sigma_1^2+\lambda)/(\sigma_n^2+\lambda) \le (\sigma_1^2+\lambda)/\lambda$. The size of $\lambda$, not the smallness of $\sigma_n$, sets the conditioning, so forming $A^\top A$ is safe for ridge even when it is not for plain least squares.

</Optional>
