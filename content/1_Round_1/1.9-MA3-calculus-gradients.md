## Overview

A neural network can have millions of numbers to tune. A single number, the **loss**, measures how wrong it is. After each batch of data, training nudges every parameter in the direction that lowers the loss. How does it know which direction that is, for every number at once?

It computes the gradient, one slope per parameter. Round 1 tests this directly. The 2026 paper asked for the derivative of `tanh` and a loop-free NumPy gradient of a mean squared error. The 2025 paper asked for the gradients of a layer and the Hessian of the logistic-regression loss.

## Example: the gradient of a mean squared error

A linear model predicts $\hat{y} = \theta_1 + \theta_2 t$ from an input $t$. We want it to fit the three points $(t, y) = (0, 1)$, $(1, 3)$ and $(2, 4)$. Its loss is the mean squared error

$$
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(y_n - \boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}_n\big)^2 ,
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_n = (1, t_n)$. The 1 is there so that the dot product $\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}_n = \theta_1 \cdot 1 + \theta_2 t_n$ equals the prediction. As in the 2026 paper, $N = 3$ counts the points and $n$ indexes them.

1. Compute the gradient $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathcal{L}$ at $\boldsymbol\theta = (1, 1)$. This is the pair of slopes, one per parameter, defined below.
2. Write it in NumPy with no Python loop, no `@` and no `.T`.

Before computing anything, guess the signs. To fit the points better, should each parameter go up or down?

## Explanation

### Derivatives: the slope of a zoomed-in graph

The **derivative** $f'(x)$ is the slope of the graph of $f$ at $x$. If the input moves by a tiny amount $h$, the output moves by about $f'(x)\,h$. In symbols,

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} .
$$

The 3Blue1Brown series builds the rules below from pictures. We write $\ln$ for the natural logarithm, which NumPy calls `np.log`.

| Function | Derivative |
| --- | --- |
| $x^n$ | $n x^{n-1}$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ |
| $\ln x$ | $1/x$ |
| $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ |
| $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ |
| $f + g$ | $f' + g'$ |
| $f g$ (product rule) | $f'g + fg'$ |
| $f/g$ (quotient rule) | $(f'g - fg')/g^2$ |
| $f(g(x))$ (chain rule) | $f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$ |

The chain rule says rates of change multiply. If $g$ multiplies small changes by 3 and $f$ multiplies them by 2, then $f(g(x))$ multiplies them by 6. For example, $\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x} = e^{-x}\cdot(-1) = -e^{-x}$.

### Derivatives of activation functions

A network puts a nonlinear **activation** function between layers. Training needs its derivative.

**Sigmoid.** $\sigma(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} = (1 + e^{-x})^{-1}$. By the chain rule,

$$
\sigma'(x) = -(1 + e^{-x})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-x}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}\cdot\frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = \sigma(x)\big(1 - \sigma(x)\big),
$$

because $1 - \sigma(x) = \dfrac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}}$. At $x = 0$, $\sigma(0) = \tfrac12$, so $\sigma'(0) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14$. Writing $s = \sigma(x)$, the product $s(1 - s)$ is largest when $s = \tfrac12$, so $\tfrac14$ is the steepest slope sigmoid has.

**Tanh.** $\tanh x = \dfrac{u}{v}$ with $u = e^x - e^{-x}$ and $v = e^x + e^{-x}$. Notice $u' = v$ and $v' = u$. By the quotient rule,

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\tanh x = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} = \frac{v^2 - u^2}{v^2} = 1 - \frac{u^2}{v^2} = 1 - \tanh^2 x .
$$

Since $\tanh 0 = 0$, the slope at $x = 0$ is 1, its largest value. For large $|x|$, $\tanh x$ is close to $\pm 1$, so the derivative is close to 0.

Both sigmoid and tanh go flat far from 0. Training a deep network multiplies one such derivative per layer, so the product can shrink toward 0. This is called the **vanishing gradient** problem.

**ReLU.** $\operatorname{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$ has derivative 0 for $x < 0$ and 1 for $x > 0$. At $x = 0$ the graph has a corner and no derivative. Libraries use 0 there by convention.

### Partial derivatives and the gradient

The focus loss takes two inputs. Write it out with the three points:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \tfrac13\Big[(1 - \theta_1)^2 + (3 - \theta_1 - \theta_2)^2 + (4 - \theta_1 - 2\theta_2)^2\Big] .
$$

A **partial derivative** treats all inputs but one as constants. Freeze $\theta_2$ and differentiate in $\theta_1$. Each square gives 2 times the bracket, times the bracket's derivative in $\theta_1$, which is $-1$ for all three:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_1} = -\tfrac23\Big[(1 - \theta_1) + (3 - \theta_1 - \theta_2) + (4 - \theta_1 - 2\theta_2)\Big] .
$$

At $(1, 1)$ the three brackets are 0, 1 and 1, so $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta_1 = -\tfrac23\cdot 2 = -\tfrac43$.

Now freeze $\theta_1$ and differentiate in $\theta_2$. The brackets' derivatives in $\theta_2$ are $0$, $-1$ and $-2$, so at $(1, 1)$ $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta_2 = -\tfrac23\big[0\cdot 0 + 1\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 2\big] = -2$.

Stack all the partial derivatives into a vector and you have the **gradient**. For a function of $d$ inputs,

$$
\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_d}\right) .
$$

For the focus loss, $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathcal{L}(1, 1) = (-\tfrac43, -2)$.

**Why the gradient points uphill.** Move the input a small step $\mathbf{v}$. Each coordinate contributes its own slope times its own step, so

$$
f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{v}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{v} .
$$

Compare steps $\mathbf{u}$ of length 1, called **unit** steps. The rate $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$ is the **directional derivative** of $f$ in direction $\mathbf{u}$. From Vectors and Matrices, $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = \lVert\nabla f\rVert\cos\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\nabla f$. This is largest when $\alpha = 0$, so $\mathbf{u}$ points along $\nabla f$. It is most negative when $\mathbf{u}$ points the opposite way. So the gradient is the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is the slope in that direction. Along a **level curve**, where $f$ stays constant, the rate is 0, so the gradient is perpendicular to level curves.

<ProblemCard id="cg-gradient-from-two-probes" />

**Gradient descent** steps the other way:

$$
\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\theta - \eta\,\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta),
$$

where $\eta$, the **learning rate**, sets the step size. On the focus problem with $\eta = 0.1$, one step takes $(1, 1)$ to $(1, 1) - 0.1\,(-\tfrac43, -2) \approx (1.133, 1.2)$. The loss drops from 0.667 to 0.227. Both parameters went up, as the negative gradient said. [Convex Optimization](2.5-DL5-advanced-optimization.md) studies how well this works.

### The chain rule with many variables

Suppose $f$ depends on $u$ and $v$, and both depend on $x$. Nudging $x$ moves $f$ along two paths, through $u$ and through $v$, and the effects add:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} .
$$

Multiply the slopes along each path from $x$ to $f$, then add the paths. For example, take $f = u^2 + v$ with $u = xy$ and $v = x + y^2$. Then $\partial f/\partial x = 2u \cdot y + 1 \cdot 1 = 2xy^2 + 1$. Substituting first, $f = x^2y^2 + x + y^2$, gives the same answer. In a deep network the substituted formula gets enormous, so the path rule works one layer at a time instead.

The focus loss has this shape. With the **residuals** $r_n = y_n - \theta_1 - \theta_2 t_n$, the gaps between target and prediction, $\mathcal{L} = \tfrac13\sum_n r_n^2$. Path $n$ from $\theta_2$ contributes $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial r_n \cdot \partial r_n/\partial\theta_2 = \tfrac23 r_n\cdot(-t_n)$. Adding the paths, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta_2 = -\tfrac23(0\cdot 0 + 1\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 2) = -2$, as before.

### Jacobians

Now let the function output a vector: $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})$, with $\mathbf{x}$ of length $d$ and $\mathbf{z}$ of length $m$. In this section both are column vectors, and a weight matrix multiplies on the left. Put the $m \times d$ partial derivatives in a matrix, the **Jacobian**:

$$
J_{ij} = \frac{\partial z_i}{\partial x_j} .
$$

Row $i$ is the gradient of output $i$. Near $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{g}$ acts like the matrix $J$: $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{v}) \approx \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) + J\mathbf{v}$.

Two Jacobians appear in almost every network.

- A linear map $\mathbf{z} = W\mathbf{x}$, with $W$ of shape $m \times d$. Since $z_i = \sum_j W_{ij}x_j$, we get $\partial z_i/\partial x_j = W_{ij}$. The Jacobian is $W$ itself.
- An activation $\phi$ applied entry by entry, $z_i = \phi(x_i)$. Each $z_i$ depends only on $x_i$, so every off-diagonal entry is 0, and the diagonal holds $\phi'(x_i)$.

**Softmax** turns a list of scores $\mathbf{z}$, called the **logits**, into probabilities. Exponentiate each score, then divide by the total $S = \sum_k e^{z_k}$, so $p_i = e^{z_i}/S$. Since $\partial S/\partial z_j = e^{z_j}$, the quotient rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_i} = \frac{e^{z_i}S - e^{z_i}e^{z_i}}{S^2} = p_i(1 - p_i),
\qquad
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} = \frac{-e^{z_i}e^{z_j}}{S^2} = -p_ip_j \quad (i \ne j).
$$

As a matrix, $J = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^\top$, where $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{p})$ has $\mathbf{p}$ on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere. In code, subtract $\max_k z_k$ from every logit before exponentiating. The factor $e^{-\max}$ cancels between top and bottom, so $\mathbf{p}$ does not change, but no exponential can overflow.

The path rule, written with matrices, says: if $\mathcal{L}$ depends on $\mathbf{x}$ through $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})$, then

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L} = J^\top\,\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L} .
$$

Entry $j$ of the right side is $\sum_i \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i}\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial x_j}$, the sum over the paths through each $z_i$. Applying this once per layer, from the loss backward, is called **backpropagation**. [Backpropagation by Hand](2.1-MA4-backpropagation-mechanics.md) works through a full network.

### Gradients of a layer

A layer computes $\mathbf{z} = W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$, with $W$ of shape $m \times d$ and a bias $\mathbf{b}$ of length $m$. The 2025 paper asked for its derivatives with respect to all three inputs.

- With respect to $\mathbf{x}$, the Jacobian is $W$, as above.
- With respect to $\mathbf{b}$: $z_i = (W\mathbf{x})_i + b_i$, so $\partial z_i/\partial b_k$ is 1 when $i = k$ and 0 otherwise. The Jacobian is the identity matrix $I$.
- With respect to $W$: the weight $W_{kj}$ appears only in $z_k$, where it multiplies $x_j$. So $\partial z_i/\partial W_{kj} = x_j$ when $i = k$, and 0 otherwise. This has three indices, one number for each output and each weight.

Inside a loss, the three indices collapse to two. Sum over the paths through each $z_i$, and only $i = k$ survives:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{kj}} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_k}\,x_j,
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W} = (\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L})\,\mathbf{x}^\top .
$$

This outer product has the same shape as $W$. In the same way, $\nabla_{\mathbf{b}}\mathcal{L} = \nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}$. A gradient always has the shape of the thing you differentiate with respect to. Use that to check every answer. Shapes are a useful constraint, but two incorrect expressions can have the same shape. Check one entry or a directional derivative when in doubt.

### A table of vector gradients

In this table $\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are vectors, $A$ is a matrix, and $g$ is any function that outputs one number.

| $f(\mathbf{x})$ | $\nabla f$ |
| --- | --- |
| $\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{x}$ | $\mathbf{a}$ |
| $\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 = \mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}$ | $2\mathbf{x}$ |
| $\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}$ | $(A + A^\top)\mathbf{x}$, which is $2A\mathbf{x}$ if $A$ is symmetric |
| $\lVert A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\rVert^2$ | $2A^\top(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b})$ |
| $g(A\mathbf{x})$ | $A^\top\,\nabla g(A\mathbf{x})$ |

Each row follows from partial derivatives. For the quadratic form, $\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x} = \sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_ix_j$, and $x_k$ appears in the terms with $i = k$ and the terms with $j = k$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_ix_j = \sum_j A_{kj}x_j + \sum_i A_{ik}x_i = (A\mathbf{x})_k + (A^\top\mathbf{x})_k .
$$

Differentiating $(A + A^\top)\mathbf{x}$ once more gives a constant matrix of second derivatives, $A + A^\top$. Row 2 is row 3 with $A = I$. Row 5 is the $J^\top$ rule, since $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ has Jacobian $A$.

The fourth row is the chain rule: $\mathbf{r} = A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}$ has Jacobian $A$, and $\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^2$ has gradient $2\mathbf{r}$, so the gradient is $A^\top \cdot 2\mathbf{r}$. Setting it to zero gives the normal equations $A^\top A\mathbf{x} = A^\top\mathbf{b}$ from least squares.

### Vectorized gradients: mean squared error

For data we switch layouts. Stack the inputs $\mathbf{x}_n$ as the rows of $X$, one example per row, so $X$ is $N \times d$. Put the targets in $\mathbf{y}$. The residuals are $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol\theta$, and $\mathcal{L} = \tfrac1N\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^2$. Entry $n$ of $\mathbf{r}$ is $y_n - \sum_j X_{nj}\theta_j$, so $\partial r_n/\partial\theta_j = -X_{nj}$, and the Jacobian of $\mathbf{r}$ is $-X$. With the second row of the table and the $J^\top$ rule,

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathcal{L} = (-X)^\top\,\tfrac{2}{N}\mathbf{r} = -\frac{2}{N}X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol\theta)
= -\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} r_n\,\mathbf{x}_n .
$$

Check the shape: $X^\top$ is $d \times N$ and $\mathbf{r}$ has length $N$, so the result has length $d$, one entry per parameter.

### Solving the focus problem

Now we can answer the question from the Example. The rows of $X$ are $(1, 0)$, $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$. At $\boldsymbol\theta = (1, 1)$ the predictions are $(1, 2, 3)$ and the residuals are $\mathbf{r} = (0, 1, 1)$. Then $\sum_n r_n\mathbf{x}_n = 0\cdot(1, 0) + 1\cdot(1, 1) + 1\cdot(1, 2) = (2, 3)$, and

$$
\nabla\mathcal{L} = -\tfrac23\,(2, 3) = \left(-\tfrac43,\ -2\right) ,
$$

the same as the partial derivatives gave. Both entries are negative, so increasing either parameter lowers the loss. At the least-squares fit $\boldsymbol\theta = (\tfrac76, 1.5)$ from Vectors and Matrices, the gradient is $(0, 0)$.

For part 2, NumPy writes the sum as `-2 / N * (X.T @ r)`. Without `@` and `.T`, write each product as a broadcast multiply followed by a sum. `X * theta` multiplies column $j$ of $X$ by $\theta_j$, so summing each row (`axis=1`) gives $\boldsymbol\theta^\top \mathbf{x}_n$ for every $n$. `r[:, None]` has shape `(N, 1)`, so `r[:, None] * X` scales row $n$ by $r_n$. Summing down the rows (`axis=0`) adds them up.

### Cross-entropy gradients

**Logistic regression** predicts a probability $p_n = \sigma(\boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}_n)$ that the label $y_n$, which is 0 or 1, equals 1. It is trained with the **binary cross-entropy** loss

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac1N\sum_n\big(y_n\ln p_n + (1 - y_n)\ln(1 - p_n)\big) .
$$

Take one example and write $s = \boldsymbol\theta^\top\mathbf{x}$, so $p = \sigma(s)$. Over a common denominator, the derivative of the loss with respect to $p$ is

$$
-\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p} = \frac{-y(1 - p) + (1 - y)p}{p(1 - p)} = \frac{p - y}{p(1 - p)} .
$$

The chain rule multiplies this by $dp/ds = p(1 - p)$, which cancels the denominator, and by $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}s = \mathbf{x}$. So one example contributes $(p - y)\,\mathbf{x}$, and averaging over the examples gives

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathcal{L} = \frac1N\sum_n(p_n - y_n)\,\mathbf{x}_n = \frac1N X^\top(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}) .
$$

**Softmax with cross-entropy.** With $C$ classes, logits $\mathbf{z}$ and $\mathbf{p} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})$, the label is **one-hot**: $\mathbf{y}$ has a 1 in the true class and 0 elsewhere. The loss is $\mathcal{L} = -\sum_k y_k\ln p_k$. Write $\ln p_k = z_k - \ln S$, with $S = \sum_j e^{z_j}$. Because the $y_k$ add up to 1,

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_k y_kz_k + \Big(\sum_k y_k\Big)\ln S = -\sum_k y_kz_k + \ln S .
$$

By the chain rule, $\partial(\ln S)/\partial z_i = e^{z_i}/S = p_i$, so

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = -y_i + p_i = p_i - y_i .
$$

For the true class $y_i = 1$. If the model gives it probability $0.9$, its gradient is $0.9 - 1 = -0.1$, so a descent step raises its logit a little. If it gives $0.1$, the gradient is $-0.9$, nine times as large, so the correction is nine times as big. The correction grows with how wrong the prediction was.

All three gradients are built from prediction minus target. For MSE (with a factor 2) and logistic regression, each example contributes that difference times its input. For softmax, the layer rule turns $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}$ into $(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})\,\mathbf{x}^\top$ for $W$. If your derived gradient lacks this form, recheck the sigmoid or softmax step.

### The Hessian: curvature and the second-derivative test

The gradient is zero at the bottom of a valley, but also at the top of a hill and at a saddle. A point where $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$ is a **stationary point**. To tell the kinds apart, use second derivatives. The **Hessian** is the matrix

$$
H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j} ,
$$

also written $\nabla^2 f$. For the functions in this guide the order of differentiation does not matter, so $H$ is symmetric.

In one variable, $f(x + h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)h + \tfrac12 f''(x)h^2$. The Hessian plays the role of $f''$: $f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{v}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{v} + \tfrac12\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v}$.

At a stationary point only the curvature term is left. If $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector of $H$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, then $\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} = \lambda\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2$, so the sign of $\lambda$ is the sign of the curvature in that direction. A symmetric matrix has perpendicular eigenvectors that cover every direction ([Eigenvalues and Spectral Theory](1.8-MA2-linear-algebra-ii-eigenvalues-eigenvectors-spectral-theory.md)). So:

- All eigenvalues positive: a **local minimum**.
- All eigenvalues negative: a **local maximum**.
- Some positive and some negative: a **saddle point**.
- An eigenvalue is 0 and the others share a sign: the test cannot decide, and you have to look at higher-order terms.

**Example.** $f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$ has $\nabla f = (3x^2 - 3,\ 2y)$, which is zero at $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$. The Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix} 6x & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. At $(1, 0)$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(6, 2)$, a minimum. At $(-1, 0)$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(-6, 2)$, a saddle: uphill along $y$, downhill along $x$.

<ProblemCard id="cg-two-weight-chain-hessian" />

**Convexity.** A **symmetric** matrix is **positive semidefinite** (PSD) when all its eigenvalues are at least 0. A function whose Hessian is PSD everywhere is **convex**, and then every stationary point is a global minimum. If the Hessian has a negative eigenvalue anywhere, $f$ is not convex. If every eigenvalue is positive everywhere, $f$ is **strictly convex** and has at most one stationary point. If every eigenvalue is at most 0 everywhere, $f$ is **concave**. A Hessian with eigenvalues of both signs makes $f$ neither.

Differentiating the MSE gradient once more gives the constant Hessian $\tfrac2N X^\top X$. For the focus data this is $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 10/3 \end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalues about 0.56 and 4.77, so $(\tfrac76, \tfrac32)$ is the only minimum. In general $\mathbf{v}^\top X^\top X\mathbf{v} = \lVert X\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 \ge 0$, so every MSE loss of a linear model is convex.

**The logistic-regression Hessian.** In the gradient $\tfrac1N\sum_n(p_n - y_n)\mathbf{x}_n$, only $p_n$ depends on $\boldsymbol\theta$, and $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\,p_n = p_n(1 - p_n)\,\mathbf{x}_n$. So

$$
H = \frac1N\sum_n p_n(1 - p_n)\,\mathbf{x}_n\mathbf{x}_n^\top = \frac1N X^\top D X ,
$$

where $D$ is diagonal with entries $p_n(1 - p_n)$. The 2025 paper summed instead of averaging, so its answer has no $\tfrac1N$. Each term is PSD, since $\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{x}_n\mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{x}_n^\top\mathbf{v})^2 \ge 0$. So binary cross-entropy is convex too.

### Checking a gradient with finite differences

To check a derived gradient numerically, nudge one input by a small $h$ in both directions:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \approx \frac{f(\mathbf{x} + h\,\mathbf{e}_i) - f(\mathbf{x} - h\,\mathbf{e}_i)}{2h},
$$

where $\mathbf{e}_i$ is 1 in position $i$ and 0 elsewhere. This **central difference** has error proportional to $h^2$. The one-sided $(f(\mathbf{x} + h\mathbf{e}_i) - f(\mathbf{x}))/h$ has error proportional to $h$. Use $h$ between about $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-5}$. If $h$ is much smaller, rounding error in the computer's arithmetic swamps the difference.

Compare your formula's gradient $\mathbf{g}_1$ with the numerical one $\mathbf{g}_2$ using the **relative error** $\lVert \mathbf{g}_1 - \mathbf{g}_2\rVert / (\lVert \mathbf{g}_1\rVert + \lVert \mathbf{g}_2\rVert)$. If it is below about $10^{-7}$, your formula is almost certainly right.

## Implementation

The code below compares each activation's derivative formula with a central-difference estimate at four points.

```python
import numpy as np

def sigmoid(x):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))

x = np.array([-2.0, 0.0, 1.0, 3.0])
h = 1e-5
for name, f, df in [
    ("sigmoid", sigmoid, lambda x: sigmoid(x) * (1 - sigmoid(x))),
    ("tanh", np.tanh, lambda x: 1 - np.tanh(x) ** 2),
    ("relu", lambda x: np.maximum(x, 0), lambda x: (x > 0).astype(float)),
]:
    numeric = (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)
    print(f"{name:8} formula {np.round(df(x), 4)}   numeric {np.round(numeric, 4)}")
```

sigmoid  formula [0.105  0.25   0.1966 0.0452]   numeric [0.105  0.25   0.1966 0.0452]
tanh     formula [0.0707 1.     0.42   0.0099]   numeric [0.0707 1.     0.42   0.0099]
relu     formula [0. 0. 1. 1.]   numeric [0.  0.5 1.  1. ]

Sigmoid and tanh agree to four decimals. ReLU disagrees only at $x = 0$, where the central difference averages the slopes 0 and 1 and gets 0.5.

Next we compute the focus-problem gradient with `@` and `.T`, then with the broadcast-and-sum version the 2026 paper required.

```python
X = np.array([[1.0, 0.0],
              [1.0, 1.0],
              [1.0, 2.0]])
y = np.array([1.0, 3.0, 4.0])

def mse_grad(X, y, theta):
    r = y - X @ theta                       # residuals, shape (N,)
    return -2 / len(y) * (X.T @ r)          # shape (d,)

def mse_grad_no_matmul(X, y, theta):
    r = y - (X * theta).sum(axis=1)                      # X theta, without @
    return -2 / len(y) * (r[:, None] * X).sum(axis=0)    # X^T r, without @ or .T

theta = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
print("gradient at (1, 1):    ", np.round(mse_grad(X, y, theta), 4))
print("without @ or .T:       ", np.round(mse_grad_no_matmul(X, y, theta), 4))
# adding 0.0 turns NumPy's -0. into 0.
print("gradient at (7/6, 1.5):", np.round(mse_grad(X, y, np.array([7 / 6, 1.5])), 10) + 0.0)
```

```output
gradient at (1, 1):     [-1.3333 -2.    ]
without @ or .T:        [-1.3333 -2.    ]
gradient at (7/6, 1.5): [0. 0.]
```

Both versions match the hand answer $(-\tfrac43, -2)$, and the gradient vanishes at the least-squares fit.

This block applies the $J^\top$ rule to the softmax Jacobian and compares the result with the shortcut $\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}$.

```python
z = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.1])
y = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0])
p = np.exp(z - z.max())                    # subtract the max so exp cannot overflow
p /= p.sum()
J = np.diag(p) - np.outer(p, p)            # softmax Jacobian, dp_i / dz_j
dL_dp = -y / p                             # gradient of L = -sum y log p
print("p          ", np.round(p, 4))
print("J^T dL/dp  ", np.round(J.T @ dL_dp, 4))
print("p - y      ", np.round(p - y, 4))
```

```output
p           [0.659  0.2424 0.0986]
J^T dL/dp   [ 0.659  -0.7576  0.0986]
p - y       [ 0.659  -0.7576  0.0986]
```

The two rows match. Only the true class (index 1 in code) has a negative entry, so a descent step raises its logit and lowers the others.

Here we check the logistic-regression gradient on 50 random examples. A loop is fine in `numeric_grad` because it only runs in a test.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.standard_normal((50, 3))
y = (rng.random(50) < 0.5).astype(float)
theta = rng.standard_normal(3)

def bce(theta):
    p = sigmoid(X @ theta)
    return -np.mean(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))

def bce_grad(theta):
    return X.T @ (sigmoid(X @ theta) - y) / len(y)

def numeric_grad(f, theta, h=1e-6):
    g = np.zeros_like(theta)
    for i in range(len(theta)):
        e = np.zeros_like(theta)
        e[i] = h
        g[i] = (f(theta + e) - f(theta - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g

ga, gn = bce_grad(theta), numeric_grad(bce, theta)
rel = np.linalg.norm(ga - gn) / (np.linalg.norm(ga) + np.linalg.norm(gn))
print("analytic", np.round(ga, 6))
print("numeric ", np.round(gn, 6))
print("relative error below 1e-7:", rel < 1e-7)
```

```output
analytic [-0.254559  0.066876 -0.149907]
numeric  [-0.254559  0.066876 -0.149907]
relative error below 1e-7: True
```

The gradients agree to six decimals, within the $10^{-7}$ threshold.

Last, we compare forward and central differences for $\sin$ at $x = 1$.

```python
f, df, x0 = np.sin, np.cos, 1.0
for h in (1e-1, 1e-2, 1e-3):
    fwd = (f(x0 + h) - f(x0)) / h
    ctr = (f(x0 + h) - f(x0 - h)) / (2 * h)
    print(f"h = {h:.0e}   forward error {abs(fwd - df(x0)):.1e}")
    print(f"           central error {abs(ctr - df(x0)):.1e}")
```

```output
h = 1e-01   forward error 4.3e-02
           central error 9.0e-04
h = 1e-02   forward error 4.2e-03
           central error 9.0e-06
h = 1e-03   forward error 4.2e-04
           central error 9.0e-08
```

Each tenfold cut in $h$ cuts the forward error by about 10 and the central error by about 100.

> **Warning.** Suppose `y` has shape `(N, 1)` and `X @ theta` has shape `(N,)`. Then `y - X @ theta` broadcasts to an `(N, N)` matrix with no error, and every number after that is wrong. Keep targets one-dimensional with `y.ravel()`, and check that your gradient has the same shape as `theta`.

<Optional title="Layout conventions">

Books disagree on how to arrange derivatives. In **numerator layout**, the derivative of a scalar with respect to a column vector is a row vector, and the Jacobian of $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})$ is $m \times d$. In **denominator layout**, that derivative is a column vector, and the Jacobian is $d \times m$. Neither is wrong, but switching between them partway through a derivation transposes formulas.

PyTorch and this guide use a mixed layout: a gradient has the shape of its variable, and a Jacobian is $m \times d$. That is why the chain rule here reads $J^\top\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}$. Some texts, including d2l.ai, use the transposed $d \times m$ matrix, so their chain rule reads $A\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}$ with $A = J^\top$. When a formula from another source has its shapes transposed, a layout difference is the usual reason.

</Optional>
