## Hierarchical Clustering

### Agglomerative clustering

Start with $n$ singletons and repeatedly merge the closest pair, producing a **dendrogram** whose merge heights show at what distance each fusion happened. Cutting it at any height yields a flat clustering, and no $K$ needs to be fixed in advance.


The complementary approach, **divisive** clustering, starts from one cluster and repeatedly splits, and bisecting k-means is the common version. Divisive methods look at the global structure first and are therefore better at the top of the tree, but there are $2^{n-1}-1$ ways to split a set, so every practical implementation is a heuristic. Agglomerative is the default because each merge step is an exact minimization over a manageable number of pairs.

### Linkage criteria

The distance between clusters $A$ and $B$:

| Linkage | $d(A,B)$ | Produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single | $\min_{a,b}\lVert a-b\rVert$ | chains, follows arbitrary shapes, and is sensitive to noise bridges |
| Complete | $\max_{a,b}\lVert a-b\rVert$ | compact, similar-diameter clusters, and splits elongated ones |
| Average | $\frac{1}{|A||B|}\sum_{a,b}\lVert a-b\rVert$ | a compromise |
| Ward | increase in within-cluster SSE from merging | spherical, similar-sized, and the k-means objective, greedily |

Single, complete, and average work with any distance matrix, including non-Euclidean ones (edit distance between strings, correlation distance between gene profiles). Ward is defined through squared Euclidean distance and means nothing without it, which is why scikit-learn refuses `linkage="ward"` with any other metric.

### Ward's criterion

$$
\Delta(A,B) = \frac{|A||B|}{|A|+|B|}\,\lVert\mu_A-\mu_B\rVert^2 .
$$

The size prefactor penalizes merging two large clusters, which is why Ward produces balanced results. Two singletons merge at cost $\tfrac12\lVert a - b\rVert^2$, while two clusters of 100 points whose centroids are the same distance apart merge at cost 100 times higher.

*Where the formula comes from:* the merged cluster's SSE minus the two separate SSEs is exactly this expression, because the mean of the union is the size-weighted average of the two means and the cross terms cancel.

### Chaining

**Single-linkage chaining** is the classic failure: a thin bridge of noise points between two well-separated blobs lets them merge at a tiny distance, since only the single closest pair matters. Delete one noise point and the two blobs stay apart until much later, so the result is unstable in exactly the way you do not want.

It is also the strength: single linkage recovers non-convex shapes that Ward never will, and is the only linkage that can trace a spiral or a crescent. The same transitivity that lets noise bridge two clusters is what lets a genuine elongated cluster hold together. In fact single-linkage clustering is precisely the minimum spanning tree of the data with its longest edges cut, which is the cleanest way to remember what it does.

### Reading a dendrogram

- **Merge height** is the linkage distance at which the fusion happened, so the vertical axis is a distance, not a count.
- **A tall vertical segment** means the two groups it joins were far apart, which is the natural place to cut.
- **Horizontal position carries no information.** Branches can be flipped at any node, so "adjacent leaves" does not mean "similar points" unless you also check the merge structure.
- **Inversions** (a merge lower than the one below it) can occur with centroid or median linkage, which are therefore rarely used, and single, complete, average, and Ward are all monotone.

The **cophenetic correlation**, the correlation between original pairwise distances and the merge heights at which each pair first joins, is the standard numeric check on whether the tree faithfully represents the data.

### Cost

$O(n^3)$ naively, $O(n^2\log n)$ with a priority queue, and $O(n^2)$ memory for the distance matrix. Single linkage is the exception: it can be computed in $O(n^2)$ time via the minimum spanning tree. Past tens of thousands of points the memory alone rules the method out: at $n = 10^5$ the distance matrix is 40 GB in single precision.

The standard workaround is to run k-means with a large $K$ first (say 1000 micro-clusters), then cluster the centroids hierarchically. You lose the fine structure and keep the tree.

**Connectivity constraints** are the other practical extension: supply a neighbour graph and only permit merges between adjacent clusters. This makes the cost near-linear and is how hierarchical clustering is applied to image segmentation, where merging two non-adjacent regions is meaningless.

### From Scratch

```python
import numpy as np


def _sqdist(A, B):
    return np.maximum((A ** 2).sum(1)[:, None] - 2 * A @ B.T + (B ** 2).sum(1)[None, :], 0.0)


def ward_agglomerative(X, K):
    """Naive O(n^3). Merge cost |A||B|/(|A|+|B|) * ||mu_A - mu_B||^2."""
    clusters = [[i] for i in range(X.shape[0])]
    while len(clusters) > K:
        best, pair = np.inf, None
        for a in range(len(clusters)):
            for b in range(a + 1, len(clusters)):
                na, nb = len(clusters[a]), len(clusters[b])
                ma, mb = X[clusters[a]].mean(0), X[clusters[b]].mean(0)
                cost = na * nb / (na + nb) * ((ma - mb) ** 2).sum()
                if cost < best:
                    best, pair = cost, (a, b)
        a, b = pair
        clusters[a] = clusters[a] + clusters[b]
        clusters.pop(b)
    labels = np.empty(X.shape[0], int)
    for k, c in enumerate(clusters):
        labels[c] = k
    return labels


def linkage_matrix(X, method="single"):
    """Returns merges as (i, j, height, size), the SciPy dendrogram format."""
    n = X.shape[0]
    D = np.sqrt(_sqdist(X, X))
    np.fill_diagonal(D, np.inf)
    members = {i: [i] for i in range(n)}
    active = list(range(n))
    out, next_id = [], n
    while len(active) > 1:
        best, pair = np.inf, None
        for ii, a in enumerate(active):
            for b in active[ii + 1:]:
                block = D[np.ix_(members[a], members[b])]
                dist = block.min() if method == "single" else \
                       block.max() if method == "complete" else block.mean()
                if dist < best:
                    best, pair = dist, (a, b)
        a, b = pair
        members[next_id] = members[a] + members[b]
        out.append((a, b, best, len(members[next_id])))
        active = [c for c in active if c not in (a, b)] + [next_id]
        next_id += 1
    return np.array(out)


def cut_tree(Z, n, K):
    """Cut a linkage matrix into K flat clusters by replaying the first n-K merges."""
    parent = {i: i for i in range(n + len(Z))}

    def find(i):
        while parent[i] != i:
            parent[i] = parent[parent[i]]
            i = parent[i]
        return i

    for m, (a, b, _, _) in enumerate(Z[: n - K]):
        parent[find(int(a))] = n + m
        parent[find(int(b))] = n + m
    roots = {}
    labels = np.empty(n, int)
    for i in range(n):
        r = find(i)
        labels[i] = roots.setdefault(r, len(roots))
    return labels
```

The $O(n^3)$ loop above is written for clarity. Real implementations keep a priority queue of inter-cluster distances and update it with the Lance–Williams recurrence, which expresses the distance from a merged cluster to any third cluster in terms of the three distances that already existed, with no recomputation from raw points.

### Worked Example

Six 1-D points: $x = (1, 2, 4, 9, 10, 20)$. Build the tree under single and under complete linkage and compare.

**Distances.**

| | 1 | 2 | 4 | 9 | 10 | 20 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | – | 1 | 3 | 8 | 9 | 19 |
| **2** | | – | 2 | 7 | 8 | 18 |
| **4** | | | – | 5 | 6 | 16 |
| **9** | | | | – | 1 | 11 |
| **10** | | | | | – | 10 |

**Single linkage.**

1. Closest pair is a tie at distance 1: $\{1,2\}$ and $\{9,10\}$. Merge both (order does not matter here), at heights 1 and 1.
2. Now the candidates are $d(\{1,2\},\{4\}) = 2$, $d(\{4\},\{9,10\}) = 5$, $d(\{9,10\},\{20\}) = 10$. Merge $\{1,2\}$ with $\{4\}$ at height **2**.
3. $d(\{1,2,4\},\{9,10\}) = 5$ (from the pair $4,9$), $d(\{9,10\},\{20\}) = 10$. Merge at height **5**.
4. Finally $d(\{1,2,4,9,10\},\{20\}) = 10$ (from the pair $10,20$). Merge at height **10**.

Merge heights: $1, 1, 2, 5, 10$. The biggest jump is from 5 to 10, so cutting there gives $\{1,2,4,9,10\}$ and $\{20\}$: the outlier splits off last and alone.

**Complete linkage.**

1. Same two merges at height 1.
2. $d(\{1,2\},\{4\}) = \max(3,2) = 3$, $d(\{4\},\{9,10\}) = \max(5,6) = 6$, and $d(\{9,10\},\{20\}) = \max(11,10) = 11$. Merge $\{1,2\}$ with $\{4\}$ at height **3**.
3. $d(\{1,2,4\},\{9,10\}) = \max(8,9,7,8,5,6) = 9$, and $d(\{9,10\},\{20\}) = 11$. Merge at height **9**.
4. Last merge at $d = \max(19,18,16,11,10) = 19$.

Merge heights: $1, 1, 3, 9, 19$. The same tree shape, but the heights are stretched, and the final gap (9 to 19) is far more pronounced, which is the general pattern: complete linkage exaggerates separation, single linkage compresses it.

**Ward, for the third opinion.** Merging $\{1,2\}$ costs $\frac{1\cdot1}{2}(1)^2 = 0.5$, and merging $\{9,10\}$ likewise $0.5$. Then adding $\{4\}$ to $\{1,2\}$ (centroid 1.5) costs $\frac{2\cdot1}{3}(4-1.5)^2 = \frac{2}{3}(6.25) = 4.17$, while joining $\{9,10\}$ (centroid 9.5) to $\{20\}$ costs $\frac{2\cdot1}{3}(10.5)^2 = 73.5$. So Ward also builds $\{1,2,4\}$ next, at a merge cost of 4.17.

**Where they would disagree.** Insert bridge points at $x = 5, 6, 7$. Single linkage now merges everything from 1 to 10 in a chain of steps of size 1 or 2, producing one long cluster, while complete linkage still sees a maximum distance of 9 across that span and keeps the ends apart until late. Single linkage treats a chain of nearby points as connected, and complete linkage asks whether all points in the proposed cluster remain close. Choose the criterion that matches the kind of group you intend to find.

## Gaussian Mixture Models

### The model

Model the data as generated by

$$
p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k), \qquad \pi_k\ge0,\ \sum_k\pi_k = 1 .
$$

Latent $z_i \in \{1,\dots,K\}$ says which component generated $x_i$. The generative story is: draw $z_i \sim \text{Categorical}(\pi)$, then draw $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{z_i}, \Sigma_{z_i})$. The log-likelihood

$$
\log p(X\mid\theta) = \sum_i \log\sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)
$$

has a sum inside the log, which blocks a closed-form maximum. If you knew each $z_i$, the sum would collapse and each component's parameters would be an ordinary Gaussian MLE, which is exactly the observation EM is built on.

### EM

**E step.** Compute responsibilities, the posterior over components:

$$
\gamma_{ik} = p(z_i = k\mid x_i) = \frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j \pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)} .
$$

**M step.** With $N_k = \sum_i\gamma_{ik}$ (the effective count):

$$
\pi_k = \frac{N_k}{n}, \qquad
\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i \gamma_{ik}x_i, \qquad
\Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i \gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)(x_i-\mu_k)^\top .
$$

These are exactly the sample mean and covariance, weighted by responsibility. Each iteration costs $O(nKd^2)$ for full covariances, dominated by the density evaluations.

### Why EM works: the ELBO

For any distribution $q(z)$,

$$
\log p(x\mid\theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p(x,z\mid\theta)}{q(z)}\right]}_{\mathcal{F}(q,\theta)\ =\ \text{ELBO}} + \underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}\big(q(z)\,\Vert\,p(z\mid x,\theta)\big)}_{\ge 0} .
$$

*Derivation:* insert $q(z)/q(z)$ inside $\log p(x) = \log\sum_z p(x,z)$ and apply the definition of KL, and the two terms recombine exactly.

Since KL $\ge 0$, the ELBO is a **lower bound** on the log-likelihood, tight exactly when $q(z) = p(z\mid x,\theta)$.

- The **E step** sets $q = p(z\mid x,\theta^{(t)})$, driving KL to 0, so $\mathcal{F} = \log p(x\mid\theta^{(t)})$: the bound touches the curve.
- The **M step** maximizes $\mathcal{F}$ over $\theta$, so $\mathcal{F}(q,\theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \mathcal{F}(q,\theta^{(t)})$.

Chaining:

$$
\log p(x\mid\theta^{(t+1)}) \ \ge\ \mathcal{F}(q,\theta^{(t+1)}) \ \ge\ \mathcal{F}(q,\theta^{(t)}) \ =\ \log p(x\mid\theta^{(t)}).
$$

The likelihood never decreases. $\blacksquare$ (It can still converge to a local maximum.)

This same decomposition, with $q$ restricted to a parametric family because the true posterior is intractable, is the variational autoencoder's objective ([VAEs](/module/?id=vae-encoder-decoder-generation)).

### k-means is hard-assignment EM

Fix $\Sigma_k = \sigma^2 I$ with equal $\pi_k$ and let $\sigma^2\to0$. The responsibility

$$
\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\exp(-\lVert x_i-\mu_k\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)}{\sum_j\exp(-\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)}
$$

is a softmax over $-\lVert x_i-\mu_k\rVert^2$ with temperature $\sigma^2$. As $\sigma^2\to0$ it becomes a one-hot indicator on the nearest centroid, the k-means assignment step, and the M step collapses to the centroid mean.

Read in reverse: a GMM is k-means with two restrictions lifted. Soft responsibilities replace hard assignment, and per-component covariances replace the shared spherical one, which is what lets a cluster be a long thin ellipse at an arbitrary angle.

### The singularity

If one component's mean sits exactly on a data point and $\Sigma_k \to 0$, the density at that point $\to\infty$ and so does the likelihood. The MLE is therefore unbounded, and practical fixes are a covariance floor (`reg_covar`), tied or diagonal covariances, or restarting when it happens.

This is not a rare pathology: with $K$ large relative to $n$, EM finds these degenerate solutions routinely, and the symptom is a log-likelihood that suddenly jumps by orders of magnitude while one component's weight collapses toward $1/n$.

### Covariance types and parameter counts

Full covariances cost $K\big(d + \frac{d(d+1)}{2}\big) + K - 1$ parameters, quadratic in $d$:

| type | parameters per component | cluster shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `full` | $d + d(d+1)/2$ | any ellipse, any orientation |
| `tied` | $d$ + one shared $d(d+1)/2$ | identical ellipses |
| `diag` | $2d$ | axis-aligned ellipses |
| `spherical` | $d + 1$ | balls (k-means-like) |

Diagonal or spherical are standard restrictions in high dimensions, where a full covariance per component is both slow and badly estimated. Model selection uses BIC: $-2\log\hat L + p\log n$, minimized over both $K$ and the covariance type, one of the few places in clustering where a principled criterion is genuinely available, because a GMM is a proper probability model.

### What a mixture buys you beyond clustering

- **Density estimation.** $p(x)$ is a usable model of the data distribution, and low $p(x)$ flags anomalies.
- **Sampling.** Draw a component, draw from its Gaussian: a generative model in two lines.
- **Held-out likelihood.** A number that says whether a fit is better, without labels and without a distance heuristic.
- **Soft membership.** Downstream models can consume $\gamma_{ik}$ as features rather than a hard label.

### From Scratch

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_triangular
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans


def _log_gauss(X, mu, Sigma):
    """Log density via Cholesky: reuse the factor to compute log|Sigma|."""
    d = X.shape[1]
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)
    diff = X - mu
    sol = solve_triangular(L, diff.T, lower=True)
    maha = (sol ** 2).sum(0)
    logdet = 2.0 * np.log(np.diag(L)).sum()
    return -0.5 * (d * np.log(2 * np.pi) + logdet + maha)


def _logsumexp(A, axis):
    m = A.max(axis, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(A - m).sum(axis, keepdims=True))).squeeze(axis)


def gmm_em(X, K, iters=200, reg=1e-6, seed=0, tol=1e-7):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n, d = X.shape
    mu = KMeans(K, n_init=3, random_state=seed).fit(X).cluster_centers_
    Sigma = np.array([np.cov(X.T) + reg * np.eye(d) for _ in range(K)])
    pi = np.full(K, 1.0 / K)
    prev_ll = -np.inf
    for _ in range(iters):
        # --- E step ---
        logp = np.stack([np.log(pi[k]) + _log_gauss(X, mu[k], Sigma[k]) for k in range(K)], 1)
        ll = _logsumexp(logp, 1)                          # per-point log evidence
        gamma = np.exp(logp - ll[:, None])
        total_ll = ll.sum()
        # --- M step ---
        Nk = gamma.sum(0) + 1e-12
        pi = Nk / n
        mu = (gamma.T @ X) / Nk[:, None]
        for k in range(K):
            diff = X - mu[k]
            Sigma[k] = (gamma[:, k, None] * diff).T @ diff / Nk[k] + reg * np.eye(d)
        if total_ll - prev_ll < tol:                      # monotone by construction
            break
        prev_ll = total_ll
    return pi, mu, Sigma, gamma, total_ll


def bic(total_ll, n, d, K, covariance="full"):
    per = {"full": d + d * (d + 1) // 2, "diag": 2 * d, "spherical": d + 1}[covariance]
    n_params = K * per + K - 1
    return -2 * total_ll + n_params * np.log(n)


def sample_gmm(pi, mu, Sigma, n, rng):
    """The generative story, run forwards."""
    ks = rng.choice(len(pi), size=n, p=pi)
    return np.array([rng.multivariate_normal(mu[k], Sigma[k]) for k in ks])
```

Three details make this stable rather than merely correct: the Cholesky factorization (never invert $\Sigma$), the log-sum-exp (densities underflow to zero in more than a few dimensions), and `reg` on the covariance diagonal (the singularity).

### Using the Library

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture, BayesianGaussianMixture
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

gmm = GaussianMixture(
    n_components=4, covariance_type="full",
    reg_covar=1e-6, n_init=5, init_params="k-means++", random_state=0,
).fit(Xs)

gmm.predict(Xs)             # hard labels (argmax of the responsibilities)
gmm.predict_proba(Xs)       # soft assignments
gmm.score_samples(Xs)       # per-point log density, an anomaly score
gmm.sample(100)             # draw new data from the fitted model

# Choosing K and the covariance type together, by BIC (lower is better).
for k in range(1, 9):
    for cov in ("full", "diag", "spherical"):
        m = GaussianMixture(k, covariance_type=cov, n_init=3, random_state=0).fit(Xs)
        print(k, cov, round(m.bic(Xs)))

# A Dirichlet-process prior prunes unused components: set n_components high
# and let the fit decide how many are actually occupied.
BayesianGaussianMixture(n_components=10, weight_concentration_prior=0.01).fit(Xs)
```

Watch `reg_covar` when a fit reports an implausibly high likelihood or a component with almost no weight: that is the singularity, and raising the floor is the fix. `n_init` matters more than for k-means, since the likelihood surface has more local maxima than the k-means objective.

### Worked Example

Fit a two-component 1-D GMM to $x = (0, 1, 8, 9)$ with fixed variances $\sigma^2 = 1$ and equal weights $\pi = (0.5, 0.5)$, starting from $\mu = (2, 7)$.

**Step 1: E step, first component.** With $\sigma^2 = 1$, the responsibility of component 1 is

$$
\gamma_{i1} = \frac{\exp(-\tfrac12(x_i-2)^2)}{\exp(-\tfrac12(x_i-2)^2) + \exp(-\tfrac12(x_i-7)^2)}
= \sigma\!\big(\tfrac12[(x_i-7)^2 - (x_i-2)^2]\big),
$$

using $\sigma(\cdot)$ for the logistic function. The exponent simplifies to $\tfrac12(45 - 10x_i) = 22.5 - 5x_i$.

| $x_i$ | $22.5 - 5x_i$ | $\gamma_{i1}$ | $\gamma_{i2}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 22.5 | $\approx 1$ | $\approx 0$ |
| 1 | 17.5 | $\approx 1$ | $2.5\times10^{-8}$ |
| 8 | $-17.5$ | $2.5\times10^{-8}$ | $\approx 1$ |
| 9 | $-22.5$ | $\approx 0$ | $\approx 1$ |

The responsibilities are already almost hard, because the components are five standard deviations apart.

**Step 2: M step.** $N_1 \approx 2$, $N_2 \approx 2$, so

$$
\mu_1 = \frac{0 + 1}{2} = 0.5, \qquad \mu_2 = \frac{8+9}{2} = 8.5, \qquad \pi = (0.5, 0.5).
$$

**Step 3: iterate.** With $\mu = (0.5, 8.5)$ the exponent becomes $\tfrac12[(x-8.5)^2 - (x-0.5)^2] = 36 - 8x$, giving responsibilities even closer to 0 and 1. The M step returns the same means: **converged** after one full iteration.

**Step 4: the log-likelihood.** At the fitted parameters, $p(x_i) = 0.5\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid 0.5, 1) + 0.5\,\mathcal{N}(x_i\mid 8.5,1)$. For $x=0$ the first term dominates: $0.5 \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-0.125} = 0.176$, so $\log p = -1.737$. By symmetry all four points give the same value, and the total log-likelihood is $4(-1.737) = -6.95$. Recomputing after the first E step (means $2$ and $7$) gives $-11.4$, so the likelihood rose, as EM guarantees.

**Step 5: now move the clusters together.** Replace the data with $x = (0, 1, 3, 4)$ and start from $\mu = (1.5, 2.5)$. The exponent is $\tfrac12[(x-2.5)^2-(x-1.5)^2] = 2 - x$, giving

$$
\gamma_{\cdot 1} = \big(\sigma(2), \sigma(1), \sigma(-1), \sigma(-2)\big) = (0.881, 0.731, 0.269, 0.119).
$$

Now the responsibilities are genuinely soft. $N_1 = 2.0$, and

$$
\mu_1 = \frac{0(0.881) + 1(0.731) + 3(0.269) + 4(0.119)}{2.0} = \frac{0 + 0.731 + 0.807 + 0.476}{2.0} = 1.007 .
$$

Every point contributed to both means, weighted by belief. k-means would have assigned $\{0,1\}$ and $\{3,4\}$ outright and computed $\mu_1 = 0.5$, but the mixture's answer is pulled toward the middle because it has not ruled out that $x=3$ came from component 1.

**Step 6: the singularity, in one line.** Free the variances and set $\mu_1 = 0$ exactly with $\sigma_1^2 \to 0$. Then $\mathcal{N}(0\mid 0,\sigma_1^2) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_1^2} \to \infty$, so $\log p(x_1) \to \infty$ while the other three points are still explained by component 2. The likelihood is unbounded above, and only `reg_covar` stops the optimizer from finding this.

5. Use `score_samples` as an anomaly detector: fit on clean data, then measure the log density of injected outliers.

## Density-Based Clustering

### DBSCAN's three kinds of point

Two parameters: radius $\varepsilon$ and $\texttt{minPts}$.

- **Core point:** has at least $\texttt{minPts}$ points within $\varepsilon$ (including itself).
- **Border point:** within $\varepsilon$ of a core point but not core itself.
- **Noise:** neither.

Clusters are the connected components of core points (linked when within $\varepsilon$ of each other), with border points attached to an adjacent cluster.

The key relation is **density reachability**: $q$ is reachable from core point $p$ if there is a chain $p = p_1, \dots, p_m = q$ where each $p_{i+1}$ is within $\varepsilon$ of the core point $p_i$. Reachability is transitive through cores but stops at borders, which is precisely why a cluster can snake around a curve but cannot leak across a sparse gap.

### The algorithm

Pick an unvisited point. If it is not core, mark it noise (provisionally, as it may later be claimed as a border point). If it is core, start a new cluster and expand: add every point within $\varepsilon$, and for each of those that is itself core, add *its* neighbourhood too, until nothing new is reachable. Repeat until every point is visited.

Cost is $n$ neighbourhood queries: $O(n^2)$ by brute force, $O(n\log n)$ with a spatial index (k-d tree, ball tree) in low dimension. Memory is $O(n)$, a real advantage over hierarchical clustering, which needs the full distance matrix.

### Properties

- **Arbitrary shapes.** Density connectivity is transitive along a chain of core points, so a crescent or a spiral is one cluster.
- **No $K$.** The number emerges from the data.
- **Explicit noise.** Outliers are labelled $-1$ rather than forced into a cluster, which is often the point of running it.
- **Not deterministic for border points.** A border point reachable from two clusters is assigned by processing order. Core points and noise are deterministic, and only the borders wobble.

On the two-moons data from the [semi-supervised module](2.19-ML14-semi-supervised-learning.md), k-means with $k = 2$ slices both crescents through the middle. `DBSCAN` with `eps=0.12` recovers each crescent nearly whole, and at `eps=0.18` the two crescents chain into a single cluster.

### Choosing the parameters

**$\texttt{minPts}$** sets how many neighbours count as "crowded". Rule of thumb: $\texttt{minPts} \ge d+1$, commonly $2d$. Larger values give smoother, more noise-tolerant clusters and more points labelled noise.

**$\varepsilon$** is chosen from a **$k$-distance plot**: for $k = \texttt{minPts}-1$, compute each point's distance to its $k$-th nearest neighbour, sort descending, and plot. Points inside clusters have small $k$-distances, and noise points have large ones. The knee in the curve is the value that separates them, and that is your $\varepsilon$.

Both parameters live in the units of the (scaled) features, so re-tune whenever the preprocessing changes.

### Failure modes and the fixes

**Varying density.** A single global $\varepsilon$ cannot handle a dense cluster next to a sparse one: raise $\varepsilon$ to find the sparse cluster and the dense ones merge, but lower it and the sparse cluster becomes noise. This is the fundamental limitation.

- **OPTICS** does not produce a flat clustering itself: it orders points so that spatially adjacent points are adjacent in the ordering, recording a *reachability distance* for each, and the resulting reachability plot has one valley per cluster: deep narrow valleys for dense clusters, shallow wide ones for sparse ones. Extracting clusters from the plot at varying depth handles the varying-density case, and scikit-learn does it for you, by the xi steepness method by default or by the dbscan method at a fixed $\varepsilon$.
- **HDBSCAN** builds DBSCAN's result for *every* $\varepsilon$ simultaneously, as a hierarchy, then selects the clusters that persist over the widest range of $\varepsilon$. It removes the $\varepsilon$ parameter entirely, leaving only a minimum cluster size, and is the sensible modern default.

**High dimension.** Choosing $\varepsilon$ becomes hard for the reasons in [the curse of dimensionality](/module/?id=decision-trees-knn): distances concentrate, so the gap between "in a cluster" and "noise" narrows toward nothing. Reduce dimension first.

**Nested or touching clusters of similar density** are merged, because density connectivity has no notion of a boundary between two equally crowded regions.

### Choosing a clustering method

| data | method | why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| spherical groups, huge $n$ | k-means | $O(nKd)$ and the shape assumption holds |
| overlapping ellipses, want probabilities | GMM | soft assignments, per-component covariance |
| multi-scale structure, small $n$ | hierarchical | the dendrogram is the finding |
| arbitrary shapes, outliers present, $K$ unknown | DBSCAN / HDBSCAN | density connectivity, explicit noise |
| clusters of very different densities | HDBSCAN / OPTICS | no single $\varepsilon$ to choose |

### From Scratch

```python
import numpy as np


def _sqdist(A, B):
    return np.maximum((A ** 2).sum(1)[:, None] - 2 * A @ B.T + (B ** 2).sum(1)[None, :], 0.0)


def dbscan(X, eps, min_pts):
    """Labels: -1 = noise, else a cluster id. DFS over the core-point graph."""
    n = X.shape[0]
    D = np.sqrt(_sqdist(X, X))
    neighbours = [np.flatnonzero(D[i] <= eps) for i in range(n)]
    is_core = np.array([len(nb) >= min_pts for nb in neighbours])
    labels = np.full(n, -1)
    cid = 0
    for i in range(n):
        if labels[i] != -1 or not is_core[i]:
            continue
        queue, labels[i] = [i], cid
        while queue:
            j = queue.pop()
            for m in neighbours[j]:
                if labels[m] == -1:
                    labels[m] = cid                       # border points join here
                    if is_core[m]:
                        queue.append(m)                   # only cores expand
        cid += 1
    return labels


def k_distance_curve(X, k):
    """Sorted distance to the k-th nearest neighbour. Read eps off the knee."""
    D = np.sqrt(_sqdist(X, X))
    np.fill_diagonal(D, np.inf)
    kth = np.sort(D, axis=1)[:, k - 1]
    return np.sort(kth)[::-1]


def core_distances(X, min_pts):
    """The distance at which a point first becomes core, the basis of HDBSCAN."""
    D = np.sqrt(_sqdist(X, X))
    return np.sort(D, axis=1)[:, min_pts - 1]
```

Two details in `dbscan` are easy to get wrong. Noise must be assigned *provisionally*, since a point that is not core may later be reached as a border point: that is why the label is overwritten inside the expansion rather than fixed on first visit. And only core points are pushed onto the queue, which is the whole difference between DBSCAN and single-linkage clustering.

### Using the Library

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN, OPTICS, HDBSCAN
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)          # eps is in feature units

# Pick eps from the k-distance knee before fitting anything.
d, _ = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=5).fit(Xs).kneighbors(Xs)
kdist = np.sort(d[:, -1])[::-1]                 # plot this, the knee is your eps

db = DBSCAN(eps=0.5, min_samples=5).fit(Xs)
db.labels_                                       # -1 means noise
(db.labels_ == -1).mean()                        # noise fraction, sanity-check it
db.core_sample_indices_

# No eps at all: cluster stability across every density threshold.
HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=15).fit(Xs)

# The reachability plot, for data with clusters at several densities.
opt = OPTICS(min_samples=5, xi=0.05).fit(Xs)
opt.reachability_[opt.ordering_]                 # plot this
```

DBSCAN has no `predict`: it is transductive, so classifying a new point means either refitting or approximating (assign to the cluster of the nearest core point within $\varepsilon$). If a noise fraction of 60% comes back, $\varepsilon$ is too small or the data has no density structure at that scale: check the $k$-distance plot rather than tuning blindly.

### Worked Example

Nine 1-D points: $x = (1,\ 1.5,\ 2,\ 2.5,\ 8,\ 8.5,\ 9,\ 15,\ 30)$, with $\varepsilon = 1$ and $\texttt{minPts} = 3$.

**Step 1: count neighbours within $\varepsilon = 1$ (including self).**

| $x$ | neighbours within 1 | count | type |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1, 1.5, 2 | 3 | **core** |
| 1.5 | 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5 | 4 | **core** |
| 2 | 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5 | 4 | **core** |
| 2.5 | 1.5, 2, 2.5 | 3 | **core** |
| 8 | 8, 8.5, 9 | 3 | **core** |
| 8.5 | 8, 8.5, 9 | 3 | **core** |
| 9 | 8, 8.5, 9 | 3 | **core** |
| 15 | 15 | 1 | not core |
| 30 | 30 | 1 | not core |

**Step 2: expand from the first core point, $x = 1$.** Its neighbourhood $\{1, 1.5, 2\}$ joins cluster 0, and expanding through the core points $1.5$ and $2$ adds $2.5$ as well. All four are core, so expansion checks each of them, but $1$, $2$ and $2.5$ add nothing new (3.5 is not present). Cluster 0 is $\{1, 1.5, 2, 2.5\}$, with all four points core: every neighbourhood is already inside the cluster, so nothing spreads it further.

**Step 3: next unvisited core point, $x = 8$.** Its neighbourhood $\{8, 8.5, 9\}$ becomes cluster 1. All three are core, so each could expand the cluster, but their neighbourhoods add nothing new and expansion stops because nothing else lies within $\varepsilon$.

**Step 4: the rest.** $x = 15$ and $x = 30$ are neither core nor within $\varepsilon$ of any core point: **noise**, labelled $-1$.

Final labels: $(0,0,0,0,\ 1,1,1,\ -1,-1)$. Two clusters and two outliers, with $K$ never specified.

**Step 5: what k-means would have done.** With $K=2$ it must place all nine points, so 15 and 30 are assigned somewhere, and the pull of 30 drags the boundary rightward until the fitted centres land near $5.9$ and $30$: the first eight points in one cluster and 30 in the other, so the two real groups merge. Even $K=3$ only partly fixes it: 30 alone forms a cluster, but 15 joins the second group and pulls its centre to $10.1$. Only at $K=4$ does k-means recover the two groups and give each outlier its own cluster. Forcing every point into a cluster is exactly what DBSCAN refuses to do.


**Step 6: vary the parameters.** Raise $\varepsilon$ to 7 with $\texttt{minPts}=3$: now $8$'s neighbourhood is $\{1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 8, 8.5, 9, 15\}$, so the two clusters and $15$ merge into one, leaving only 30 as noise. Lower $\varepsilon$ to 0.6: the point 1.5 has neighbours $\{1, 1.5, 2\}$ so it stays core, but 8.5 has only $\{8, 8.5, 9\}$ at distance 0.5, still core, while $2.5$ stops being core and becomes a border point of cluster 0. Small changes to $\varepsilon$ move points between border and noise, but the *core* structure is much more stable, which is why the $k$-distance knee is a reliable guide.

**Step 7: where a single $\varepsilon$ fails.** Replace the second group with $(8, 8.05, 8.1)$, much denser, and spread the first over $(1, 3, 5, 7)$. No single $\varepsilon$ works: at $\varepsilon = 0.2$ only the dense group is found and the sparse one is all noise, while at $\varepsilon = 2.5$ the sparse group is found and everything merges. This is precisely the case HDBSCAN was built for.

The IOAI syllabus lists DBSCAN alongside hierarchical and spectral clustering. It is the one to know first, the standard answer to "my clusters are not blobs."

## Intuition

For two elongated groups, distances to a centre, distances between nearest members, and density connectivity may suggest different partitions. Sketch the data and predict how each rule will behave before running it. This helps distinguish a poor fit of the algorithm from a mismatch between its objective and your intended grouping.

EM adds another perspective: each observation contributes to several components through responsibilities, then each component is refitted using those weights. Its bound argument explains why exact E and M steps do not lower the likelihood. That improvement is local to the chosen model and does not ensure a useful clustering or a global optimum, and covariance collapse is one reason to impose constraints.
