## Overview

A linear classifier draws a straight boundary between two classes. When many boundaries separate the training data, a **support vector machine** picks the one farthest from both classes, the one with the widest **margin**. The training points closest to the boundary, the **support vectors**, are the only ones that decide it. Kernels then let the same construction draw curved boundaries.

Round 1 asks for the pieces by hand: the distance from a point to the boundary, the margin from a weight vector, which points are support vectors and what $C$ does. The syllabus also names one proof for the written round, showing that a function is a valid kernel, and the explanation has a section for it.

## Example: three points, one line

Three training points: $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 3)$ in class −1, and $(3, 2)$ in class $+1$. Find the maximum-margin line and the width of its margin, the weights $\mathbf{w}$ and bias $b$ scaled so that every support vector satisfies $y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) = 1$, and the multipliers $\alpha_i$ in $\mathbf{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i$.

The two negatives sit at $x_1 = 1$ and the positive at $x_1 = 3$, so the widest empty strip between the classes is the one between those two verticals. The explanation turns that picture into the general method.

## Explanation

### A hyperplane and the distance to it

A linear classifier has a weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ and a bias $b$. It computes the **score** $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$ and predicts its sign. The boundary is where the score is zero. That is a line in two dimensions, a plane in three, and a **hyperplane** in general.

The labels here are −1 and $+1$, not 0 and 1. Then one inequality says a point is on the correct side: $y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) > 0$, label and score with the same sign.

Two facts about the geometry are used all the time.

1. $\mathbf{w}$ is perpendicular to the boundary. Take two points $\mathbf{x}_1$ and $\mathbf{x}_2$ on it. Both have score zero, and subtracting the two equations gives $\mathbf{w}^\top(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2) = 0$.
2. The distance from a point to the boundary is its score divided by the length of $\mathbf{w}$:

$$
r = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}.
$$

The reason for the second: walk from $\mathbf{x}$ straight to the boundary and call the landing point $\mathbf{x}_0$. The walk runs along $\mathbf{w}$, so $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}_0 + r\,\mathbf{w}/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, with $r$ the distance walked. Take the score of both sides. The score of $\mathbf{x}_0$ is zero and $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w} = \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$, which leaves $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = r\,\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$.

$r$ is a **signed distance**: positive on the side $\mathbf{w}$ points to and negative on the other. Multiplied by the label, it is positive for every correctly classified point. The margin is the empty strip around the boundary, reaching the closest training point on each side.

For the example, any line that separates the classes has to pass between $x_1 = 1$, where both negatives are, and $x_1 = 3$, where the positive is. That strip is 2 wide, so no line can be farther than 1 from its nearest point. The vertical line $x_1 = 2$ is at distance exactly 1 from all three points. It is the maximum-margin line, and its margin is 2 wide.

### Fixing the scale

Multiply $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ by the same positive number and the boundary does not move: the score is still zero in the same places. So the size of $\mathbf{w}$ is ours to choose. Choose it so that the closest points have a score of exactly $+1$ or −1. Then:

- every training point satisfies $y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1$,
- the closest points are at distance $1 / \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$, by the distance formula,
- the margin is $2 / \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ wide.

A wider margin means a shorter $\mathbf{w}$. Minimizing $\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ finds the same $\mathbf{w}$ and is easier to work with, because it has no square root. The **hard-margin SVM** is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{w}, b}\quad & \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 \\
\text{subject to}\quad & y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 \ \text{ for all } i .
\end{aligned}
$$

In words: the shortest $\mathbf{w}$ that still gives every point a score of at least 1 on its own side.

For the example the line $x_1 = 2$ is $\mathbf{w} = (1, 0)$, $b = -2$. The three scores are −1, −1 and $+1$, each exactly on its side of the margin, and $2 / \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = 2$ agrees with the width found above.

### Soft margin and the hinge loss

Real classes overlap. Even when they do not, one stray point can force a needlessly narrow margin. So let points break the rule, at a price. Each point gets a **slack** $\xi_i \ge 0$, the amount by which its score falls short of 1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\xi}}\quad & \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i \\
\text{subject to}\quad & y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i,\ \ \xi_i \ge 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

$C$ is the price of slack. A large $C$ punishes every violation and gives a narrow margin that bends to the data. A small $C$ tolerates violations for a wider margin.

At the best solution no point takes more slack than it needs, so $\xi_i = \max(0,\ 1 - y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b))$. Putting that into the objective removes the constraints:

$$
\min_{\mathbf{w}, b}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b)\big).
$$

The second term is the **hinge loss**. It is zero for a point that clears its side of the margin and grows in a straight line as the point moves inside. So an SVM is a loss plus a penalty on the weights, like [logistic regression](1.27-ML8-linear-logistic-regression.md), with the hinge in place of the log loss.

### The dual, and what a support vector is

The same problem has a second form, called the **dual**. In place of the weights it has one unknown per training point, $\alpha_i \ge 0$, which measures how hard point $i$ pushes on the boundary. [Convex Optimization](2.5-DL5-advanced-optimization.md) derives duals in general: attach a multiplier to each constraint and set the derivatives of the Lagrangian to zero. Here that gives three conditions:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i, \\
\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0,
\qquad
0 \le \alpha_i \le C .
\end{gathered}
$$

The weights are a combination of the training points, the pushes of the two classes balance, and no point pushes harder than $C$. The $\alpha_i$ themselves solve

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\quad & \sum_i \alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j\,\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_j \\
\text{subject to}\quad & 0 \le \alpha_i \le C,\ \ \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The training points appear in it only as dot products $\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_j$. The kernel trick below depends on that.

A condition called **complementary slackness** says a multiplier can be nonzero only when its constraint holds with equality. So the value of $\alpha_i$ says where a point sits:

| $\alpha_i$ | Where the point sits |
|---|---|
| 0 | clear of the margin, with no effect on $\mathbf{w}$ |
| between 0 and $C$ | exactly on the edge of the margin |
| $C$ | inside the margin or on the wrong side |

The points with $\alpha_i > 0$ are the support vectors. $\mathbf{w}$ is built from them alone, so deleting any other training point changes nothing. The bias comes from any support vector on the edge of the margin, $b = y_i - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i$.

For the example all three points touch the margin, so all three are support vectors. $\mathbf{w} = (1, 0)$ and $\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$ give three equations in the three unknowns:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\alpha_1 - \alpha_2 + 3\alpha_3 &= 1, \\
-\alpha_1 - 3\alpha_2 + 2\alpha_3 &= 0, \\
-\alpha_1 - \alpha_2 + \alpha_3 &= 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The third gives $\alpha_3 = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2$. The first then gives $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = \tfrac12$, and the second gives $\alpha_1 = \alpha_2$, so $\alpha_2 = \tfrac14$. So $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12)$. The dual objective at that point is $1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$, the same value as $\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$. The two forms of the problem reach the same value whenever the primal problem is feasible, a property called **strong duality**.

### The kernel trick

A straight boundary is limiting, and the dual shows the way out. The algorithm only ever uses dot products between points, and a dot product is a measure of how alike two points are. Swap it for another measure of likeness, a **kernel** $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')$, and the same algorithm draws a curved boundary.

A kernel is a dot product in disguise: $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \phi(\mathbf{x})^\top\phi(\mathbf{x}')$, where $\phi$ maps each point to a new, longer list of features. What makes it cheap is that $k$ is computed straight from $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}'$, and $\phi$ is never built. A linear SVM on the new features is a curved boundary on the original ones.

- The **polynomial kernel** $(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}' + 1)^p$ has a $\phi$ that holds every product of up to $p$ of the original features.
- The **RBF kernel** $\exp(-\gamma\lVert\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\rVert^2)$ is near 1 for two close points and near 0 for two distant ones. Its $\phi$ is infinitely long, and the kernel still costs one exponential. $\gamma$ sets how far each training point's influence reaches.

Prediction needs no $\mathbf{w}$ either. The score of a new point is $\sum_i \alpha_i y_i\, k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}) + b$, a sum over the support vectors. Distances are in the units of the features, so standardize them before an RBF SVM, or $\gamma$ means something different along every axis. The implementation checks three claims: that $\phi$ exists, that skipping it saves a great deal, and that the algorithm needs nothing but the kernel values.

### Which functions are kernels

Not every measure of likeness is a kernel. The test uses the **Gram matrix** of a set of points, the table of all their kernel values, $K_{ij} = k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$. $k$ is a valid kernel exactly when every Gram matrix is symmetric and **positive semidefinite**, meaning $\mathbf{v}^\top K \mathbf{v} \ge 0$ for every vector $\mathbf{v}$. This is **Mercer's condition**, and it guarantees that some $\phi$ exists even when nobody can write it down. There are two ways to prove it, and a written round can ask for either:

- **Exhibit $\phi$.** For $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = (\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}')^2$ in two dimensions, expanding the square gives $x_1^2 x_1'^2 + 2x_1x_2x_1'x_2' + x_2^2x_2'^2$, which is the dot product of $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = (x_1^2, \sqrt2\,x_1x_2, x_2^2)$ with $\phi(\mathbf{x}')$. Any Gram matrix of a $\phi$ is $\Phi\Phi^\top$, and $\mathbf{v}^\top\Phi\Phi^\top\mathbf{v} = \lVert\Phi^\top\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 \ge 0$.
- **Show $\mathbf{v}^\top K \mathbf{v} \ge 0$ directly.** For $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = f(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x}')$, $\mathbf{v}^\top K\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i,j} v_i v_j f(\mathbf{x}_i) f(\mathbf{x}_j) = \big(\sum_i v_i f(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)^2 \ge 0$.

Three rules build new kernels from old ones: a sum of kernels is a kernel, a positive multiple is, and a product is. So a polynomial of a kernel with nonnegative coefficients is a kernel. The RBF kernel follows as a limit of such polynomials times an $f(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x}')$ factor.

To show that something is not a kernel, one Gram matrix with a negative eigenvalue is enough. The implementation finds one for $\tanh(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}' - 2)$.

### What C and gamma do

The two settings of an RBF SVM pull in different directions. $C$ prices margin violations, as above. $\gamma$ sets the reach of each training point: a small $\gamma$ gives every point a wide influence and a boundary close to a line, a large $\gamma$ draws an island around each point. The implementation cross-validates both on two interleaved moons. At $\gamma = 0.1$ the boundary is too smooth and needs $C = 100$ to reach 0.940. At $\gamma = 100$ and $C = 100$ the training set is fitted perfectly and the held-out score is 0.917. The best cells are $(C, \gamma) = (1, 10)$ and $(10, 1)$, both at 0.963.

### More than two classes

An SVM separates two classes. **One-vs-rest** trains $K$ of them, one class against everything else, and predicts the class whose score is highest. **One-vs-one** trains $K(K - 1)/2$, one per pair, and lets them vote. The pairwise problems are smaller, which suits a dual whose training cost grows between quadratically and cubically with the number of points. `SVC` uses one-vs-one and `LinearSVC` uses one-vs-rest. An SVM is a good fit for many small and medium-sized datasets, especially when the boundary is complicated but the sample count is modest.

## Implementation

### The example in scikit-learn

A huge $C$ makes every violation hugely expensive, so on separable data `SVC`'s solution is the hard-margin one. `dual_coef_` holds $\alpha_i y_i$ for the support vectors:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X = np.array([[1., 1.], [1., 3.], [3., 2.]])
y = np.array([-1, -1, 1])
svc = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1e6).fit(X, y)            # a huge C: no violations allowed
print("w", np.round(svc.coef_[0], 2), "b", round(float(svc.intercept_[0]), 2))
print("support vectors", svc.support_, "alpha_i * y_i", np.round(svc.dual_coef_[0], 2))
print("margin", round(2 / np.linalg.norm(svc.coef_[0]), 2))
```

```output
w [1. 0.] b -2.0
support vectors [0 1 2] alpha_i * y_i [-0.25 -0.25  0.5 ]
margin 2.0
```

Every number from the hand solution: $\mathbf{w} = (1, 0)$, $b = -2$, all three points as support vectors, $\alpha = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12)$ behind the signs, and a margin of 2.

### Solving the dual

On 80 points the dual is a quadratic program a general solver can handle, and it reproduces what `SVC` computes:

```python
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs

X, y01 = make_blobs(n_samples=80, centers=[[-1.5, -1.0], [1.5, 1.0]], cluster_std=1.1, random_state=0)
y = np.where(y01 == 1, 1.0, -1.0)
C = 1.0
G = (y[:, None] * X) @ (y[:, None] * X).T                    # G_ij = y_i y_j x_i . x_j

def negative_dual(a):
    return 0.5 * a @ G @ a - a.sum()                          # minimize the negated dual

res = minimize(negative_dual, np.zeros(len(y)), jac=lambda a: G @ a - 1.0, method="SLSQP",
               bounds=[(0.0, C)] * len(y),
               constraints=[{"type": "eq", "fun": lambda a: a @ y, "jac": lambda a: y}],
               options={"maxiter": 1000, "ftol": 1e-12})
alpha = res.x
w = (alpha * y) @ X                                          # w = sum_i alpha_i y_i x_i
free = (alpha > 1e-6) & (alpha < C - 1e-6)                   # on the margin exactly
b = np.mean(y[free] - X[free] @ w)

svc = SVC(kernel="linear", C=C).fit(X, y)
print(f"w from the dual {np.round(w, 3)}, from SVC {np.round(svc.coef_[0], 3)}")
print(f"b from the dual {b:.3f}, from SVC {svc.intercept_[0]:.3f}")
sv = alpha > 1e-6
print(f"support vectors: {sv.sum()} (SVC finds {len(svc.support_)}), "
      f"{free.sum()} on the margin, {(alpha > C - 1e-6).sum()} at alpha = C")
print(f"every other point has alpha = 0: {np.all(alpha[~sv] < 1e-6)}")

X4, y4 = make_blobs(n_samples=200, centers=4, random_state=0)
ovo = SVC(kernel="linear", decision_function_shape="ovo").fit(X4, y4)
print(f"\n4 classes, one-vs-one: {ovo.decision_function(X4[:1]).shape[1]} pairwise scores per point")
```

```output
w from the dual [0.979 0.796], from SVC [0.979 0.796]
b from the dual -0.164, from SVC -0.164
support vectors: 11 (SVC finds 11), 3 on the margin, 8 at alpha = C
every other point has alpha = 0: True

4 classes, one-vs-one: 6 pairwise scores per point
```

Of 80 points, 11 determine the classifier. The other 69 have $\alpha_i = 0$ and could be deleted without moving $\mathbf{w}$ or $b$. Four classes give $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ pairwise scores.

### Checking a kernel

The three claims from the explanation, and Mercer's condition on two candidate kernels:

```python
from itertools import combinations_with_replacement

def phi_quadratic(X):
    """The explicit feature map behind k(x, y) = (x.y + 1)^2."""
    n, d = X.shape
    cols = [np.ones(n)]                                   # the constant term
    cols += [np.sqrt(2) * X[:, j] for j in range(d)]      # linear terms
    for j, k in combinations_with_replacement(range(d), 2):

Y = rng.standard_normal((4, 3))
K_direct = (X @ Y.T + 1.0) ** 2
K_explicit = phi_quadratic(X) @ phi_quadratic(Y).T

print(f"  max |k_direct - phi(x).phi(y)| = {np.abs(K_direct - K_explicit).max():.2e}")

    print(f"  {d:>5} {1 + d + d*(d+1)//2:>11,} {d:>12,}")

Xt = rng.standard_normal((80, 4)); yt = (Xt[:,0]*Xt[:,1] > 0).astype(int)
```

### From scratch: subgradient descent on the hinge loss

The hinge objective is convex but has a kink where $1 - y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) = 0$. At a kink there is no single slope, and any value between the slopes on its two sides can stand in for the gradient. Such a stand-in is called a **subgradient**, and the code uses 0 there. A point beyond its margin plane contributes nothing. A point inside contributes $-C\,y_i\mathbf{x}_i$ to the gradient for $\mathbf{w}$ and $-C\,y_i$ to the gradient for $b$, and the regularizer adds $\mathbf{w}$:

```python
def train_svm(X, y, C=1.0, lr=0.01, epochs=500):
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    b = 0.0
    for _ in range(epochs):
        margins = y * (X @ w + b)            # y_i (w^T x_i + b)
        viol = margins < 1                   # points inside the margin
        grad_w = w - C * (y[viol] @ X[viol]) # subgradient of (1/2)||w||^2 + C * hinge
        grad_b = -C * np.sum(y[viol])
        w -= lr * grad_w
        b -= lr * grad_b
    return w, b
```

`SVC` solves the dual with a specialized solver and adds kernels, but the linear SVM is these ten lines. On the 80 blob points from the dual section, 500 epochs at a step of 0.01 land within 0.02 of `SVC`'s weights and within 0.002 of its objective:

```python
Xb, yb01 = make_blobs(n_samples=80, centers=[[-1.5, -1.0], [1.5, 1.0]], cluster_std=1.1, random_state=0)
yb = np.where(yb01 == 1, 1.0, -1.0)
w, b = train_svm(Xb, yb, C=1.0, lr=0.01, epochs=500)
svc = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1.0).fit(Xb, yb)

def objective(w, b):
    return 0.5 * w @ w + np.sum(np.maximum(0.0, 1 - yb * (Xb @ w + b)))

print(f"SVC:                 w {np.round(svc.coef_[0], 3)}, b {svc.intercept_[0]:.3f}, objective {objective(svc.coef_[0], svc.intercept_[0]):.3f}")
```

The same ten lines on the three-point example. Change the points, the labels or `C`:

<EmbeddedPyEditor packages="numpy">
import numpy as np

X = np.array([[1., 1.], [1., 3.], [3., 2.]])       # change the points
y = np.array([-1., -1., 1.])                       # and the labels
C, lr, epochs = 1.0, 0.01, 5000                    # and the price of a violation

w, b = np.zeros(2), 0.0
for _ in range(epochs):
    margins = y * (X @ w + b)
    viol = margins < 1
    w -= lr * (w - C * (y[viol] @ X[viol]))
    b -= lr * (-C * np.sum(y[viol]))
print("w =", np.round(w, 3), " b =", round(float(b), 3), " margin =", round(float(2 / np.linalg.norm(w)), 3))
print("y (w.x + b) per point:", np.round(y * (X @ w + b), 3))
</EmbeddedPyEditor>

With $C = 1$ the loop reaches $\mathbf{w} = (1.015, 0.020)$ and $b = -2.0$ at the three decimals the printout uses. The margin is $2 / \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$ and those weights give $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = 1.0152$, so $2 / 1.0152 = 1.9701$, which is the 1.97 reported. A step of 0.01 is too coarse to settle, so $\mathbf{w}$ oscillates around the hand solution $\mathbf{w} = (1, 0)$, $b = -2$, whose margin is exactly 2.
