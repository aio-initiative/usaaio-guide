## Overview

Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum of the features: a line through the points in one dimension, a plane in two. Logistic regression predicts a probability by passing that same weighted sum through an S-shaped function, the sigmoid, and reads a class off the probability. Both are **supervised** learning: each is fitted to examples with known targets. Every later neural model is built from these two. A neuron with a sigmoid is logistic regression, and the last layer of a neural classifier is the same sigmoid or softmax on a weighted sum.

Round 1 tests both the math and the code. The syllabus gives "derive an estimator in a linear regression model" as the kind of theory it expects. [2025 Round 1](https://forum.beaver-edge.ai/c/ai-olympiads/usa-north-america-ai-olympiad/8), Problem 3 spent 18 parts on one logistic pipeline: load and encode a table, standardize it, and derive the gradient and the Hessian of the loss. Then came a `LogisticRegression` class with gradient descent and Newton's method and its test accuracy, with nothing but NumPy, pandas and `StandardScaler` allowed.

## Explanation

### The model

With one feature the model is a line:

$$
\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x .
$$

$w_0$ is the **intercept**, the prediction at $x = 0$. $w_1$ is the **slope**, how much the prediction rises when $x$ goes up by 1. With $d$ features there is one weight per feature:

$$
\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x_1 + \dots + w_d x_d .
$$

Two pieces of notation keep this short. First, give every example an extra feature that is always 1. The intercept multiplies that feature, and the prediction becomes a dot product, $\hat{y} = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$. Second, stack the examples as the rows of a matrix $X$, the **design matrix**: one row per example, one column per feature, the column of ones first. Then $X\mathbf{w}$ is the list of all $n$ predictions. For the example,

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix} w_0 \\ w_1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

### The loss

A **residual** is how far a prediction misses, $y_i - \hat{y}_i$. On a plot it is the vertical gap between a point and the line. Least squares picks the weights that make the sum of the squared residuals as small as possible:

$$
L(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2 = \lVert X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \rVert^2 .
$$

The two forms say the same thing. $X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}$ is the list of misses, and $\lVert \cdot \rVert^2$ adds up the squares of its entries.

The misses are squared, and not taken in absolute value, for three reasons. The squared loss is smooth, its minimum has a formula, and it is the right loss when the noise is Gaussian, as [Loss Functions](1.28-ML9-loss-functions.md) shows.

### The normal equations

Plotted against the weights, $L$ is a bowl. It has one lowest point, and at the lowest point the slope in every direction is zero. So take the derivative of $L$ with respect to each weight and set it to zero. The line has two weights, which gives two equations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_0} &= 2\sum_i (w_0 + w_1 x_i - y_i) = 0, \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} &= 2\sum_i (w_0 + w_1 x_i - y_i)\,x_i = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by 2 and collect the sums. Both equations are linear in $w_0$ and $w_1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n\,w_0 + \Big(\sum_i x_i\Big)\,w_1 &= \sum_i y_i, \\
\Big(\sum_i x_i\Big)\,w_0 + \Big(\sum_i x_i^2\Big)\,w_1 &= \sum_i x_i y_i .
\end{aligned}
$$

For the example, $n = 4$, $\sum x_i = 6$, $\sum x_i^2 = 14$, $\sum y_i = 9$ and $\sum x_i y_i = 18$, so

$$
4w_0 + 6w_1 = 9, \qquad 6w_0 + 14w_1 = 18 .
$$

The first equation gives $w_0 = (9 - 6w_1)/4$. Putting that into the second gives $5w_1 = 4.5$, so $w_1 = 0.9$ and $w_0 = 0.9$. The line is $\hat{y} = 0.9 + 0.9x$.

In matrix form the same two equations are

$$
\begin{aligned}
& X^\top X\,\mathbf{w} = X^\top \mathbf{y}, \\[4pt]
& \text{with}\quad
X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
X^\top \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 9 \\ 18 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

These are the **normal equations**, and in this form they hold for any number of features. The derivation is the same step, done for all the weights at once. The list of derivatives is the **gradient**, $\nabla L = 2X^\top (X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$, and setting it to zero gives the normal equations. Solving them for the weights:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y} .
$$

One matrix solve fits the model. Three facts go with it:

- **When it works.** $X^\top X$ has an inverse as long as no column of $X$ can be built from the others. A duplicated feature breaks it.
- **Why it is the minimum.** A bowl-shaped function is called **convex**. Wherever a convex function is flat, it is at its lowest.
- **What it costs.** About $nd^2 + d^3$ operations. With thousands of features, gradient descent is cheaper: start anywhere and repeat $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta \cdot \tfrac{2}{n} X^\top (X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$, at $nd$ operations per step.

With one feature the answer has a shorter form: $w_1 = \operatorname{Cov}(x, y) / \operatorname{Var}(x) = 1.125 / 1.25 = 0.9$ and $w_0 = \bar{y} - w_1 \bar{x} = 2.25 - 1.35 = 0.9$.

### Residuals

The line predicts 0.9, 1.8, 2.7 and 3.6 at the four points, so the residuals $y_i - \hat{y}_i$ are 0.1, 0.2, −0.7 and 0.4. Their squares add up to 0.70, and the root mean squared error is $\sqrt{0.70 / 4} = 0.418$.

The residuals add up to zero. They always do when the model has an intercept, because that is what the first of the two equations above says. At $x = 5$ the line predicts $0.9 + 0.9 \cdot 5 = 5.4$.

### Linear in the weights

"Linear" means linear in the weights, not in $x$. A column of $X$ can hold any fixed function of the input, such as $x^2$, $\log x$ or $\sin x$. Add a column of $x^2$ and the same normal equations fit a parabola.

The price is columns. Every term of degree at most 3 in 10 variables, such as $x_1$, $x_1 x_2$ or $x_1^2 x_3$, makes $\binom{13}{3} = 286$ columns. In 100 variables it makes 176,851. The [kernel trick](1.15-ML3-kernel-methods-svms.md) avoids that explosion, and choosing the degree is what [Bias-Variance](2.16-ML11-bias-variance-cross-validation.md) is about.

One case is not covered. A parameter inside a function, like the frequency $\omega$ in $\sin(\omega x)$, is not a weight on a fixed column. Fitting it takes nonlinear least squares: `scipy.optimize.curve_fit`, started from a grid search. [2026 Round 2, Day 2](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-2-day-2-problems.pdf), Problem 5 had that shape.

### Ridge and lasso

Least squares trusts the training data completely. With many features, or with features that nearly copy each other, it fits noise. The sign is large weights of opposite sign that cancel on the training set and nowhere else. **Regularization** fixes that by charging for the size of the weights:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}}\ \tfrac12\lVert X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2 + \lambda\,\Omega(\mathbf{w}) .
$$

The first term is the fit. $\Omega(\mathbf{w})$ is the **penalty**, a measure of how large the weights are. $\lambda \ge 0$ sets the trade. At $\lambda = 0$ this is plain least squares, and the larger $\lambda$ is, the harder the weights are pushed toward zero. **Ridge** uses half the sum of squares, $\Omega = \tfrac12 \sum_j w_j^2$. **Lasso** uses the sum of absolute values, $\Omega = \sum_j \lvert w_j \rvert$.

One weight shows how they differ. Say least squares alone would pick the value $z$, and minimize $\tfrac12(w - z)^2 + \lambda\,\Omega(w)$ instead.

- Ridge, with $\Omega = \tfrac12 w^2$, gives $w = z / (1 + \lambda)$. The weight is divided by $1 + \lambda$.
- Lasso, with $\Omega = \lvert w \rvert$, gives $w = \operatorname{sign}(z)\,\max(\lvert z \rvert - \lambda,\ 0)$. The size drops by $\lambda$, and a weight smaller than $\lambda$ becomes zero. This rule is called **soft-thresholding**.

With $\lambda = 1$:

| Least squares | Ridge | Lasso |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 1.5 | 2 |
| 0.5 | 0.25 | 0 |

Ridge halves both weights. Lasso takes 1 off both, which sends the small one to zero. A feature whose weight is zero is out of the model, so lasso selects features as it fits.

With two weights the picture is the same. Each penalty keeps the weights inside a region around zero, a diamond for lasso and a disk for ridge, and the solution is where a contour of the squared error first touches the region. The diamond's corners sit on the axes, where one weight is zero.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/ridge-lasso-geometry.svg" alt="Two panels. Each shows the least-squares solution as a dot with elliptical contours of the squared error around it, and a shaded region around the origin. Left, lasso: the region is a diamond, and the contour touches it at the corner on the vertical axis, where the first weight is zero. Right, ridge: the region is a disk, and the contour touches it at a point off both axes.">
The same squared error, two regions. The contour meets the diamond at a corner, so lasso sets $w_1 = 0$. It meets the disk off the axes, so ridge shrinks both weights and zeroes neither.
</Figure>

Ridge keeps a formula, $\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$. The added $\lambda I$ makes the inverse exist for any $\lambda > 0$, even with duplicated features. In a neural network the same penalty is called weight decay. Lasso has no formula, because $\lvert w \rvert$ has a corner at zero, and it is solved step by step.

Three rules go with either penalty:

- **Standardize the features first.** The penalty charges every weight at the same rate, and a feature in grams instead of kilograms needs a weight 1,000 times smaller.
- **Leave the intercept out of the penalty.** Shrinking it pulls the predictions toward zero instead of toward the mean of $\mathbf{y}$.
- **Choose $\lambda$ by cross-validation.** The training loss always prefers $\lambda = 0$.

<ProblemCard id="llr-ridge-two-scales" />

Lasso's weakness is correlated features. It keeps one and zeroes the other, more or less arbitrarily, where ridge splits the weight between them. The **elastic net** adds both penalties to get zeros without the arbitrary choice. In scikit-learn, `Lasso` minimizes $\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \alpha\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1$ and `Ridge` minimizes $\lVert\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \alpha\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$, so the same $\alpha$ means different strengths in the two classes.

### From a number to a probability

Now the target is a label, 0 or 1, and the model should output the probability of class 1. The weighted sum $z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}$ can be any number, and a probability has to lie between 0 and 1. The **sigmoid** squashes one into the other:

$$
p = \sigma(z), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} .
$$

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sigmoid-probability.svg" alt="The sigmoid curve: probability on the vertical axis against the weighted sum z on the horizontal axis. It rises from 0 on the left through 0.5 at z = 0 to 1 on the right. The left half is marked predict class 0 and the right half predict class 1.">
A large negative $z$ gives a probability near 0, a large positive $z$ gives one near 1, and $z = 0$ gives exactly 0.5.
</Figure>

The model predicts class 1 when $p \ge 0.5$, which is when $z \ge 0$. So the boundary between the two classes is where $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} = 0$: a straight line with two features, a flat plane with more. Logistic regression is a **linear classifier**, however curved the sigmoid looks.

A weight is read through the odds. The **odds** of class 1 are $p / (1 - p)$, so a probability of 0.8 is odds of 4 to 1. Solving the sigmoid for $z$ gives

$$
\log \frac{p}{1 - p} = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} .
$$

The weighted sum is the logarithm of the odds, the **log-odds**, and that is how a weight is read. Say a spam filter puts weight $w_j = 1.1$ on the number of links in an email. One more link raises the log-odds by 1.1, so the odds get multiplied by $e^{1.1} \approx 3.0$. An email at odds of 1 to 2, a probability of 1/3, moves to about 3 to 2, a probability of 0.60. The same extra link takes an email at 0.9 only to 0.96, because the factor acts on the odds and not on the probability.

One derivative is needed below: $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\,(1 - \sigma(z))$.

<ProblemCard id="llr-hours-of-sun-odds" />

### Maximum likelihood and the log loss

The weights are chosen to make the observed labels as probable as possible. That principle is **maximum likelihood**.

Take one example. The model gives class 1 the probability $p_i$. If the label is 1, the probability of what happened is $p_i$. If the label is 0, it is $1 - p_i$. One expression covers both cases:

$$
P(y_i) = p_i^{\,y_i}\,(1 - p_i)^{\,1 - y_i} .
$$

With $y_i = 1$ the second factor is 1, and with $y_i = 0$ the first one is.

The examples are independent, so the probability of all the labels is the product of these terms. That product is the **likelihood**. A product of many numbers below 1 underflows, so take its logarithm, which turns the product into a sum. Maximizing the sum is the same as minimizing its negative:

$$
J(\mathbf{w}) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\, y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\,\Big].
$$

This is the **log loss**, also called the **binary cross-entropy**, and the weights that minimize it are the **maximum-likelihood estimator**. For each example only one of the two terms is nonzero, and it is $-\log$ of the probability the model gave to the true label.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/log-loss-curve.svg" alt="The loss, minus log p, against the probability the model gave to the true label. It falls from above 3 near probability 0 to 0 at probability 1. Three points are marked: 2.303 at probability 0.1, 0.693 at 0.5 and 0.105 at 0.9.">
The loss on one example. A confident right answer costs almost nothing. A confident wrong answer costs a lot, without limit as the probability goes to 0.
</Figure>

Squared error on $p_i$ would be a loss too. It is the worse choice: its gradient fades for a confident wrong answer, and it is not convex in $\mathbf{w}$.

<ProblemCard id="llr-two-group-mle" />

### The gradient and the Hessian

The gradient of $J$ comes out simple. For each example, take the error $p_i - y_i$, multiply it by the input $\mathbf{x}_i$, and add them up:

$$
\nabla J = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i - y_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i = X^\top\big(\sigma(X\mathbf{w}) - \mathbf{y}\big).
$$

Both forms were asked for, one part each, in 2025 Round 1. It is linear regression's gradient $X^\top(X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$ with the prediction $X\mathbf{w}$ replaced by $\sigma(X\mathbf{w})$.

The derivation is the chain rule in three links. For one example, write $z_i = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i$, $p_i = \sigma(z_i)$, and $J_i$ for its term of the loss.

1. Loss to probability: $\dfrac{\partial J_i}{\partial p_i} = -\dfrac{y_i}{p_i} + \dfrac{1 - y_i}{1 - p_i}$.
2. Probability to weighted sum: $\dfrac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_i} = p_i(1 - p_i)$, the sigmoid's derivative.
3. Weighted sum to weights: $\dfrac{\partial z_i}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{x}_i$.

The first two multiply to something short:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \Big(-\frac{y_i}{p_i} + \frac{1 - y_i}{1 - p_i}\Big)\, p_i(1 - p_i) \\
&\quad = -y_i(1 - p_i) + (1 - y_i)\,p_i \\
&\quad = p_i - y_i .
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying by the third gives $(p_i - y_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i$, and adding over the examples gives the formula.

The **Hessian** is the matrix of second derivatives. It measures curvature: how fast the gradient changes as the weights move. Differentiate the gradient once more. Only $p_i$ depends on $\mathbf{w}$, with $\partial p_i / \partial \mathbf{w} = p_i(1 - p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^2 J &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i(1 - p_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top = X^\top S X, \\[4pt]
S &= \operatorname{diag}\big(p_1(1 - p_1), \dots, p_n(1 - p_n)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

$S$ is a diagonal matrix with one positive number per example. Because those numbers are positive, the curvature is never negative, in any direction $\mathbf{v}$:

$$
\mathbf{v}^\top X^\top S X\, \mathbf{v} = \sum_i S_{ii}\,(X\mathbf{v})_i^2 \ge 0 .
$$

A matrix with this property is called **positive semidefinite**, and a function whose Hessian has it everywhere is convex. So $J$ is a bowl with no false bottoms, and with a well-chosen learning rate gradient descent reaches its lowest point from any start.

There is no formula for that point. Setting the gradient to zero gives $X^\top(\sigma(X\mathbf{w}) - \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{0}$, where $\mathbf{w}$ sits inside the sigmoid and no rearrangement gets it out. The weights are found step by step.

<ProblemCard id="llr-copied-column-hessian" />

### Two solvers

**Gradient descent** steps against the gradient: $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla J$. The step size $\eta$ is the **learning rate**. Average $J$ over the examples, and $\eta \approx 0.1$ works on standardized features whatever $n$ is. Too small an $\eta$ crawls. Too large a one overshoots, and the loss rises instead of falling, so watch the loss per step.

**Newton's method** also uses the curvature:

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,(\nabla^2 J)^{-1}\,\nabla J,
$$

with $\eta = 1$ the full step. Near its lowest point a convex loss looks like a quadratic bowl, and a Newton step jumps straight to the bottom of that bowl. It arrives in a handful of steps where gradient descent needs thousands, and each step costs a $d \times d$ solve. The 2025 problem asked for both, with $\eta$ as a parameter.

<ProblemCard id="llr-first-newton-step" />

### When the weights run away

Sometimes a straight line separates the two training classes perfectly. Then the loss has no minimum. Doubling $\mathbf{w}$ moves every $p_i$ closer to its label and lowers the loss, and so does doubling it again. The loss falls toward 0 and never arrives, so every step makes the weights larger.

There are two fixes. A penalty $\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ added to $J$ gives a finite answer. In scikit-learn, `LogisticRegression` has it on by default, with strength $C = 1/\lambda$ and $C = 1$. Or stop after a fixed number of steps, as a 200-step gradient descent does. The implementation shows the runaway happen.

### More than two classes

With $K$ classes, each class $k$ gets its own weight vector $\mathbf{w}_k$ and its own score $z_k = \mathbf{w}_k^\top \mathbf{x}$. The **softmax** turns the $K$ scores into $K$ probabilities:

$$
p_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}} .
$$

Each one is positive and they add up to 1. The loss is again minus the log of the probability given to the true class, $J = -\sum_i \log p_{i, y_i}$, the **cross-entropy**. Its gradient for class $k$ is $\sum_i (p_{ik} - [y_i = k])\,\mathbf{x}_i$, where $[y_i = k]$ is 1 when example $i$ belongs to class $k$ and 0 otherwise. That is the binary formula again, error times input, so the same gradient descent fits it.

For $K = 2$ the softmax reduces to the sigmoid. In scikit-learn, `LogisticRegression` does this by default when $y$ has more than two values, and the [MLP module](1.18-DL1-neural-networks-perceptrons-mlps-activations.md) uses the same softmax as its last layer.

## Implementation

### Least squares in NumPy

The example, by the normal equations:

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.array([0., 1., 2., 3.])
y = np.array([1., 2., 2., 4.])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(4), x])       # the design matrix: a ones column, then x
print(X.T @ X)
print(X.T @ y)
w = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)       # the normal equations
print(w)
```

```output
[[ 4.  6.]
 [ 6. 14.]]
[ 9. 18.]
[0.9 0.9]
```

`np.linalg.solve` factorizes the matrix instead of inverting it, which is faster and loses less precision than `inv` followed by a product. The residuals, the error, and the same fit from `lstsq`, which handles a rank-deficient $X$ too:

```python
pred = X @ w
resid = y - pred
print(pred, resid, f"RMSE {np.sqrt(np.mean(resid ** 2)):.3f}")
print(np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0])
```

```output
[0.9 1.8 2.7 3.6] [ 0.1  0.2 -0.7  0.4] RMSE 0.418
[0.9 0.9]
```

### A logistic regression class

The class the 2025 problem asked for: a solver argument, a `fit` that runs whole-batch iterations and records the loss, `predict` and `score`. The features arrive with a ones column, so $w_0$ is the first weight. Both solvers are the formulas above, with the gradient and the Hessian divided by $n$:

```python
def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

class LogisticRegressionScratch:
    def __init__(self, solver="gd", lr=0.1, n_iter=1000):
        self.solver, self.lr, self.n_iter = solver, lr, n_iter

    def fit(self, X, y):                                  # X: (n, d) with a ones column, y: 0 or 1
        self.w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
        p = sigmoid(X @ self.w)
        q = np.clip(p, 1e-12, 1 - 1e-12)                # keeps the logs finite
        self.losses = [-np.mean(y * np.log(q) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - q))]
        for _ in range(self.n_iter):
            p = sigmoid(X @ self.w)
            grad = X.T @ (p - y) / len(y)                   # X^T (p - y) / n
            if self.solver == "gd":
                self.w -= self.lr * grad
            else:                                           # newton
                H = X.T @ (X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]) / len(y)   # X^T S X / n
                self.w -= self.lr * np.linalg.solve(H, grad)
            q = np.clip(sigmoid(X @ self.w), 1e-12, 1 - 1e-12)  # the loss at the new weights
            self.losses.append(-np.mean(y * np.log(q) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - q)))
        return self

    def predict(self, X):
        return (sigmoid(X @ self.w) >= 0.5).astype(int)

    def score(self, X, y):
        return np.mean(self.predict(X) == y)
```

`X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]` scales row $i$ of $X$ by $S_{ii}$, so `X.T @ (...)` is $X^\top S X$ without building the $n \times n$ diagonal matrix. On two Gaussian clusters, 150 points each:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
m = 150
X0 = rng.standard_normal((m, 2)) + np.array([-1.5, -1.5])
```

### Ridge and lasso, checked

When the columns of $X$ are orthogonal the regularized problems split into the one-weight problems from the explanation, and scikit-learn reproduces both formulas. Then a problem with 50 features of which 5 matter:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

print("soft threshold :", np.round(soft, 3) + 0.0)
print("ridge (sklearn):", np.round(Ridge(alpha=lam * n, fit_intercept=False).fit(X, y).coef_, 3) + 0.0)
print("z / (1 + lam)  :", np.round(z / (1 + lam), 3) + 0.0)

# Many features, few of them real: 60 examples, 50 features, 5 true weights.
```

<Optional title="Naive Bayes, the other way round">

Logistic regression models $P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$, the probability of the class given the features. It says nothing about how the features themselves are spread out. A model like this is called **discriminative**: it learns the boundary between the classes and nothing else.

**Naive Bayes** goes the other way. It models how each class produces its features, $P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$, and how common each class is, $P(y)$. Then Bayes' theorem from [Probability and Bayes](1.10-PS1-probability-foundations.md) turns these around: $P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ is proportional to their product,

$$
P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto P(y)\,P(\mathbf{x} \mid y).
$$

A model of how the data is produced is called **generative**.

$P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$ is a distribution over the whole feature vector, and with $d$ features it is hard to estimate. Naive Bayes gets around this with the assumption that gives it its name: given the class, the features are independent.

$$
P(\mathbf{x} \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^{d} P(x_j \mid y).
$$

That turns one $d$-dimensional distribution into $d$ one-dimensional ones. Each needs only a mean and a variance for a Gaussian feature, or counts for a discrete one. Fitting is a single pass, with no optimizer, no learning rate and no iterations, which makes naive Bayes a quick first baseline.

The assumption is usually false. Two pixels side by side are not independent given the digit, and two words in a sentence are not independent given the topic. So the question is what a false assumption costs. The code below makes two Gaussian classes in 40 dimensions, once with independent features and once with every pair of features correlated at 0.6. Each accuracy is the average over 20 training sets of that size, scored on 6,000 test points.

```python
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

D, MU_SCALE = 40, 0.4

def dataset(n, seed, corr):
    """Two Gaussian classes. corr=0 makes the features independent given
    the class, which is what naive Bayes assumes."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    mu = np.random.default_rng(0).standard_normal(D) * MU_SCALE
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(np.full((D, D), corr) + np.eye(D) * (1 - corr))
    y = (r.random(n) < 0.5).astype(int)
    X = r.standard_normal((n, D)) @ L.T + np.where(y[:, None] == 1, mu, -mu)
    return X, y

for corr, label in ((0.0, "independent, so the assumption HOLDS"),
                    (0.6, "correlated, so the assumption FAILS")):
    Xte, yte = dataset(6000, 999, corr)
    print(f"\n{label}")
    print(f"  {'n':>5} {'NaiveBayes':>11} {'LogReg':>8}   ahead")
    for n in (40, 200, 3000):
        nb = np.mean([GaussianNB().fit(*dataset(n, s, corr)).score(Xte, yte)
                      for s in range(20)])
        lr = np.mean([LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000).fit(*dataset(n, s, corr)).score(Xte, yte)
                      for s in range(20)])
        print(f"  {n:>5} {nb:>11.3f} {lr:>8.3f}   {'NB' if nb > lr else 'LR'}")
```

```output

independent, so the assumption HOLDS
      n  NaiveBayes   LogReg   ahead
     40       0.921    0.938   LR
    200       0.969    0.960   NB
   3000       0.975    0.973   NB

correlated, so the assumption FAILS
      n  NaiveBayes   LogReg   ahead
     40       0.830    0.995   LR
    200       0.943    0.998   LR
   3000       0.992    0.999   LR
```

The same two models in the same dimension, and the winner flips on one property of the data. When the features really are independent given the class, the naive Bayes assumption is true. Naive Bayes then gets more out of each example and pulls ahead by $n = 200$. When the features are correlated, the assumption is wrong and more data does not fix it. At $n = 40$ naive Bayes scores $0.830$ against logistic regression's $0.995$, and at $n = 3000$ it is still behind.

Correlation also hurts the probabilities. When two features carry the same evidence, such as a feature and a copy of it, multiplying their likelihoods counts that evidence twice. Naive Bayes then becomes **overconfident**, with its predicted probabilities pushed toward 0 and 1. A model is **calibrated** when its probabilities match how often it is right: of the examples it gives 0.9, about 90% are in that class. The `predict` of naive Bayes can still be accurate while its `predict_proba` is badly calibrated, so it is a poor choice when you need the probability itself.

<ProblemCard id="llr-copied-feature-naive-bayes" />

You may read that naive Bayes beats logistic regression on small training sets, because its stronger assumption acts like a prior. That did not happen here at $n = 40$, with either kind of data. The parameter counts suggest why. `GaussianNB` fits a mean and a variance per feature per class, $2 \times 2 \times 40 = 160$ numbers, while logistic regression fits $41$. A version that shares one variance per feature between the two classes fits 120 numbers, and on the independent data it does beat logistic regression at $n = 40$. So whether naive Bayes wins on little data depends on how many numbers it has to estimate. The version for text, where every number is a count, comes after the table.

| | logistic regression | naive Bayes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| models | $P(y \mid \mathbf{x})$ | $P(y)\,P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$ |
| kind | discriminative | generative |
| fitting | iterative, no closed form | one pass, closed form |
| key assumption | the log-odds are linear in $\mathbf{x}$ | features independent given $y$ |
| handles correlated features | yes | no, and more data does not fix it |
| calibrated probabilities | usually | often overconfident |
| can generate new data | no | yes |

The last row is where the name comes from. Naive Bayes can draw new examples: pick a class from $P(y)$, then each feature from $P(x_j \mid y)$. [VAEs](3.10-RP3-vae-encoder-decoder-generation.md) and [diffusion models](3.16-GM4-generative-adding-noise.md) are generative in the same sense, with far richer models of how the data is produced.

**Counting words.** In text classification the features are word counts. For each class, naive Bayes keeps a probability for every word $v$ in the vocabulary $V$: $P(v \mid y)$ is the chance that a word drawn from a class-$y$ message is $v$. Fitting is counting. $P(y)$ is the fraction of training messages in class $y$, and

$$
P(v \mid y) = \frac{\text{count}(v, y) + \alpha}{N_y + \alpha\,|V|},
$$

where $\text{count}(v, y)$ is how often $v$ appears in the class-$y$ messages, $N_y$ is the total number of words in them, and $|V|$ is the number of different words. With $\alpha = 0$ this is the plain fraction, the maximum-likelihood estimate. For each class, a new message gets the score

$$
\log P(y) + \sum_{v} n_v \log P(v \mid y),
$$

where $n_v$ counts how often $v$ appears in the message. The message goes to the class with the higher score. Two details make this work in practice, and the code shows both.

```python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

docs = ["win money now", "win a free prize now", "free money offer",          # spam
        "meeting at noon", "project meeting notes", "lunch at noon tomorrow"]  # not spam
y = np.array([1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0])
vec = CountVectorizer()
X = vec.fit_transform(docs).toarray()               # word counts, one row per message

def fit(X, y, alpha):
    """Class priors and smoothed word probabilities, both as logs."""
    log_prior = np.log(np.array([np.mean(y == c) for c in (0, 1)]))
    counts = np.array([X[y == c].sum(axis=0) for c in (0, 1)]) + alpha
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):                # alpha = 0 takes the log of 0 on purpose
        log_word = np.log(counts / counts.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return log_prior, log_word

def class_scores(x, log_prior, log_word):
    """log P(y) + sum over the message's words of count * log P(word | y)."""
    present = x > 0
    return log_prior + (log_word[:, present] * x[present]).sum(axis=1)

x = vec.transform(["free lunch meeting"]).toarray()[0]
print("scores with no smoothing  :", class_scores(x, *fit(X, y, alpha=0.0)))

s = class_scores(x, *fit(X, y, alpha=1.0))
mine = np.exp(s - s.max()) / np.exp(s - s.max()).sum()
sk = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0).fit(X, y).predict_proba(x[None])[0]
print(f"add-one smoothing         : p(not spam, spam) = {np.round(mine, 4)}")
print(f"matches sklearn           : {np.allclose(mine, sk)}")

p = np.full(500, 0.01)                              # 500 words, each with probability 0.01
print(f"\nproduct of 500 probabilities: {np.prod(p)}, sum of their logs: {np.log(p).sum():.1f}")
```

```output
scores with no smoothing  : [-inf -inf]
add-one smoothing         : p(not spam, spam) = [0.6667 0.3333]
matches sklearn           : True

product of 500 probabilities: 0.0, sum of their logs: -2302.6
```

The first line is the **zero-count problem**. `free` never appears in the not-spam training messages, and `lunch` and `meeting` never appear in spam. Without smoothing, a word that a class never saw gives that class probability zero, whatever the rest of the message says. Here both classes get zero, so the classifier cannot decide at all. Adding $\alpha = 1$ to every count keeps every probability positive. This is **add-one smoothing**, the Laplace smoothing from Probability and Bayes: one imaginary count for each possible outcome, and here the outcomes are the $|V|$ words. The message then comes out two to one for not spam, the same as scikit-learn's `MultinomialNB`. A larger $\alpha$ pulls every word toward the same probability, $1/|V|$, so like $\lambda$ in ridge it trades fit for caution.

The last line shows why naive Bayes works with sums of logs, as the log loss does. Five hundred probabilities of $0.01$ multiply to $10^{-1000}$, far below the smallest number a 64-bit float can hold. So the product is $0.0$ for every class, and the classes can no longer be compared. The sum of the logs, $-2302.6$, is an ordinary number, and sums of logs rank the classes the same way the products would.
