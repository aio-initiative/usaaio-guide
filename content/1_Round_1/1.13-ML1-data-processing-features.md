## Overview

A model sees a matrix of numbers. Everything between the table you are given and that matrix is preprocessing: filling gaps, putting columns on one scale, turning categories into numbers. Sometimes it also means computing a column the model could not find on its own. Each step has a correct version and a version that quietly reads the test set, and the two give different scores on the grader.

Round 1 asks for this directly. [2025 Round 1](https://forum.beaver-edge.ai/c/ai-olympiads/usa-north-america-ai-olympiad/8), Problem 3 opened with pandas inspection of a passenger table, dummy columns with the first category dropped, a `StandardScaler` fitted on the training split, and a ones column, before any model was trained. [2026 Round 1](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-1-problems.pdf), Problem 9 allowed one model, k-nearest neighbors, and graded on a hidden test set, so the score was decided by the columns and their scale.

## Example: five passengers and a test row

A training table has five passengers and a test table has one. The label is whether the passenger survived.

|      | age     | fare | port | survived |
| ---- | ------- | ---- | ---- | -------- |
| 1    | 20      | 10   | S    | 0        |
| 2    | 30      | 16   | C    | 1        |
| 3    | missing | 18   | S    | 0        |
| 4    | 40      | 22   | Q    | 0        |
| 5    | 50      | 34   | S    | 1        |
| test | 45      | 36   | Q    | ?        |

Four steps turn it into numbers:

**(a)** Fill the missing age with the median of the training ages.

**(b)** Standardize age and fare with the mean and standard deviation of the training rows.

**(c)** One-hot encode port with the first category dropped.

**(d)** Write the matrix a logistic regression receives for the training rows, with a ones column first, and the row it receives for the test passenger.

Every number in that matrix is computed from the training rows. The explanation does the four steps by hand and says what each one is for.

## Explanation

### Look at the table first

Before any transform, look. `df.shape` and `df.dtypes` say what the table holds. `df.describe()` gives the range, mean and quartiles of every numeric column, `df.isna().sum()` counts the missing values per column, and `value_counts()` lists the values of a text column. For the example, age has one missing value, fare runs from 10 to 34 with mean 20, and port takes three values with S in three of the five rows. On a real table this is where the problems show: a column in the wrong unit, a value like −1 or 9999 standing in for missing, a heavy tail, duplicated rows, a label that is 90% one class. The [pandas module](1.2-PY2-pandas-data-cleaning-storing.md) covers the calls. This module is about what to do with what they show.

### Split first

The median in (a), the mean and standard deviation in (b) and the list of categories in (c) are all learned from data. They are parameters, like weights, and they are fitted on the training rows only. Validation and test rows are then transformed with the fitted values, never with their own. The [bias-variance module](2.16-ML11-bias-variance-cross-validation.md#the-split) explains why validation rows must stay unseen, and a scaler that has used the test rows' mean has seen the test rows. The grader's hidden rows are transformed with the training statistics too, because those are the only statistics the submission has.

### Scaling

Distance models and gradient descent both care about the units of a column. A squared Euclidean distance adds $(x_j - x'_j)^2$ over columns, so a column in cents outvotes a column in years by the square of the ratio. The [kNN module](1.29-ML10-decision-trees-knn.md#distance-needs-comparable-features) measured the effect on the breast cancer data: 0.951 with a scaler, 0.916 without. Gradient descent on logistic regression needs a learning rate small enough for the largest column and then crawls along the rest, and the [regression module](1.27-ML8-linear-logistic-regression.md#the-pipeline-a-contest-asks-for) measured 0.895 on raw columns against 0.965 scaled. Penalties care as well. Ridge shrinks every weight by the same rule, and the same effect needs a weight a thousand times larger on a column in kilometers than on one in meters. Trees are the exception. A split at $x > 3$ is the split $300x > 900$, and no rescaling that keeps the order of the values changes a tree.

The transforms, and when each one fits:

- **z-score**, $(x - \mu)/\sigma$, `StandardScaler`. The default. Mean 0, standard deviation 1, and the shape of the distribution is unchanged.
- **min-max**, $(x - \min)/(\max - \min)$, `MinMaxScaler`. The range becomes $[0, 1]$. For columns with real bounds and no outliers, a pixel value or an age. One outlier squeezes every other value into a sliver.
- **log**, $\log x$ or $\log(1 + x)$, for heavy tails: fares, incomes, counts, anything where most values are small and a few are huge. A z-score is a linear map, so the z-scores of such a column are still a heavy tail. The log changes the shape.
- **clipping**, which caps values beyond a threshold, such as $z = 3$, at the threshold, so a handful of extreme rows do not set the scale for the rest. `RobustScaler`, which centers on the median and divides by the interquartile range, is the other way to stop outliers from choosing the scale.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/scaling-transforms.svg" alt="Three histograms of the area error column of the breast cancer data: raw, after z-scoring, and after taking the log. The first two have the same long right tail with different axis numbers, and the second has a dashed line at 3. The third is close to symmetric.">
The area error column of the breast cancer data, 569 values. The z-score changes the axis and keeps the shape, so the tail is still a tail. The log folds it in. Clipping would cap everything past the dashed line at 3.
</Figure>

For the example, the training ages after (a) are $20, 30, 35, 40, 50$, with mean 35 and standard deviation 10, so they become $-1.5, -0.5, 0, 0.5, 1.5$, and the test age 45 becomes $(45 - 35)/10 = 1.0$. The fares have mean 20 and standard deviation 8, so $10, 16, 18, 22, 34$ become $-1.25, -0.5, -0.25, 0.25, 1.75$, and the test fare 36 becomes 2.0. That is step (b). The standard deviation here divides by $n$, as `StandardScaler` does. In pandas, `describe()` and `.std()` divide by $n - 1$ and report 8.94 for the fares. Doing (b) with that number puts every fare z-score off by a factor of 0.894.

### Encoding categories

A model cannot multiply S by a weight. **One-hot encoding** turns a column with $K$ values into $K$ indicator columns, one per value, each 1 on the rows with that value and 0 elsewhere. Coding the values as integers instead, C as 0, Q as 1, S as 2, is wrong for unordered categories. It tells a distance model that S is twice as far from C as Q is, and a linear model that the effect of S is twice the effect of Q. Integer codes are right when the order is real, small, medium, large, and `OrdinalEncoder` exists for that case.

With a ones column in the matrix, the $K$ indicators add up to the ones column. The columns are then linearly dependent, and the normal equations of the [regression module](1.27-ML8-linear-logistic-regression.md#one-hot-encoding) have no unique solution. Dropping one indicator removes the dependence: `drop_first=True` in `pd.get_dummies`, `drop="first"` in `OneHotEncoder`. The dropped category is the baseline, and its effect lives in the intercept. For the example the categories in sorted order are C, Q, S, C is dropped, and the columns are `port_Q` and `port_S`. Row 2, port C, is $(0, 0)$, row 4, port Q, is $(1, 0)$, and the three S rows are $(0, 1)$. That is step (c). The drop is only needed for an unregularized linear fit. `OneHotEncoder`'s default `drop=None` keeps all $K$, which is right for trees, nearest neighbors and penalized models, because dropping one level makes it the all-zero row and breaks the symmetry between categories.

Two things go wrong at test time. A test row with a value the training table never had has no column, and `OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")` gives such a row all zeros. `pd.get_dummies` run on the test table alone builds its columns from the test table's own values, a different set of columns. The implementation shows both.

With thousands of values, zip codes or words, one-hot encoding makes thousands of sparse columns. Grouping the rare values into one category, `min_frequency`, is the cheap fix. **Target encoding** replaces each value by the mean label of its rows, one column in place of thousands. It leaks unless the means are computed out of fold, because a row's own label is inside its category's mean, and `TargetEncoder.fit_transform` does the out-of-fold step itself. **Shrink rare levels toward the global mean.** A level with one or two rows has a mean built from one or two labels, so out of fold alone leaves it chasing its own fold's noise. Weight the level mean against the global mean by the level's row count, $enc(c) = (n_c\bar y_c + m\,\bar y)/(n_c + m)$. A larger $m$ trusts a rare level less, and scikit-learn's `TargetEncoder` ships the same shrinkage behind its `smooth` parameter. On the example table, port C and port Q each appear once, with labels 1 and 0, so raw target encoding hands each of them its own single label back. With a smoothing weight of $m = 10$ and global mean $\bar y = 2/5 = 0.4$, port Q gets $\frac{1(0) + 10(0.4)}{1 + 10} = \frac{4}{11} = 0.36$ and port C gets $\frac{1(1) + 10(0.4)}{1 + 10} = \frac{5}{11} = 0.45$, ten elevenths of the way to the global mean, which is the honest answer for a level seen once. Smoothing is about rare levels, not the leak. Only the out-of-fold computation fixes the leak. A **feature cross** multiplies indicator columns from two categoricals, so a linear model can give the pair (port C, first class) a weight of its own, which the two columns separately cannot.

### Missing values

Three options. Drop the rows, when there are few and the gaps have nothing to do with the label. Fill with a statistic, the median for a numeric column and the most frequent value for a categorical one, with `SimpleImputer`, the statistic fitted on the training rows. Or fill and add a $0/1$ column saying the value was missing, `add_indicator=True`. Missingness is often a signal: a lab test nobody ordered says something about the patient, and the fill alone erases it. For the example the present training ages are $20, 30, 40, 50$, their median is 35, and row 3 gets it. That is step (a). A missing test age would get the same 35, not a median of the test table.

Step (d) stacks the pieces: a ones column, the two z-scores and the two indicators. Row 1 is $(1, -1.5, -1.25, 0, 1)$ and the test passenger is $(1, 1, 2, 1, 0)$. The implementation prints the whole matrix.

### Making features

Feature engineering is using what you know about the problem to hand the model a column in which the pattern is simple. The usual moves:

- a ratio or difference of related columns, such as price per square foot
- the log of a heavy-tailed column
- the parts of a date: hour, weekday, month
- for a cyclic quantity such as an angle or a time of day $t$ with period $T$, encode it as $\big(\sin\tfrac{2\pi t}{T}, \cos\tfrac{2\pi t}{T}\big)$, so that hour 23 and hour 0 are adjacent, which a raw integer encoding gets badly wrong
- an aggregate over a group, such as a customer's mean order
- the product of two columns that a domain reason says should interact
- the polynomial columns of the [regression module](1.27-ML8-linear-logistic-regression.md#linear-in-the-weights), which let a linear model fit a curve

**Binning** cuts a numeric column into ranges and one-hot encodes the range. A linear model then gets one weight per range and can fit a hump or a step that no weight on the raw column can. The implementation measures it: a linear fit of shoppers against temperature scores $R^2 = 0.02$, and the same linear model on eight quantile bins of temperature scores 0.84. Too many bins leaves too few rows per bin. Too few flattens the pattern.

<ProblemCard id="fe-bins-for-a-power-curve" />

For images the same idea is **augmentation**: label-preserving transforms of the training rows, such as flips, crops and noise, applied to the training set only. The [CNN module](1.30-CV7-convolutional-neural-networks.md) covers it.

One measurement of what a column can do. Two concentric rings are the classes, and their two coordinates are padded with 40 columns of Gaussian noise.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/feature-radius.svg" alt="Left: the first two columns, two concentric rings of points. Middle: two of the noise columns, a single round blob with both classes mixed. Right: histograms of the radius for each class, two separated peaks.">

On all 42 columns, 5-nearest neighbors scores 0.515, chance. The distances say why: the 40 noise columns carry 98% of the squared distance between any two rows, so the nearest neighbors are nearest in noise. The rings differ in one quantity, the radius $r = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2}$, and on that one column the same model scores 0.997. Same rows, same model.

### Leakage

**Data leakage** is any path by which information from outside the training rows reaches the model or its preprocessing. The validation score then says one thing and the hidden test set another. It takes several forms:

- a scaler, imputer or encoder fitted on all rows before the split
- feature selection on all rows, with the labels, before cross-validation
- a target encoding computed over all rows
- the same row, or a near copy, on both sides of the split
- a time series shuffled, so the model trains on the future of what it is tested on, the subject of the [time series module](2.20-ML15-time-series.md)
- a column computed after the outcome is known, such as `recovery_amount` for a loan default

They are not equally bad, and the implementation measures two. A `StandardScaler` fitted on all 569 rows of the breast cancer data before five-fold cross-validation changes nothing at four decimals, for logistic regression or for kNN. A mean and a standard deviation from 569 rows barely move when 114 of them are held out. Selecting the 20 columns most correlated with the label out of 1,000 columns of pure noise, before cross-validation, reports 0.880 accuracy on labels that are coin flips. Among 1,000 random columns, twenty correlate with any labels by luck, and selecting on all rows bakes the luck into every fold. Done inside the folds, the same procedure reports 0.570. The leaks that read the labels are the dangerous ones.

### Pipelines

The defense is mechanical. A `Pipeline` is a list of steps. `fit` runs each step's `fit_transform` on the training rows in order and then fits the model, and `predict` runs each step's `transform` and then the model. A `ColumnTransformer` inside it sends the numeric columns through one chain and the categorical columns through another. `cross_val_score(pipeline, X, y)` refits every step on each training fold, so the fit-on-the-training-rows rule holds by construction. The fitted pipeline is the one object the submission calls on the hidden rows.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/preprocessing-flow.svg" alt="Two rows of boxes. Top: training rows, then fit and transform the imputer, scaler and encoder, then fit the model. Bottom: test rows, then transform only with the fitted objects, then predict and score. Arrows from the top boxes down to the bottom ones carry the same statistics and the same model.">
Every fitted object is fitted on the training rows and reused on the test rows. A `Pipeline` does this by construction.
</Figure>

## Implementation

### The example in pandas and scikit-learn

The three fitted objects, each fitted on the training table, and one function that applies them to any table:

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder

train = pd.DataFrame({"age": [20, 30, np.nan, 40, 50],
                      "fare": [10, 16, 18, 22, 34],
                      "port": ["S", "C", "S", "Q", "S"]})
test = pd.DataFrame({"age": [45], "fare": [36], "port": ["Q"]})

imputer = SimpleImputer(strategy="median").fit(train[["age", "fare"]])       # medians of the training rows
scaler = StandardScaler().fit(imputer.transform(train[["age", "fare"]]))     # mean and std of the training rows
encoder = OneHotEncoder(drop="first", sparse_output=False).fit(train[["port"]])
print("medians:", imputer.statistics_)
print("means:", scaler.mean_, " stds:", scaler.scale_)
print("dummy columns:", encoder.get_feature_names_out())

def prepare(df):                                   # the same fitted objects for every table
    numeric = scaler.transform(imputer.transform(df[["age", "fare"]]))
    dummies = encoder.transform(df[["port"]])
    return np.column_stack([np.ones(len(df)), numeric, dummies])

print(prepare(train))
print(prepare(test))
```

```output
medians: [35. 18.]
means: [35. 20.]  stds: [10.  8.]
dummy columns: ['port_Q' 'port_S']
[[ 1.   -1.5  -1.25  0.    1.  ]
 [ 1.   -0.5  -0.5   0.    0.  ]
 [ 1.    0.   -0.25  0.    1.  ]
 [ 1.    0.5   0.25  1.    0.  ]
 [ 1.    1.5   1.75  0.    1.  ]]
[[1. 1. 2. 1. 0.]]
```

The medians, means and standard deviations are the hand values, and the five rows are the matrix of step (d). The test row is $(1, 1, 2, 1, 0)$: a ones column, age one standard deviation above the training mean, fare two above, port Q.

The same four steps with nothing but NumPy. Change the training table or the test row:

```python
import numpy as np

age = np.array([20., 30., np.nan, 40., 50.])         # change the training table
fare = np.array([10., 16., 18., 22., 34.])
port = np.array(["S", "C", "S", "Q", "S"])
test_age, test_fare, test_port = 45.0, 36.0, "Q"     # and the test row

median = np.nanmedian(age)                            # every statistic comes from the training rows
age = np.where(np.isnan(age), median, age)
mu = np.array([age.mean(), fare.mean()])
sd = np.array([age.std(), fare.std()])                # divides by n, like StandardScaler
columns = sorted(set(port))[1:]                       # drop the first category

def row(a, f, p):
    return np.concatenate([[1.0], (np.array([a, f]) - mu) / sd, [float(p == c) for c in columns]])

print("median", median, " means", mu, " stds", sd, " dummy columns", columns)
print(np.array([row(a, f, p) for a, f, p in zip(age, fare, port)]))
print(row(test_age, test_fare, test_port))
```

### `get_dummies` on the test table

`pd.get_dummies` builds its columns from the values it is given. On the training table it gives `port_Q` and `port_S`. On the one-row test table it sees a single value, drops it as the first category, and returns no port columns at all:

```python
print(pd.get_dummies(train, columns=["port"], drop_first=True, dtype=int))
print(pd.get_dummies(test, columns=["port"], drop_first=True, dtype=int))
columns = pd.get_dummies(train, columns=["port"], drop_first=True, dtype=int).columns
print(pd.get_dummies(test, columns=["port"], dtype=int).reindex(columns=columns, fill_value=0))
```

```output

1  30.0    16       0       0
2   NaN    18       0       1
3  40.0    22       1       0
```

The third call is the fix: encode without dropping, then `reindex` to the training columns, which adds the missing ones as zeros and drops any extra. `OneHotEncoder` fitted on the training table does the same bookkeeping itself, and with `handle_unknown="ignore"` a value never seen in training becomes a row of zeros instead of an error.

### Target encoding, out of fold

```python
class SmoothedTargetEncoder:
    """enc(c) = (n_c * ybar_c + m * ybar) / (n_c + m)

    A level seen once is pulled almost entirely to the global mean, so a
    level with a row or two cannot push its own rows' encodings far.
    """

    def __init__(self, m=10.0):
        self.m = m

    def fit(self, col, y):
        self.global_ = y.mean()
        self.map_ = {}
        for v in np.unique(col):
            mask = col == v
            n_c, ybar_c = mask.sum(), y[mask].mean()
            self.map_[v] = (n_c * ybar_c + self.m * self.global_) / (n_c + self.m)
        return self

    def transform(self, col):
        return np.array([self.map_.get(v, self.global_) for v in col])


def out_of_fold_target_encode(col, y, n_folds=5, m=10.0, seed=0):
    """Each row's encoding is computed from folds that exclude it, so no row
    can ever see its own label. This is the version that does not leak."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n = col.size
    folds = rng.permutation(n) % n_folds
    out = np.empty(n)
    for k in range(n_folds):
        tr, te = folds != k, folds == k
        enc = SmoothedTargetEncoder(m).fit(col[tr], y[tr])
        out[te] = enc.transform(col[te])
    return out
```

### Binning a hump

Shoppers against temperature, with the pattern a hump around 22 degrees. A linear model on the raw column cannot fit it. The same linear model on one-hot bins of the column can, one weight per bin:

```python
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import KBinsDiscretizer, PolynomialFeatures

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
temp = rng.uniform(0, 40, 600)                                                # degrees
shoppers = 100 * np.exp(-((temp - 22) / 6) ** 2) + rng.normal(0, 8, 600)      # a hump around 22
t_train, t_test, s_train, s_test = train_test_split(temp, shoppers, test_size=200, random_state=0)
models = {
    "linear in temperature": LinearRegression(),
    "cubic polynomial": make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(3), LinearRegression()),
    "8 quantile bins": make_pipeline(KBinsDiscretizer(n_bins=8, encode="onehot-dense", strategy="quantile"), LinearRegression()),
    "16 quantile bins": make_pipeline(KBinsDiscretizer(n_bins=16, encode="onehot-dense", strategy="quantile"), LinearRegression()),
}
for name, model in models.items():
    print(f"{name:<22} test R^2 {model.fit(t_train[:, None], s_train).score(t_test[:, None], s_test):.3f}")
```

```output
linear in temperature  test R^2 0.021
cubic polynomial       test R^2 0.555
8 quantile bins        test R^2 0.840
16 quantile bins       test R^2 0.916
```

The straight line explains nothing and the cubic about half. Eight bins reach 0.840 and sixteen reach 0.916, which leaves 25 training rows in each of the 16 bins.

### A pipeline, cross-validated

A synthetic ticket table with a fifth of the ages missing, a heavy-tailed fare and a three-valued port. The numeric columns go through an imputer and a scaler, the categorical column through an encoder, and the whole pipeline is what gets cross-validated:

```python
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 1000
age = rng.normal(35, 12, n)
fare = rng.lognormal(3.0, 0.9, n)
port = rng.choice(["C", "Q", "S"], size=n, p=[0.2, 0.1, 0.7])
logit = -0.04 * (age - 35) + 1.2 * (np.log(fare) - 3.0) + np.array([{"C": 1.0, "Q": -0.5, "S": -0.3}[p] for p in port])
y = (rng.uniform(size=n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logit))).astype(int)
age[rng.uniform(size=n) < 0.2] = np.nan                                       # a fifth of the ages go missing
df = pd.DataFrame({"age": age, "fare": fare, "port": port})
print(df.head(3))
print("missing per column:", df.isna().sum().to_dict())

preprocess = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"), StandardScaler()), ["age", "fare"]),
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(drop="first", handle_unknown="ignore"), ["port"]),
])
folds = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
for name, model in [("logistic regression", LogisticRegression()), ("5-NN", KNeighborsClassifier(5))]:
    pipe = Pipeline([("prep", preprocess), ("model", model)])
    print(f"{name:<20} five-fold accuracy {cross_val_score(pipe, df, y, cv=folds).mean():.3f}")
pipe = Pipeline([("prep", preprocess), ("model", LogisticRegression())]).fit(df, y)
print("columns the model sees:", list(pipe[:-1].get_feature_names_out()))
```

```output
         age       fare port
0  36.508763  58.294732    S
1  33.414742  26.402116    S
2  42.685072  23.875407    S
missing per column: {'age': 212, 'fare': 0, 'port': 0}
logistic regression  five-fold accuracy 0.681
5-NN                 five-fold accuracy 0.643
columns the model sees: ['num__age', 'num__fare', 'cat__port_Q', 'cat__port_S']
```

`cross_val_score` fits the imputer, the scaler and the encoder five times, once per training fold, and the held-out fold only ever meets `transform`. The fitted `pipe` takes a raw DataFrame with missing ages and text ports and returns predictions, which is the object a submission needs.

### Transform the test rows with the training statistics

The other half of the rule. A model fitted on scaled rows has to be given scaled rows, scaled with the same statistics. Give it the raw ones and it predicts one class for everything:

```python
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer

X, y_bc = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y_bc, test_size=0.25, random_state=0, stratify=y_bc)
scaler = StandardScaler().fit(X_train)
model = LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000).fit(scaler.transform(X_train), y_train)
print(f"test rows scaled with the training statistics: {model.score(scaler.transform(X_test), y_test):.3f}")
print(f"test rows left raw:                            {model.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}")
print("classes predicted on the raw rows:", np.bincount(model.predict(X_test), minlength=2))
```

```output
test rows scaled with the training statistics: 0.958
test rows left raw:                            0.371
classes predicted on the raw rows: [143   0]
```

0.371 is the share of malignant rows in the test set. The raw columns are hundreds of times larger than the scaled ones the weights were fitted for, so every score lands on the same side of the threshold. A pipeline cannot make this mistake, because its `predict` scales first.

### Two leaks, measured

The scaler leak and the selection leak, side by side. First the scaler, fitted on all 569 rows before cross-validation against fitted inside each fold. Then selection of the 20 best columns from 1,000 columns of noise, on all rows against inside each fold:

```python
from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, f_classif

folds = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
for name, model in [("logistic regression", LogisticRegression(max_iter=5000)), ("5-NN", KNeighborsClassifier(5))]:
    scaler_on_all = cross_val_score(model, StandardScaler().fit_transform(X), y_bc, cv=folds).mean()
    in_pipeline = cross_val_score(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), model), X, y_bc, cv=folds).mean()
    print(f"{name:<20} scaler fit on all rows {scaler_on_all:.4f}   scaler inside the folds {in_pipeline:.4f}")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X_noise = rng.standard_normal((100, 1000))                         # 100 rows, 1,000 columns of noise
y_noise = rng.integers(0, 2, 100)                                  # labels that are coin flips
selected = SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20).fit(X_noise, y_noise)      # the selection sees every label
leaky = cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000), selected.transform(X_noise), y_noise, cv=5).mean()
honest = cross_val_score(make_pipeline(SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)),
                         X_noise, y_noise, cv=5).mean()
print(f"selection on all rows {leaky:.3f}   selection inside the folds {honest:.3f}")
```

```output
logistic regression  scaler fit on all rows 0.9789   scaler inside the folds 0.9789
5-NN                 scaler fit on all rows 0.9649   scaler inside the folds 0.9649
selection on all rows 0.880   selection inside the folds 0.570
```

The scaler leak is invisible here, and on most tables it is small. The selection leak turns coin flips into 0.880, and nothing in the number warns you. The procedure that produced it looks like careful work, so the defense is the pipeline, not care.
