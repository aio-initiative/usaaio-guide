## Overview

Your new model gets 87 of 100 test examples right. The old one is known to get 80%. Is the new model better, or did it get a lucky test set? And why is the fraction correct, 87%, the right estimate of the model's accuracy in the first place?

Statistical inference turns data into estimates and says how much to trust them. Picking the value that makes the data most likely is also where the usual training losses come from. The USAAIO syllabus lists Hoeffding's inequality under probability and statistics. The same tools tell you how big a test set must be before a score means anything.

## Example: is 87 out of 100 better than 80%?

A baseline classifier is known to have 80% accuracy. Your model gets 87 of 100 test examples right.

1. Give a range of accuracies that is plausible for your model.
2. If your model were no better than 80%, how surprising would 87 out of 100 be?

## Explanation

### Maximum likelihood: the parameters that make the data least surprising

Model each test example as a coin flip that comes up "correct" with an unknown probability $p$, the model's true accuracy. Which $p$ best explains 87 correct out of 100?

For a given $p$, the chance of one particular order of 87 rights and 13 wrongs is

$$
L(p) = p^{87}(1 - p)^{13} .
$$

Counting all possible orders would multiply every $L(p)$ by the same number $\binom{100}{87}$. That does not move the peak, so we leave it out. Now try a few values. Compared with its value at $p = 0.87$, $L(p)$ is about $5 \times 10^{-14}$ times as large at $p = 0.5$. It is $0.18$ times as large at $p = 0.8$ and $0.008$ times as large at $p = 0.95$. The probability of the data, viewed as a function of the parameter, is the **likelihood**. The **maximum likelihood estimate** (MLE) is the $p$ that makes it largest. We write it $\hat{p}$, where the hat marks an estimate computed from data.

Differentiating a product of 100 factors is messy. Its logarithm turns the product into a sum and peaks at the same $p$, because $\ln$ is increasing. This is the **log-likelihood** $\ell(p)$. Set its derivative to zero:

$$
\ell(p) = 87\ln p + 13\ln(1 - p), \qquad \ell'(p) = \frac{87}{p} - \frac{13}{1 - p} = 0 .
$$

Clearing the fractions gives $87(1 - p) = 13p$, so $87 = 100p$ and $\hat{p} = 0.87$. In general, $k$ successes in $n$ trials give $\hat{p} = k/n$. The fraction correct is the MLE of the accuracy. On this page $L$ and $\ell$ mean likelihood and log-likelihood, as in most statistics books. Elsewhere in the guide they count and index layers.

**The Gaussian MLE.** Accuracy is one number. Many models also have to estimate a spread, and there the MLE has a catch. Take data $x_1, \dots, x_n$ from a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Each point has density

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\, e^{-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} .
$$

Take logs and add over the $n$ points:

$$
\ell(\mu, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 .
$$

Setting $\partial\ell/\partial\mu = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu) = 0$ gives $\hat\mu = \bar{x}$, the sample mean. For the variance, treat $v = \sigma^2$ as the variable. Then $\partial\ell/\partial v = -\frac{n}{2v} + \frac{1}{2v^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 = 0$, and putting in $\mu = \bar{x}$ gives $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2$. For the data 3, 5, 7, 9: $\hat\mu = 6$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = (9 + 1 + 1 + 9)/4 = 5$.

### Bias, and why the sample variance divides by n − 1

An **estimator** is a recipe that turns a sample into a guess, such as "take the mean". It is **unbiased** if its average over many samples equals the true value. The sample mean is unbiased. The MLE of the variance is not: on average, $\mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$, a slight underestimate.

The reason is that $\bar{x}$ comes from the same data, and it is the point that makes the squared deviations as small as possible. Deviations from the true mean $\mu$ would be larger. To measure the gap, write $x_i - \mu = (x_i - \bar{x}) + (\bar{x} - \mu)$, square, and add over $i$. The cross term is $2(\bar{x} - \mu)\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})$, which is zero because deviations from the mean sum to zero. That leaves

$$
\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar{x} - \mu)^2 .
$$

The first sum averages $n\sigma^2$. The second term averages $n\operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}) = n \cdot \sigma^2/n = \sigma^2$, since an average of $n$ independent draws has variance $\sigma^2/n$. So the left side averages $(n - 1)\sigma^2$.

Dividing by $n - 1$ instead gives the unbiased **sample variance** $s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2$. This is Bessel's correction. For 3, 5, 7, 9 it gives $20/3 \approx 6.67$. NumPy's `var` divides by $n$ unless you pass `ddof=1`, while pandas' `Series.var` divides by $n - 1$. Note $s$ is **not** an unbiased estimator of $\sigma$, because $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is concave and Jensen gives $\mathbb{E}[s] \le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[s^2]} = \sigma$. Unbiasedness does not survive nonlinear transformation.

### MLE is where the training losses come from

Two everyday training losses are negative log-likelihoods, so minimizing them maximizes a likelihood.

- **Classification.** Say the model gives probability $q$ to class 1, and the true label is $y$ (1 or 0). The probability of the observed label is $q^y(1 - q)^{1-y}$. Its negative log, $-\big(y\ln q + (1 - y)\ln(1 - q)\big)$, is the **cross-entropy loss**.
- **Regression.** Suppose $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, where $f(x)$ is the model's prediction and $\varepsilon$ is Gaussian noise with variance $\sigma^2$. The negative log-likelihood of the data is $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - f(x_i))^2$ plus a constant. Maximizing the likelihood is the same as minimizing the **mean squared error**.

[Loss Functions](1.28-ML9-loss-functions.md) develops this further.

### MAP: priors become regularizers

[Probability and Bayes](1.10-PS1-probability-foundations.md) combined a prior with the likelihood to get a posterior. Let $\theta$ stand for whatever we are estimating, such as the accuracy. The **maximum a posteriori** (MAP) estimate is the $\theta$ at the posterior's peak. The posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior, so taking logs gives

$$
\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \big[\ln P(\text{data} \mid \theta) + \ln P(\theta)\big] ,
$$

where $\arg\max_\theta$ means "the $\theta$ that makes this largest". Training minimizes the negative of this bracket. So $-\ln P(\theta)$ is an extra penalty added to the loss, which pulls the estimate toward values the prior favors. A penalty like this is called a **regularizer**.

- **A coin with a Beta prior.** With prior Beta$(a, b)$ and $k$ successes in $n$ trials, the posterior is Beta$(a + k,\ b + n - k)$. Its density is proportional to $p^{a+k-1}(1 - p)^{b+n-k-1}$. That has the same shape as a likelihood with $a + k - 1$ successes and $b + n - k - 1$ failures, so the same derivative puts its peak at $\frac{a + k - 1}{a + b + n - 2}$. Its mean is $\frac{a + k}{a + b + n}$. With a Beta(2, 2) prior, 87 of 100 gives a MAP of $88/102 \approx 0.863$, pulled slightly from 0.87 toward $\tfrac12$. A flat prior, Beta(1, 1), gives back the MLE.

- **A Gaussian prior on weights is ridge regression.** In [Linear & Logistic Regression](1.27-ML8-linear-logistic-regression.md) you will meet ridge regression, which adds a penalty $\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ to the squared error. Take noise variance $\sigma^2$, a data matrix $X$ with one example per row, targets $\mathbf{y}$, and a prior that makes each weight $\mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$. The negative log-posterior is, up to constants, $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$. Multiplying by $2\sigma^2$ gives the ridge objective with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$.

**One weight, worked through.** Take $y = wx + \varepsilon$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$ and prior $w \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$. The log-posterior is $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - wx_i)^2 - \frac{w^2}{2\tau^2}$ plus a constant, a downward parabola in $w$. A density whose log is a parabola is Gaussian. Matching the $w^2$ term gives its variance, and setting the derivative to zero gives its mean. The **precision** (one over the variance) of the posterior is the prior's precision plus the data's:

$$
\frac{1}{\operatorname{Var}(w \mid \text{data})} = \frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{\sum_i x_i^2}{\sigma^2}, \qquad
\mathbb{E}[w \mid \text{data}] = \operatorname{Var}(w \mid \text{data}) \cdot \frac{\sum_i x_i y_i}{\sigma^2} .
$$

A Gaussian peaks at its mean, so the mean is also the MAP estimate. With $\sigma = \tau = 1$ and data $(1, 2)$, $(3, 5)$: $\sum x_i^2 = 10$ and $\sum x_i y_i = 17$. The MLE is $17/10 = 1.7$, while the posterior has precision 11, variance $1/11$ and mean $17/11 \approx 1.55$. A prediction at a new $x$ has mean $x\,\mathbb{E}[w \mid \text{data}]$ and variance $\sigma^2 + x^2\operatorname{Var}(w \mid \text{data})$: the noise plus the uncertainty about $w$.

### How close is an average to the truth?

The accuracy estimate $\hat{p}$ is an average of $n$ independent 0-or-1 outcomes. Every inference about a score starts from how close such an average lands to its mean. The law of large numbers says it gets close. Three inequalities, Markov, Chebyshev and Hoeffding, say how close with a guarantee. The central limit theorem gives a sharper answer that is only approximate.

**The law of large numbers.** As $n$ grows, the average of independent draws from the same distribution (with a finite mean) converges to the true mean. It does not say how fast.

**Markov's inequality.** For a non-negative variable $X$ and any $a > 0$, $P(X \ge a) \le \mathbb{E}[X]/a$. If a model's average loss is 0.2, at most 20% of examples can have loss 1 or more. It uses only the mean, so it is crude.

**Chebyshev's inequality.** Let each draw have mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. The average $\bar{X}$ of $n$ independent draws has the same mean and variance $\sigma^2/n$. Being at least a distance $t$ from the mean, $|\bar{X} - \mu| \ge t$, is the same event as $(\bar{X} - \mu)^2 \ge t^2$. Markov applied to the non-negative variable $(\bar{X} - \mu)^2$, whose mean is $\sigma^2/n$, gives

$$
P\big(|\bar{X} - \mu| \ge t\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n t^2} .
$$

The **tolerance** $t$ is how far from the truth we allow the average to land. The bound holds for any distribution with a finite variance.

**Hoeffding's inequality.** If each draw lies between $a$ and $b$, the bound becomes exponential in $n$:

$$
P\big(|\bar{X} - \mu| \ge t\big) \le 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2nt^2}{(b - a)^2}\right) .
$$

**Sizing a test set.** How many examples guarantee that the measured accuracy is within $t = 0.05$ of the truth with probability at least 95%? We need the failure probability on the left to be at most 0.05.

- Chebyshev needs the variance. A 0/1 outcome has variance $p(1 - p)$, which is largest, $\tfrac14$, at $p = \tfrac12$. So we need $\frac{1/4}{n \cdot 0.0025} = \frac{100}{n} \le 0.05$, which gives $n \ge 2000$.
- Hoeffding with $[a, b] = [0, 1]$ needs $2e^{-2n(0.0025)} \le 0.05$, so $e^{-0.005n} \le 0.025$. Taking logs, $0.005n \ge \ln 40 \approx 3.69$, so $n \ge 738$.

Both are guarantees for every distribution on $[0, 1]$. Hoeffding is far tighter because it uses the whole bounded range, not only the variance. Its bound falls exponentially in $n$: doubling $n$ squares the factor $e^{-2nt^2}$. Both need independent test examples. Ten augmented crops of one photo count as closer to one example than to ten.

**The central limit theorem.** For large $n$, the average of independent draws from the same distribution is close to normal, $\bar{X} \approx \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$. This holds whatever the shape of the original distribution, as long as its variance is finite. It is an approximation, not a guarantee, but it is much sharper than the bounds. The typical error of $\hat{p}$ is its **standard error**, the standard deviation of $\hat{p}$:

$$
\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} .
$$

We plug in $\hat{p}$ because we do not know $p$. For the focus problem, $\sqrt{0.87 \cdot 0.13/100} \approx 0.0336$.

When the draws are themselves Gaussian, the average is exactly normal and the CLT carries no error. The remaining unknown is $\sigma$. Replacing it by the sample $s$ is why the standardized mean $\frac{\bar X - \mu}{s/\sqrt n}$ follows the **$t$ distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom**, $t_{n-1}$, rather than the standard normal. It has heavier tails and converges to the normal as $n \to \infty$, and in practice $t \approx z$ for $n \gtrsim 30$. A $t$-table makes the gap concrete: $t_{30,\,0.975} = 2.042$, about 4% larger than the normal's $1.96$, and the gap is bigger when $n$ is smaller. Intervals and tests for a mean then use the $t_{n-1}$ quantile in place of the normal one.

### Confidence intervals: a score with a margin of error

A normal variable lands within 1.96 standard deviations of its mean 95% of the time. So 95% of the time, $|\hat{p} - p| \le 1.96 \cdot \text{SE}$. That inequality reads the same both ways: "$\hat{p}$ is near $p$" is the same statement as "$p$ is near $\hat{p}$". So $p$ lies in the **95% confidence interval**

$$
\hat{p} \pm 1.96 \cdot \text{SE} = 0.87 \pm 1.96 \cdot 0.0336 = [0.804,\ 0.936] .
$$

The 95% describes the method, not this one interval. If you drew a new test set many times and built a new interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true $p$.

The same 1.96 sizes a test set. Setting the half-width $1.96\sqrt{0.25/n}$ (the worst case, $p = \tfrac12$) to 0.05 gives $n \ge (1.96/0.05)^2 \cdot 0.25 \approx 384.2$, so 385 examples. Hoeffding needed 738 and Chebyshev 2000, because they are guarantees and the CLT is an approximation.

Hoeffding gives a guaranteed interval too. Setting $2e^{-2nt^2} = 0.05$ with $n = 100$ gives $t = \sqrt{\ln 40/200} \approx 0.136$, so $0.87 \pm 0.136$. That is about twice as wide, because it must hold for every distribution on $[0, 1]$.

### Hypothesis tests: could this be luck?

A **hypothesis test** turns the question around. Suppose the new model is no better: $p = 0.80$. This "nothing changed" assumption is called the **null hypothesis**. How often would a model with $p = 0.80$ score 87 or more on 100 examples by luck?

Under the null hypothesis the standard error is $\sqrt{0.80 \cdot 0.20 / 100} = 0.04$. Our score is $0.87 - 0.80 = 0.07$ above 0.80, which is $0.07 / 0.04 = 1.75$ standard errors. This count of standard errors is the **z-score**. A normal variable lands 1.75 or more standard deviations above its mean about 4% of the time. You read this from a normal table, or compute $1 - \Phi(1.75) \approx 0.040$, where $\Phi(z)$ is the chance that a standard normal variable is at most $z$. That 4% is the **p-value**: the chance of a result at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true.

We count only results above 0.80, because only a better model interests us. This is a **one-sided** test, and its 5% cutoff is $z = 1.645$. The 1.96 in the interval splits 5% between two tails, 2.5% each.

Reject when the p-value is under the cutoff $\alpha$ (5% here).

| | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reject | Type I error ($\alpha$) | correct (power $= 1-\beta$) |
| Fail to reject | correct | Type II error ($\beta$) |

The normal curve is an approximation here too. The exact binomial probability of 87 or more correct when $p = 0.8$ is about 4.7%, even closer to the 5% line.

Testing many models on one test set inflates false wins. If 20 models that are no better each have a 5% chance of a false "win", you expect about one win by luck. If their results were independent, the chance of at least one would be $1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 64\%$. Without independence there is still a cap, the **union bound**: the chance that at least one of several events happens is at most the sum of their chances. For 5 such models that is $5 \cdot 5\% = 25\%$. Keep a fresh test set for the final comparison.

### Solving the focus problem

**Part 1.** The accuracy is 0.87 with standard error 0.0336, so the 95% confidence interval is $[0.804,\ 0.936]$.

**Part 2.** If the true accuracy were 0.80, a score of 87 or more would happen about 4% of the time ($z = 1.75$). That is under the 5% cutoff, but only barely.

Both answers say the same thing. 80% sits just outside the plausible range, so the gain is probably real, but not by a wide margin. Even if the true accuracy is 0.87, a fresh 100-example test scores 86 or less about 43% of the time. A score of 86 gives $z = 1.5$, which does not clear the cutoff. A larger test set settles it.

### The bootstrap: intervals without a formula

The formula above works for accuracy. A median, a correlation or the F1 score (a classification score from [Classification Metrics](2.3-ML6-model-evaluation.md)) often has no simple formula. The **bootstrap** gets an interval by simulation instead:

1. Treat your 100 test results as a stand-in for all possible test examples.
2. Draw 100 of them **with replacement** (some appear twice, some not at all) and compute the score.
3. Repeat thousands of times and sort the scores. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, with 2.5% of the scores below and 2.5% above, are the ends of the 95% interval.

Each resample stands in for a different test set. Their spread estimates how much the score would move on a real one.

The bootstrap has limits. It needs $n$ reasonably large and a statistic that depends smoothly on the data, and it fails for extremes like the maximum: a resample contains the sample maximum with probability $1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \to 1 - 1/e \approx 63\%$, so most replicates repeat the same top value and the interval is unreliable.

## Implementation

The first block computes the MLE and the sample variance for 3, 5, 7, 9. It then averages both variance estimates over 200,000 random samples of size 4 with true variance 4, and ends with the MLE and MAP for the coin.

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.array([3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.0])
print(f"mean {x.mean():.3f}, var /n {x.var():.3f}, var /(n-1) {x.var(ddof=1):.3f}")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
samples = rng.normal(0.0, 2.0, size=(200_000, 4))      # true variance 4, samples of size n = 4
print(f"avg MLE variance:  {samples.var(axis=1).mean():.3f}  (theory 3.000)")
print(f"avg sample variance: {samples.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean():.3f}  (theory 4.000)")

k, trials = 87, 100
a, b = 2, 2                                            # Beta(2, 2) prior
print(f"MLE k/n = {k / trials:.4f},  MAP with Beta(2, 2) = {(a + k - 1) / (a + b + trials - 2):.4f}")
```

```output
MLE mean 6.000, MLE variance (divide by n) 5.000, sample variance (n - 1) 6.667
avg MLE variance:  3.002  (theory 3.000)
avg sample variance: 4.003  (theory 4.000)
MLE k/n = 0.8700,  MAP with Beta(2, 2) = 0.8627
```

The divide-by-$n$ estimate averages 3.00, not 4, which is the $\frac{n-1}{n} = \frac34$ bias. The $n - 1$ version averages 4.00.

The next block computes the test-set size for $\pm 0.05$ at 95% from each bound.

```python
from math import log, ceil

t, delta = 0.05, 0.05                         # tolerance and failure probability
print(f"Chebyshev: n >= {ceil(1 / (4 * t**2 * delta))}")
print(f"Hoeffding: n >= {ceil(log(2 / delta) / (2 * t**2))}")
print(f"CLT (approximate): n >= {ceil(1.96**2 * 0.25 / t**2)}")
```

```output
Chebyshev: n >= 2000
Hoeffding: n >= 738
CLT (approximate): n >= 385
```

The CLT's 385 is about half of Hoeffding's 738, because Hoeffding must hold for every distribution on $[0, 1]$.

The next block averages 30 draws from the exponential distribution, which is skewed, with mean 1 and variance 1. It does this 100,000 times and checks how normal the averages look.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
draws = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(100_000, 30))
means = draws.mean(axis=1)
se = 1 / np.sqrt(30)
print(f"mean of the averages {means.mean():.3f}, sd {means.std():.4f} (theory {se:.4f})")
print(f"within 1.96 SE of 1: {np.mean(np.abs(means - 1) <= 1.96 * se):.3f}")
```

```output
mean of the averages 1.000, sd 0.1822 (theory 0.1826)
within 1.96 SE of 1: 0.953
```

The spread of the averages, 0.182, matches $1/\sqrt{30}$. And 95.3% of them land within 1.96 standard errors of 1, close to the 95% a normal variable gives.

The next block computes the interval and the test from the focus problem, and the Hoeffding interval for comparison.

```python
from math import erf, sqrt, log

def normal_cdf(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2)))

n, correct = 100, 87
p_hat = correct / n
se = sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / n)
lo, hi = p_hat - 1.96 * se, p_hat + 1.96 * se
print(f"accuracy {p_hat:.2f}, standard error {se:.4f}")
print(f"95% confidence interval [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]")

p0 = 0.80                                     # the baseline we want to beat
z = (p_hat - p0) / sqrt(p0 * (1 - p0) / n)
print(f"z = {z:.2f}, one-sided p-value = {1 - normal_cdf(z):.4f}")

h = sqrt(log(2 / 0.05) / (2 * n))
print(f"Hoeffding 95% interval [{p_hat - h:.3f}, {min(1.0, p_hat + h):.3f}]  (half-width {h:.3f})")
```

```output
accuracy 0.87, standard error 0.0336
95% confidence interval [0.804, 0.936]
z = 1.75, one-sided p-value = 0.0401
Hoeffding 95% interval [0.734, 1.000]  (half-width 0.136)
```

The numbers match the hand calculation. The Hoeffding interval runs past 1, so the code clips it at 1.000, since an accuracy cannot exceed 1.

The next block checks what "95%" means by simulation. A model with true accuracy 0.85 is tested on 10,000 different test sets of 100 examples, and we count how many of the intervals contain 0.85.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
true_p, num_sets, covered = 0.85, 10_000, 0
for _ in range(num_sets):
    acc = rng.binomial(n, true_p) / n
    s = sqrt(acc * (1 - acc) / n)
    covered += (acc - 1.96 * s <= true_p <= acc + 1.96 * s)
print(f"intervals that contain the true accuracy: {covered / num_sets:.3f}")
```

```output
intervals that contain the true accuracy: 0.931
```

Only 93.1% of the intervals contain 0.85, a little below 95%. The formula rests on the normal approximation, which is not exact at $n = 100$.

Last, the bootstrap on the same 100 results.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
results = np.array([1] * 87 + [0] * 13)        # 1 = correct, one entry per test example
boot = [rng.choice(results, size=n, replace=True).mean() for _ in range(5000)]
print(f"bootstrap 95% interval [{np.percentile(boot, 2.5):.3f}, {np.percentile(boot, 97.5):.3f}]")
```

```output
bootstrap 95% interval [0.800, 0.930]
```

The bootstrap interval agrees with the formula's $[0.804, 0.936]$ to within a percentage point, without using a formula.

> **Warning.**
>
> A p-value of 0.04 means: if the model were no better, a result at least this good would happen 4% of the time. It does not mean there is a 4% chance the model is no better. It does not establish the size or practical importance of an effect: with $n = 10^6$, a meaningless $0.1\%$ difference is already "significant" ($z \approx 2$).

<Optional title="Why coverage is not exactly 95%">

The interval $\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$ is called the **Wald interval**. Its true coverage jumps up and down as $p$ and $n$ change, because $\hat{p}$ can take only the $n + 1$ values $0, 1/n, \dots, 1$. At $p = 0.85$ and $n = 100$, adding up the binomial probabilities of the $\hat{p}$ values whose interval contains 0.85 gives 93.3%, in line with the simulation. The shortfall is worst near 0 or 1. At $p = 0.01$ and $n = 100$ the Wald interval contains the truth only 63% of the time.

The **Wilson interval** solves $|\hat{p} - p| \le 1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ for $p$ instead of plugging in $\hat{p}$. It never goes below 0 or above 1. Averaged over $p$ from 0.01 to 0.99 at $n = 100$, it covers about 95%, against about 93% for the Wald interval. At a single $p$ it can still miss: at $p = 0.85$ it also covers 93.3%. Brown, Cai and DasGupta (*Statistical Science*, 2001) recommend it over the Wald interval for small $n$.

<Optional title="Comparing two models on the same test set">

When both models are scored on the same examples, compare them example by example. Only the examples where the models disagree carry information. Say model A is right and B wrong on $b$ examples, and the reverse on $c$ examples. If the models are equally good, each disagreement is a fair coin flip, and the z-score is $(b - c)/\sqrt{b + c}$. This is **McNemar's test**. It is usually more sensitive than comparing two separate confidence intervals, because the examples both models find easy or hard cancel out.

For a continuous score per example (a regression output, a ranking score) the same comparison applies: take differences $d_i = x_{1i} - x_{2i}$ and test whether $\bar d = 0$, with $t = \bar d/(s_d/\sqrt n)$ on $n - 1$ degrees of freedom, where $s_d$ is the sample standard deviation of the differences. Read the p-value from the $t$ distribution, or from the normal table when $n$ is about 30 or more.

</Optional>

<Optional title="Bayesian linear regression and other priors">

With prior $\mathbf{w} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \tau^2 I)$, noise variance $\sigma^2$ and $X$ holding one example per row, the posterior over all the weights is Gaussian with

$$
\Sigma_{\text{post}} = \Big(\frac{X^\top X}{\sigma^2} + \frac{I}{\tau^2}\Big)^{-1},
\qquad
\boldsymbol\mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\,\Sigma_{\text{post}}X^\top\mathbf{y} ,
$$

the matrix version of "precisions add". The mean is the ridge solution. At a new input $\mathbf{x}_*$, the prediction has variance $\sigma^2 + \mathbf{x}_*^\top\Sigma_{\text{post}}\mathbf{x}_*$, which grows as $\mathbf{x}_*$ moves away from the training data.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, d, sigma, tau = 30, 3, 0.5, 1.0                     # noise sd sigma, prior w ~ N(0, tau^2 I)
X = rng.normal(size=(n, d))                            # one example per row
y = X @ rng.normal(scale=tau, size=d) + sigma * rng.normal(size=n)

Sigma_post = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X / sigma**2 + np.eye(d) / tau**2)
mu_post = Sigma_post @ X.T @ y / sigma**2
ridge = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + (sigma**2 / tau**2) * np.eye(d), X.T @ y)
print(f"posterior mean equals ridge, lambda = sigma^2 / tau^2: {np.allclose(mu_post, ridge)}")
for r in (0.5, 2.0, 8.0):
    x_new = r * np.ones(d) / np.sqrt(d)                # a new input at distance r from the origin
    print(f"predictive sd at distance {r}: {np.sqrt(sigma**2 + x_new @ Sigma_post @ x_new):.3f}")
```

```output
posterior mean equals ridge, lambda = sigma^2 / tau^2: True
predictive sd at distance 0.5: 0.503
predictive sd at distance 2.0: 0.542
predictive sd at distance 8.0: 0.979
```

Near the data the predictive spread is barely above the noise level of 0.5. At distance 8 it nearly doubles, to 0.98. A ridge fit gives only the mean, so it does not show this growing uncertainty.

Other priors give other penalties. A Laplace prior has density proportional to $e^{-|w|/s}$ for a scale $s$. Its negative log is $|w|/s$ plus a constant, so the MAP penalty is a multiple of $|w|$, the lasso's $L_1$ penalty (Tibshirani, 1996). Weight decay in a neural network trained with plain SGD is the Gaussian prior again. With adaptive optimizers such as Adam the two are not the same (Loshchilov and Hutter, arXiv:1711.05101).

</Optional>
