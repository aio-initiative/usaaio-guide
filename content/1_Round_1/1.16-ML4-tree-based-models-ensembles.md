## Overview

A deep decision tree is low-bias and high-variance: it fits its training set exactly and a different sample would give a different tree. **Ensemble learning** trains many models and combines them. **Bagging** trains them in parallel on resampled data and averages, which cuts variance. **Boosting** trains them in sequence, each on what the ones before got wrong, which cuts bias. Random forests are bagging plus one trick, and gradient boosting is the model to beat on tabular data.

Round 1 asks which family reduces which error and why a random forest beats plain bagging. The explanation answers both with one formula.

## Example: averaging ten noisy models

$B$ models each predict the same quantity with variance $\sigma^2 = 1$, and every pair of predictions has correlation $\rho = 0.3$. The ensemble predicts their average. What is the variance of the average for $B = 10$, what is the smallest $B$ that brings it within 10% of its limit as $B \to \infty$, and what lowers that limit: adding models or making them less correlated?

The question asks for the variance of an average of correlated estimators, and its answer is the design rule behind every bagged model.

## Explanation

### The variance of an average

Train $B$ models and look at their predictions $Z_1, \dots, Z_B$ at one input. Each prediction depends on the training data, which is a random sample, so each one varies. Suppose every prediction has the same variance $\sigma^2$, and every pair has the same **correlation** $\rho$, a number that says how much two models' errors move together. Then the covariance of two different predictions is $\rho\sigma^2$.

The ensemble predicts the average, $\bar{Z} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_b Z_b$. The variance of a sum is the sum of the covariances of all the pairs, each prediction paired with itself included:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{Z}) &= \frac{1}{B^2}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i, Z_j) \\
&= \frac{1}{B^2}\Big(B\,\sigma^2 + (B^2 - B)\,\rho\sigma^2\Big),
\end{aligned}
$$

There are $B$ terms with $i = j$, each equal to $\sigma^2$, and $B^2 - B$ terms with $i \ne j$, each equal to $\rho\sigma^2$. Simplifying,

$$
\boxed{\;\operatorname{Var}(\bar{Z}) = \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2\;}
$$

The second term goes to zero as $B$ grows. That is what adding models buys. The first term does not depend on $B$, so no amount of averaging gets below $\rho\sigma^2$. The correlation between the models sets a floor.

For the example, $B = 10$ gives $0.3 + 0.7 / 10 = 0.37$. The limit is 0.3, and the variance is within 10% of it when $(1 - \rho) / B \le 0.1\rho$, so $B \ge 10 \cdot 0.7 / 0.3 = 23.3$: twenty-four models. Adding models past that point does almost nothing. Only a smaller $\rho$ lowers the floor, and a random forest gets one by letting each split see only some of the features.

The formula for three correlations. Each curve flattens onto its own floor $\rho\sigma^2$ within a few dozen models.
</Figure>

### Bagging

The formula says to use low-bias, high-variance base models and make them as different from each other as possible. **Bagging** (bootstrap aggregating) gets different models from one training set by giving each a **bootstrap sample**: $n$ rows drawn with replacement from the $n$ rows. A given row is left out of one draw with probability $1 - 1/n$, so out of all $n$ draws with probability $(1 - 1/n)^n \to 1/e \approx 0.37$. Each tree sees about 63% of the distinct rows, so the trees differ, and their votes are averaged. Deep trees are the right base model: they have plenty of variance to remove and little bias to keep.

The 37% a tree never saw are its **out-of-bag** rows. Predicting each row with only the trees that did not train on it gives a validation score for free, with no separate split. It can stand in for cross-validation when data is scarce, and it cannot replace the final test set once it has been used to choose anything.

### Random forests

Bootstrapping alone leaves the trees correlated. When one feature is much stronger than the rest, every tree splits on it near the root and the trees look alike, so $\rho$ stays high and the floor stays high. A **random forest** adds a second source of randomness aimed at $\rho$: at every split the tree may only choose among a random subset of the features, usually $\sqrt{d}$ of them. Different trees are pushed onto different features, the correlation drops, and the floor comes down. That one change is the whole difference between bagging and random forests, and the code below measures that correlation directly. In scikit-learn the default is $\sqrt{d}$ for a classifier and all $d$ features for a regressor, so a `RandomForestRegressor` is plain bagging until `max_features` is set.

A forest also ranks its features. `feature_importances_` is the impurity each feature's splits removed, averaged over the trees. It is computed from the training rows and favors features with many distinct values, so confirm a ranking with permutation importance on held-out rows.

<ProblemCard id="ens-copied-column-importance" />

Averaging is not limited to trees. A **voting** ensemble combines different model types, by majority label (hard voting) or by averaging their probabilities (soft voting). **Stacking** fits a small model on their held-out predictions to learn how to weight them. Both help only when the members make different mistakes, which is the same correlation story.

### Boosting

Boosting works on the other error, bias. It trains **weak learners**, often trees of depth 1 to 3, in sequence, each one on what the ensemble so far gets wrong, and adds them up. A weak learner is a model that performs only slightly better than random guessing. **AdaBoost** keeps a weight $w_i$ on every training row. Start with equal weights on all examples. Each round fits a learner $h_t$ to the weighted rows, computes its weighted error $\varepsilon_t$, and gives it the vote

$$
\alpha_t = \tfrac12 \ln \frac{1 - \varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}.
$$

(This is the classic binary AdaBoost form. scikit-learn's `AdaBoostClassifier` uses SAMME, which for two classes at its default `learning_rate` of 1.0 reports $\ln\frac{1 - \varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}$, exactly twice this, and the doubled weights give the same predictions.)

It then multiplies the weight of every misclassified row by $e^{\alpha_t}$ and of every correct row by $e^{-\alpha_t}$ and renormalizes, so the next learner concentrates on the rows this one got wrong. The prediction is the sign of $\sum_t \alpha_t h_t(\mathbf{x})$. A learner at chance gets no vote, and one worse than chance gets a negative one.

<ProblemCard id="ens-adaboost-after-one-round" />

**Gradient boosting** replaces the reweighting with a gradient. Start from the best constant, $F_0 = \bar{y}$ for squared error, and at every round:

1. For each row, compute the direction in which its prediction should move to lower the loss. That is the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the prediction, $r_i = -\partial L(y_i, F(\mathbf{x}_i)) / \partial F(\mathbf{x}_i)$, called the **pseudo-residual**. For squared error it is the ordinary residual $y_i - F(\mathbf{x}_i)$. For log loss with $p = \sigma(F)$ it is $y_i - p_i$.
2. Fit a small regression tree $h$ to the pseudo-residuals. Grown until each leaf holds one training row, such a tree reproduces those residuals exactly on the training rows: every row's residual is its own leaf value, so $h(\mathbf{x}_i) = r_i$.
3. Step: $F \leftarrow F + \nu\, h$, with a **learning rate** $\nu$ around 0.1.

Each round is a step of gradient descent in which the step direction is a tree. Bias falls round by round, and so does the training error, all the way to zero if the rounds continue, so boosting overfits where bagging does not. The number of rounds is chosen on a validation set. A smaller $\nu$ needs more rounds and usually ends lower, and the tree depth caps how much one round can fit. Fitting each tree to a random fraction of the rows, `subsample` below 1, lowers the variance as well, which is the trade [bias-variance cross-validation](2.16-ML11-bias-variance-cross-validation.md) measures directly.

<ProblemCard id="ens-absolute-error-first-round" />

**XGBoost** also uses the curvature of the loss. For each row it computes the first derivative $g_i$ and the second derivative $h_i$ of the loss with respect to the prediction. For log loss they are $g = p - y$ and $h = p(1 - p)$. With a penalty $\lambda$ on the leaf values, the best value of a leaf and the gain of a split have formulas:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w^* &= -\frac{G}{H + \lambda}, \\[4pt]
\text{gain} &= \frac12\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\right] - \gamma,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $G$ and $H$ are the sums of $g_i$ and $h_i$ over the rows in a leaf, and $\gamma$ is a fixed price per split. Each leaf value is a Newton step, the same step as in [logistic regression](1.27-ML8-linear-logistic-regression.md). LightGBM and scikit-learn's `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` speed up the split search by binning every feature into a histogram, and both take missing values as they are. On a table of numbers, one of these with a tuned learning rate and round count is the baseline everything else is measured against.

## Implementation

### The formula, simulated

Each model's prediction is a shared part every model has plus its own noise, so that pairs correlate at $\rho$. Change `rho` and watch the average's variance land on the formula:

<EmbeddedPyEditor packages="numpy">
import numpy as np

rho, sigma, trials = 0.3, 1.0, 20000                # change rho
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for B in (1, 5, 10, 24, 100):
    shared = rng.normal(size=(trials, 1))           # the part every model has in common
    own = rng.normal(size=(trials, B))              # each model's own noise
    Z = sigma * (np.sqrt(rho) * shared + np.sqrt(1 - rho) * own)
    print(f"B = {B:>3}: simulated variance {Z.mean(axis=1).var():.3f}   formula {rho * sigma**2 + (1 - rho) / B * sigma**2:.3f}")
</EmbeddedPyEditor>

### Bagging and a forest, measured

The data is noisy, so that variance is what matters: 300 training rows from a ring with 15% of the labels flipped, so no model beats 0.85 on average. The 4,000 test rows are there so that a third decimal means something.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.ensemble import BaggingClassifier, RandomForestClassifier

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

def make_data(n):
    X = rng.uniform(-3, 3, size=(n, 2))
    label = ((X[:, 0] ** 2 + X[:, 1] ** 2) > 3.5).astype(int)   # a ring
    flip = rng.random(n) < 0.15                                 # 15% label noise: the ceiling is 0.85
    return X, np.where(flip, 1 - label, label)

X_train, y_train = make_data(300)
X_test, y_test = make_data(4000)

tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(f"one deep tree: test accuracy {tree.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}")
print(f"{'trees':>5} {'bagging':>8} {'forest':>7}")
for B in (1, 5, 25, 100):
    bag = BaggingClassifier(DecisionTreeClassifier(), n_estimators=B, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
    forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=B, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(f"{B:>5} {bag.score(X_test, y_test):>8.3f} {forest.score(X_test, y_test):>7.3f}")
```

```output
one deep tree: test accuracy 0.700
trees  bagging  forest
    1    0.728   0.726
    5    0.760   0.767
   25    0.770   0.777
  100    0.771   0.780
```

One tree, trained to purity on noisy labels, scores 0.700. Five bagged trees score six points higher, and the gain flattens after 25, as the formula says it should.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/ensemble-accuracy.svg" alt="Test accuracy against the number of trees on a log axis for bagging and a random forest, both rising steeply over the first ten trees and flattening below the dashed noise ceiling of 0.85, with a lower dashed line for one deep tree.">
The same run for every number of trees from 1 to 200. Most of the gain comes from the first ten trees, and the forest sits a little above bagging from then on.
</Figure>

The forest's edge is the floor. With two features it may use only one at each split, and the correlation between its trees' predictions is lower:

```python
def mean_correlation(ensemble):
    votes = np.array([t.predict(X_test) for t in ensemble.estimators_], dtype=float)
    C = np.corrcoef(votes)
    return (C.sum() - len(votes)) / (len(votes) * (len(votes) - 1))   # mean of the off-diagonal entries

bag = BaggingClassifier(DecisionTreeClassifier(), n_estimators=100, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=100, oob_score=True, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(f"mean correlation between trees: bagging {mean_correlation(bag):.3f}, forest {mean_correlation(forest):.3f}")
print(f"forest out-of-bag accuracy {forest.oob_score_:.3f}, test accuracy {forest.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}")
```

```output
mean correlation between trees: bagging 0.481, forest 0.430
forest out-of-bag accuracy 0.793, test accuracy 0.780
```

The out-of-bag score, computed from nothing but the training rows, lands within 0.013 of the test accuracy.

### Boosting rounds

The same data with gradient boosting, scored after every round:

```python
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingClassifier, HistGradientBoostingClassifier

for lr in (0.01, 0.1, 1.0):
    gb = GradientBoostingClassifier(n_estimators=300, learning_rate=lr, max_depth=3, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
    acc = np.array([np.mean(p == y_test) for p in gb.staged_predict(X_test)])
    print(f"learning rate {lr:<4}: best {acc.max():.3f} after {acc.argmax() + 1} rounds, {acc[-1]:.3f} after 300")
hgb = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(f"HistGradientBoostingClassifier with defaults: test accuracy {hgb.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}")
```

```output
learning rate 0.01: best 0.798 after 154 rounds, 0.791 after 300
learning rate 0.1 : best 0.795 after 15 rounds, 0.746 after 300
learning rate 1.0 : best 0.767 after 3 rounds, 0.735 after 300
HistGradientBoostingClassifier with defaults: test accuracy 0.780
```

At a learning rate of 0.1 the ensemble peaks at round 15 and then fits the flipped labels, losing five points by round 300. At 0.01 it peaks later and higher and barely declines, which is the usual trade: a small rate with many rounds. At 1.0 it is done in three rounds and worse. The number of rounds is a hyperparameter to be chosen on held-out data, and `n_iter_no_change` stops the fit when a validation score stalls. `HistGradientBoostingClassifier` with its defaults, 100 rounds at a rate of 0.1 and early stopping only above 10,000 rows, scores 0.780. That is below the best round at either smaller rate, so the defaults are a starting point.

### Gradient boosting, written out

The loop itself, for squared error, next to scikit-learn's:

```python
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.uniform(-3, 3, size=(600, 2))
y = np.sin(X[:, 0]) * X[:, 1] + 0.5 * rng.normal(size=600)
Xtr, ytr, Xva, yva = X[:400], y[:400], X[400:], y[400:]

def boost(X, y, rounds, lr=0.1, depth=5):
    """Gradient boosting for squared error: every tree is fit to the current residuals."""
    base = y.mean()
    F = np.full(len(y), base)
    trees = []
    for _ in range(rounds):
        residual = y - F                               # the negative gradient of (y - F)^2 / 2
        tree = DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=depth, random_state=0).fit(X, residual)
        F += lr * tree.predict(X)
        trees.append(tree)
    return base, trees

def predict(model, X, lr=0.1):
    base, trees = model
    return base + lr * sum(t.predict(X) for t in trees)

ours = boost(Xtr, ytr, rounds=600)
lib = GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=600, learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=5,
                                random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
same = np.allclose(predict(ours, Xtr), lib.predict(Xtr), rtol=0, atol=1e-10)
print(f"the loop reproduces scikit-learn on the training rows: {same}")

val = np.array([np.mean((p - yva) ** 2) for p in lib.staged_predict(Xva)])
trn = np.array([np.mean((p - ytr) ** 2) for p in lib.staged_predict(Xtr)])
best = int(val.argmin())
print(f"validation MSE is lowest after {best + 1} rounds")
for m in (10, best + 1, 200, 600):
    print(f"  after {m:>3} rounds: train MSE {trn[m - 1]:.4f}   validation MSE {val[m - 1]:.3f}")
```

```output
the loop reproduces scikit-learn on the training rows: True
validation MSE is lowest after 66 rounds
  after  10 rounds: train MSE 0.5160   validation MSE 0.793
  after  66 rounds: train MSE 0.0894   validation MSE 0.391
  after 200 rounds: train MSE 0.0151   validation MSE 0.414
  after 600 rounds: train MSE 0.0002   validation MSE 0.445
```

Nine lines reproduce the library to $10^{-10}$. Training error heads for zero because every round removes part of what is left, and validation error turns at round 66.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/boosting-rounds.svg" alt="Training and validation mean squared error against the number of boosting rounds on a log axis. Training error falls toward zero. Validation error drops quickly, is lowest at round 66, and rises slowly afterward.">
Every round of the run above. The training curve never stops falling. The validation curve turns at round 66 and drifts up as the trees start fitting the noise.
</Figure>
