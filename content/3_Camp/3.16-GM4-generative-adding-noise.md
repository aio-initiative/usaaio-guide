## Adding noise

### The problem

The VAE of part 2 and the GAN of part 3 both turn random numbers into an image in a single pass, and each fails in its own way. For example, with the two images of this chapter:

- The **VAE** of part 2 gives varied images, but blurry ones. Codes of 0.8, 0 and −0.4 decode to (0.9, 0.1, 0.9, 0.1), (0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5) and (0.3, 0.7, 0.3, 0.7).
- The collapsed **GAN** of part 3 gives a sharp image, but the same one every time: LEFT, and never RIGHT.

The VAE gives three different blends, with pixels such as 0.9 and 0.1, 0.5 and 0.5, 0.3 and 0.7: varied, but blurry. The collapsed GAN gives LEFT three times: sharp, but always the same. Three samples from each of the two earlier models, and what is wanted.

GAN training is also unstable, because two networks have to be kept in balance. A better approach would train one network with a simple, stable loss, and still produce images that are both sharp and varied.

### Earlier approaches

#### GANs

As in part 3, the generator makes the whole image in one pass, guided only by a discriminator that is itself still learning.

#### Early diffusion

Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015) proposed generating by reversing a slow process that turns data into noise. The idea was sound, but its images were far behind GANs until DDPM (2020) simplified the training.

### Many small steps

**The forward process.** Diffusion splits generation into many small steps. This step covers the **forward process**, which goes the other way: it destroys an image by adding a little random noise at each of $T$ steps, 1,000 in DDPM, until almost only noise is left. Nothing is learned here. It is a fixed recipe that produces training examples for [Removing noise](#lesson-removing-noise).

**One jump.** The noise added at each step is drawn from a normal distribution, so the image after $t$ steps can be computed in one jump:

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon
$$

Here $x_0$ is the clean image and $\varepsilon$ is random noise. The number $\bar\alpha_t$ follows a fixed schedule, falling from 1 toward 0 as $t$ grows.

**Reading the formula.** The image is scaled down by $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and the noise is scaled by $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$. For example, at $\bar\alpha_t = 0.659$ the image is multiplied by $\sqrt{0.659} = 0.812$ and the noise by $\sqrt{0.341} = 0.584$. A pixel of 1 with noise of 0.13 becomes 0.812 × 1 + 0.584 × 0.13 = 0.888.

#### The derivation

**One step.** Each step scales the image down slightly and adds a little fresh noise:

$$
x_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, \varepsilon_t,
$$

with a new draw $\varepsilon_t$ at every step. DDPM's $\beta_t$ rises evenly from 0.0001 at step 1 to 0.02 at step 1000. At step 500, $\beta_t = 0.01004$, so each pixel is multiplied by $\sqrt{1 - 0.01004} = 0.995$ and gets noise with spread $\sqrt{0.01004} = 0.100$. Write $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ for the share of the signal each step keeps.

**Two steps.** Substituting the step before gives

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}}\, x_{t-2} + \sqrt{\alpha_t (1 - \alpha_{t-1})}\, \varepsilon_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\, \varepsilon_t.
$$

The two noise terms are independent normals with mean 0, so their sum is a normal whose variance is the sum of theirs, $\alpha_t (1 - \alpha_{t-1}) + (1 - \alpha_t) = 1 - \alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}$. Two steps are one step with $\alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}$ in place of $\alpha_t$.

**Any number of steps.** Repeating the merge back to $x_0$ multiplies all the $\alpha$'s together, $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \cdots \alpha_t$, which gives the one-jump formula: given $x_0$, $x_t$ is normal with mean $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0$ and variance $1 - \bar\alpha_t$. The merge works for any two stretches of steps. Steps 1 to 200 multiply LEFT by $\sqrt{0.659} = 0.812$ and add noise of variance $1 - 0.659 = 0.341$. Steps 201 to 500 keep $\alpha_{201} \cdots \alpha_{500} = 0.1192$, so they multiply by $\sqrt{0.1192} = 0.345$ and add noise of variance $1 - 0.1192 = 0.8808$. Together they multiply by $0.812 \times 0.345 = 0.280$ and add noise of variance $0.1192 \times 0.3410 + 0.8808 = 0.9214$, which is $1 - 0.0786$, the one jump to step 500.

The arrow for steps 201 to 500 is labeled times 0.345, noise variance 0.881. A dashed arc from x0 to x500 is labeled one jump: times 0.280, noise variance 0.921. Underneath: 0.812 times 0.345 = 0.280, and 0.1192 times 0.3410 plus 0.8808 = 0.9214.">

### Running example

Take LEFT and one draw of random noise, ε = (0.13, −0.13, 0.64, 0.10). With DDPM's schedule:

| Step t | $\bar\alpha_t$ | $x_t$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | (1, 0, 1, 0) |
| 50 | 0.971 | (1.008, −0.022, 1.094, 0.017) |
| 200 | 0.659 | (0.888, −0.076, 1.186, 0.058) |
| 500 | 0.079 | (0.405, −0.125, 0.895, 0.096) |
| 1000 | 0.00004 | (0.136, −0.130, 0.646, 0.100) |

At step 50 the image is LEFT with a slight disturbance. By step 500 the image is scaled down to $\sqrt{0.079} = 0.28$ while the noise is scaled to 0.96, and by step 1000 the result is essentially $\varepsilon$ itself: pure noise with no trace of LEFT.

```python
import numpy as np

betas = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, 1000)       # DDPM's noise schedule
alpha_bar = np.cumprod(1 - betas)

x0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0])           # LEFT
eps = np.array([0.13, -0.13, 0.64, 0.10])
for t in (50, 200, 500, 1000):
    a = alpha_bar[t - 1]
    print(t, round(a, 5), (np.sqrt(a) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - a) * eps).round(3))
```

### Common questions

**If one jump gives any step, why have steps at all?** The jump is a shortcut for making training examples. The steps matter in the other direction: removing a little noise is a problem a network can learn, while turning pure noise into an image in one go is what GANs struggle with.

**Why is the image scaled down as the noise is added?** To keep the overall size of the numbers steady. The squares of the two factors add up to 1: at step 200, 0.659 + 0.341 = 1. Without the scaling, the values would grow with every step.

**Is anything of the image left at step 1000?** Almost nothing. The image is multiplied by $\sqrt{0.00004} = 0.006$, so the first pixel of LEFT contributes 0.006 to a value of 0.136.

**Is the noise the same at every step?** In the one-jump formula, $\varepsilon$ is a single draw used for whichever step is wanted. Each training example uses a new draw.

The next section, [Removing noise](#lesson-removing-noise), learns to run this process backwards.

## Removing noise

### The problem

[Adding noise](#lesson-adding-noise) turns any image into noise. To generate, that process has to run backwards: start from pure noise and remove a little at a time until an image appears.

Adding noise needs no knowledge. Removing it does. For example, adding noise turned LEFT into (0.136, −0.130, 0.646, 0.100) at step 1000. The same noise turns RIGHT into (0.130, −0.124, 0.640, 0.106), almost the same four numbers. Nothing in either says which image it came from.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/generative-problem-denoise.svg" alt="On the left, a 2 by 2 image of pure noise at step 1000, with pixels 0.14, -0.13, 0.65 and 0.10. An arrow with a question mark leads to two possible results, LEFT or RIGHT. A note says: a training image, not some other four numbers.">
The reverse process has to turn noise into one of the training images.
</Figure>

To get from numbers like these to (1, 0, 1, 0) or (0, 1, 0, 1), and not to some other four numbers, the model has to know what the training images look like. It also has to learn that with a simple, stable loss, which was the aim of [Adding noise](#lesson-adding-noise).

### Earlier approaches

#### Denoising filters

Classical filters remove noise by averaging neighboring pixels. They also blur the image, and they cannot make an image appear out of pure noise.

#### Predicting the clean image

The network could be trained to output the clean image directly. DDPM found that predicting the **noise** instead gave better images and a simpler loss.

### Predicting the noise

**The network.** A network, usually a U-Net (see [Segmentation](2.7-CV3-semantic-segmentation-unet-masks.md#lesson-segmentation) in Build a Vision Model), is given a noisy image $x_t$ and the step $t$, and predicts the noise $\varepsilon$ that was added.

**Training.** Training is simple:

1. Take a training image and a random step $t$.
2. Add noise as in [Adding noise](#lesson-adding-noise).
3. Train the network to predict that noise, using the mean squared error.

For example, a true noise of (0.1, −0.1) and a prediction of (0.2, −0.2) give a loss of $(0.1^2 + 0.1^2) \div 2 = 0.01$.

**From noise to image.** From a predicted noise $\hat\varepsilon$, the clean image can be estimated by solving the one-jump formula from [Adding noise](#lesson-adding-noise) for $x_0$:

$$
\hat{x}_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\; \hat\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.
$$

For example, at step 500 of the running example, $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 0.28$ and $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} = 0.96$. A noisy pixel of 0.405 with a predicted noise of 0.247 gives (0.405 − 0.96 × 0.247) ÷ 0.28 = 0.60.

**Generating.** Start from pure noise at $t = 1000$ and repeat: estimate the clean image, then move to a slightly less noisy image that is consistent with that estimate. DDPM took 1,000 steps, adding fresh noise at each one. DDIM (Song et al., 2020) showed that a deterministic reverse process needs far fewer steps than 1,000, roughly 10 to 50 times fewer, with a smooth trade-off between the number of steps and the quality. The example below uses 10.

#### The reverse step

**Bayes' rule.** Given the clean image $x_0$, the step back from $x_t$ to $x_{t-1}$ has an exact distribution. By Bayes' rule, and because the forward step from $x_{t-1}$ does not look at $x_0$,

$$
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \propto q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\, q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0).
$$

Both factors are normal in $x_{t-1}$, pixel by pixel. The forward step from [Adding noise](#lesson-adding-noise), $x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, \varepsilon_t$, puts $x_{t-1}$ near $x_t / \sqrt{\alpha_t}$ with variance $\beta_t / \alpha_t$. The one-jump formula puts it near $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0$ with variance $1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}$.

**Two normals multiplied.** The product of two normal curves in the same variable is a normal curve. Its precision, one over the variance, is the sum of the two precisions, and its mean is the average of the two centers weighted by their precisions. Simplifying with $\alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t = 1 - \bar\alpha_t$ gives

$$
\tilde\beta_t = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \beta_t, \qquad
\tilde\mu_t = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_0.
$$

For LEFT at step 500, $\alpha_{500} = 0.98996$, $\bar\alpha_{500} = 0.0786$ and $\bar\alpha_{499} = 0.0794$. The top-left pixel, $x_{500} = 0.405$, gives the centers $0.405 \div \sqrt{0.98996} = 0.407$, with precision $0.98996 \div 0.01004 = 98.6$, and $\sqrt{0.0794} \times 1 = 0.282$, with precision $1 \div 0.9206 = 1.09$. In the formula above the coefficients are $\sqrt{0.98996} \times 0.9206 \div 0.9214 = 0.994$ for $x_t$ and $\sqrt{0.0794} \times 0.01004 \div 0.9214 = 0.003$ for $x_0$, so the weighted mean is $0.994 \times 0.405 + 0.003 \times 1 = 0.406$ and the variance is $\tilde\beta_{500} = 0.0100$. One step back moves the pixel very little, and almost all of the information comes from $x_t$.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/generative-reverse-step.svg" alt="Three boxes: x0 = LEFT, x499 and x500. An arrow from x0 to x499 is labeled one jump: center 0.282, variance 0.921. An arrow from x499 to x500 is labeled one step: times 0.995, variance 0.0100. A dashed arc from x500 back to x499 is labeled true reverse step, given x0: mean 0.994 x500 + 0.003 x0, variance 0.0100.">
The true reverse step at step 500 combines the two forward facts about $x_{499}$: where the one jump from $x_0$ puts it, and what one step turned it into.
</Figure>

**The noise form.** At sampling time $x_0$ is unknown. Substituting $x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon) / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ from [Adding noise](#lesson-adding-noise) turns the mean into

$$
\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon\Big),
$$

so the noise is the only unknown, and a network that predicts it gives the whole step. With the true noise 0.13, the top-left pixel's mean is $(0.405 - 0.0105 \times 0.13) \div 0.995 = 0.406$, as before.

**Check by simulation.** Drawing a million pairs $(x_{499}, x_{500})$ for the top-left pixel of LEFT and fitting the straight line that best predicts $x_{499}$ from $x_{500}$ gives slope 0.994, intercept 0.003 and leftover variance 0.0100, the two coefficients and the variance above.

**Sampling with fresh noise.** DDPM's sampler uses this mean with the predicted noise in place of the true one, then adds fresh noise:

$$
x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \hat\varepsilon\Big) + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, z,
$$

with $z$ a new random draw, and none on the last step. At step 500 the fresh noise has spread $\sqrt{0.01004} = 0.100$, and 1,000 such steps make one image.

**The DDIM jump.** From the estimate $\hat x_0$ at step $t$ and the noise $\hat\varepsilon$ it implies, DDIM moves to an earlier step $t'$ with no fresh noise:

$$
x_{t'} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t'}}\, \hat x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t'}}\, \hat\varepsilon.
$$

The result has the form the network was trained on at step $t'$, so one jump can span many steps. For start A of the running example below, (0.19, −0.52, −0.41, −2.44), at step 1000, $\hat x_0 = (0.504, 0.496, 0.504, 0.496)$ and $\hat\varepsilon = (0.187, -0.523, -0.413, -2.443)$. With $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{900}} = 0.0166$ and $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{900}} = 0.9999$, the first pixel of $x_{900}$ is $0.0166 \times 0.504 + 0.9999 \times 0.187 = 0.195$. Fewer, longer jumps rely on estimates made at higher noise, and with too few of them the result is a blend:

| Sampling steps | Final image from start A |
|---|---|
| 1 | (0.504, 0.496, 0.504, 0.496) |
| 2 | (0.690, 0.310, 0.690, 0.310) |
| 3 | (0.881, 0.119, 0.881, 0.119) |
| 5 | (0.996, 0.004, 0.996, 0.004) |
| 10 | (1, 0, 1, 0), LEFT |

#### The loss

**KL between normals.** DDPM's training objective is a bound like the ELBO of the VAE in [part 2](2.13-RP1-representation-learning-autoencoders-vaes-latent-spaces.md#lesson-vae). Its main terms, one per step, are the KL divergence from the true reverse step to the network's step, a normal with mean $\mu_\theta$ and a fixed variance $\sigma_t^2$. For two normals with means $m_1, m_2$ and spreads $s_1, s_2$, the logarithm of the ratio of their densities at $x$ is

$$
\ln \frac{s_2}{s_1} - \frac{(x - m_1)^2}{2 s_1^2} + \frac{(x - m_2)^2}{2 s_2^2}.
$$

Averaging it over $x$ drawn from the first normal turns $(x - m_1)^2$ into $s_1^2$ and $(x - m_2)^2$ into $s_1^2 + (m_1 - m_2)^2$, which gives

$$
\text{KL} = \ln \frac{s_2}{s_1} + \frac{s_1^2 + (m_1 - m_2)^2}{2 s_2^2} - \frac{1}{2}
$$

([Information Theory for AI](3.1-PS4-information-theory-core.md) works this through). With $m_2 = 0$ and $s_2 = 1$ it is the VAE's KL term from [part 2](2.13-RP1-representation-learning-autoencoders-vaes-latent-spaces.md#lesson-vae). DDPM sets $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$. For the top-left pixel at step 500, $s_1^2 = \tilde\beta_{500} = 0.01003$ and $s_2^2 = \beta_{500} = 0.01004$, so the variance terms, $\ln(s_2/s_1) + s_1^2/(2 s_2^2) - \tfrac{1}{2}$, come to 0.0000002. The means, 0.00123 apart (below), add $0.00123^2 \div (2 \times 0.01004) = 0.000075$. With equal variances only the means count, $\text{KL} = (m_1 - m_2)^2 / (2\sigma_t^2)$.

**The weight.** In the noise form the two means differ only through the noise, $m_1 - m_2 = \dfrac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\,(\hat\varepsilon - \varepsilon)$, so for each pixel

$$
\text{KL} = \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\, \alpha_t\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\, (\varepsilon - \hat\varepsilon)^2.
$$

At step 500 the factor in front of the noise error is 0.0105 for the means and 0.0055 for the KL. The predicted noise of the running example below is off by 0.117 in every pixel, so the means differ by $0.0105 \times 0.117 = 0.00123$ and the KL is $0.0055 \times 4 \times 0.117^2 = 0.0003$, four times the 0.000075 above. Dropping the weight and averaging over the pixels leaves the mean squared error on the noise, 0.014 here, which is the loss DDPM trains with. Ho et al. found that dropping the weight gave better images.

#### The step as an input

**Why the step is needed.** The same noisy image means different things at different steps. Read as step 500, the running example's $x_{500} = (0.405, -0.125, 0.895, 0.096)$ could have come from LEFT or RIGHT, with weights 0.6 and 0.4. Read as step 200, where an image keeps more of its signal, the same numbers point to LEFT with weight 0.959, and the best predicted noise changes sign in the first pixel:

| Read as step | Best estimate of the clean image | Best predicted noise |
|---|---|---|
| 200 | (0.959, 0.041, 0.959, 0.041) | (−0.640, −0.270, 0.199, 0.108) |
| 500 | (0.6, 0.4, 0.6, 0.4) | (0.247, −0.247, 0.757, −0.017) |
| 800 | (0.513, 0.487, 0.513, 0.487) | (0.385, −0.144, 0.876, 0.077) |

A network that is not told the step has to give one answer for all three rows.

**A vector for the step.** DDPM gives $t$ to the network as sines and cosines, the way [Build a Transformer](2.30-TF10-transformer-setup.md#lesson-add-position) gives a position. With 4 numbers they are $(\sin t, \cos t, \sin(t/100), \cos(t/100))$: step 500 becomes (−0.468, −0.884, −0.959, 0.284) and step 200 becomes (−0.873, 0.487, 0.909, −0.416). The slow pair changes little between nearby steps, (−0.959, 0.284) at step 500 and (−0.956, 0.293) at step 501.

**Into the network.** A small MLP turns the vector into one number per channel, and every block of the U-Net adds it to its features, so each layer can adjust to the noise level. Later U-Nets predict a scale and a shift per channel from the step instead. Giving $t \div 1000$ as one extra input also works on small problems.

### Running example

With only two training images, the best possible network can be computed exactly: its estimate of the clean image is an average of LEFT and RIGHT, weighted by how likely each is to have produced the noisy image.

**Training.** Take LEFT at step 500 from [Adding noise](#lesson-adding-noise), (0.405, −0.125, 0.895, 0.096). The image is scaled down to 0.28 under noise of 0.96, so even the best network is unsure: it puts weight 0.6 on LEFT and 0.4 on RIGHT, and estimates the clean image as (0.6, 0.4, 0.6, 0.4). The noise it predicts is close to the true noise:

| | Pixel 1 | Pixel 2 | Pixel 3 | Pixel 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| True noise ε | 0.13 | −0.13 | 0.64 | 0.10 |
| Predicted noise | 0.247 | −0.247 | 0.757 | −0.017 |

The loss is the mean squared difference, 0.014.

**Generating.** Two different starts from pure noise, each run for 10 steps:

| Step t | Estimate from start A | Estimate from start B |
|---|---|---|
| 1000 | (0.50, 0.50, 0.50, 0.50) | (0.50, 0.50, 0.50, 0.50) |
| 800 | (0.53, 0.47, 0.53, 0.47) | (0.47, 0.53, 0.47, 0.53) |
| 600 | (0.61, 0.39, 0.61, 0.39) | (0.37, 0.63, 0.37, 0.63) |
| 400 | (0.81, 0.19, 0.81, 0.19) | (0.16, 0.84, 0.16, 0.84) |
| 200 | (1.00, 0.00, 1.00, 0.00) | (0.00, 1.00, 0.00, 1.00) |
| Final image | LEFT | RIGHT |

Start A is (0.19, −0.52, −0.41, −2.44) and start B is (1.05, 1.78, −2.55, −0.14). At first the estimate is a gray blend. As the noise falls, it commits to one image, and different starts end at different images, so the model produces both kinds of image, unlike the collapsed GAN of part 3.

```python
import numpy as np

alpha_bar = np.cumprod(1 - np.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, 1000))
abar = lambda t: 1.0 if t == 0 else alpha_bar[t - 1]
images = np.array([[1.0, 0, 1, 0], [0, 1.0, 0, 1]])   # LEFT, RIGHT

def estimate(x, t):           # the best possible network's clean-image estimate
    d = ((x - np.sqrt(abar(t)) * images) ** 2).sum(axis=1) / (2 * (1 - abar(t)))
    w = np.exp(-(d - d.min()))
    return w @ images / w.sum()

x = np.array([0.19, -0.52, -0.41, -2.44])             # start A: pure noise
for t in range(1000, 0, -100):
    x0 = estimate(x, t)
    eps = (x - np.sqrt(abar(t)) * x0) / np.sqrt(1 - abar(t))
    x = np.sqrt(abar(t - 100)) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - abar(t - 100)) * eps
print(x.round(3))                                      # LEFT
```

### Common questions

**Why predict the noise and not the image?** The two carry the same information, because the formula above turns one into the other. Their numerical weighting across noise levels differs, so parameterization can change optimization even when the underlying quantities determine one another. DDPM found that training on the noise gave better images.

**Why does the first estimate look like a gray blend?** At step 1000 the noisy image says almost nothing about which image it came from, so the best estimate is the average of all of them: (0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5) for LEFT and RIGHT.

**What decides whether the result is LEFT or RIGHT?** The starting noise. Start A leans slightly towards LEFT, the estimate moves that way, and each step makes the lean stronger. A different start gives a different image, which is where the variety comes from.

**Why not finish in one step?** One step from pure noise returns the gray average. The image only becomes sharp because each step works on a less noisy input, where the estimate can commit.

The next section, [Guidance and Stable Diffusion](#lesson-guidance-and-stable-diffusion), chooses which image to make.

## Guidance and Stable Diffusion

### The problem

The model from [Removing noise](#lesson-removing-noise) makes a random image like its training images. Which one it makes depends only on the starting noise: start A ends at LEFT and start B at RIGHT.

A user wants a particular image, described in a prompt such as "a cat on a skateboard", and wants the result to follow the prompt closely whatever the starting noise.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/generative-problem-guidance.svg" alt="Two rows. With no prompt, start A leads to LEFT and start B leads to RIGHT. With the prompt left, start A leads to LEFT and start B must also lead to LEFT.">
Without a prompt the starting noise decides the image. With a prompt, every start should end at the image that was asked for.
</Figure>

There is also a cost problem. Diffusion runs the network once for every step, for dozens of steps, and each run works on the whole image. A 512 × 512 color image is 786,432 numbers, so 50 steps means processing all 786,432 of them 50 times. Generation should be fast enough to run on one GPU.

### Earlier approaches

#### Classifier guidance

Dhariwal and Nichol (2021) steered sampling with a separate classifier, trained on noisy images, that pushed each step toward the requested class. This needed an extra network and worked only for the classes it was trained on, not free text.

#### Diffusion on pixels

Running every step on full-size pixels is slow and memory-hungry. A 512 × 512 color image is 786,432 numbers, and the network processes all of them at every one of dozens of steps.

### Guidance and latent diffusion

**Conditioning.** The prompt is given to the network as an extra input. In Stable Diffusion, a text encoder from CLIP turns the prompt into vectors, and the U-Net reads them through **cross-attention**: attention as in Build a Transformer, with queries from the image and keys and values from the text.

**Classifier-free guidance.** During training, the prompt is sometimes left out, so the same network learns to estimate both with and without a prompt. When sampling, it computes both and pushes past the prompted estimate:

$$
\text{guided} = \text{unprompted} + w \times (\text{prompted} - \text{unprompted}).
$$

For example, an unprompted estimate of 0.28 and a prompted one of 1 differ by 0.72. With $w = 1$ the guided value is 0.28 + 0.72 = 1, just the prompted estimate. With $w = 3$ it is 0.28 + 3 × 0.72 = 2.44.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/generative-guidance.svg" alt="A number line for one pixel. The unprompted estimate is at 0.28 and the prompted estimate, which is also the guided value for w = 1, is at 1. Both lie in a shaded band marking the valid pixel values, 0 to 1. A dashed arrow continues in the same direction past the band to the guided values for w = 3, at 2.44, and for w = 7.5, at 5.68.">
Guidance for the top-left pixel in the running example. Larger $w$ moves further along the line from the unprompted estimate through the prompted one.
</Figure>

**Choosing w.** Larger $w$ follows the prompt more closely, at the cost of variety. Stable Diffusion's default is 7.5. Too large a $w$ gives harsh, oversaturated images.

**Noise as a slope.** For the best network, the predicted noise is a slope: $\hat\varepsilon = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \times$ (slope of $\ln p(x_t)$), where $p(x_t)$ says how likely a noisy image is. At step 500 of start B, the unprompted noise is (1.088, 1.736, −2.512, −0.184), and $-0.960$ times the slope of $\ln p(x_t)$, measured numerically, gives the same four numbers.

**Guidance as reweighting.** By Bayes' rule, $\ln p(x_t \mid \text{prompt}) = \ln p(x_t) + \ln p(\text{prompt} \mid x_t) - \ln p(\text{prompt})$, and the last term does not change with $x_t$. So the prompted minus the unprompted noise is $-\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ times the slope of $\ln p(\text{prompt} \mid x_t)$, the slope of a classifier that comes for free. Guidance with weight $w$ follows $\ln p(x_t) + w \ln p(\text{prompt} \mid x_t)$, which reweights the noisy images by $p(\text{prompt} \mid x_t)^w$, so $w$ above 1 favors images where the prompt is unmistakable. At step 500 of start B, $p(\text{left} \mid x_t) = 0.28$, the unprompted estimate's weight on LEFT. The prompted minus the unprompted noise is the signal factor $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{500}} = 0.280$ from [Adding noise](#lesson-adding-noise), divided by the noise factor $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{500}} = 0.960$, with its sign changed, times the prompted minus the unprompted estimate: $-0.292 \times 0.72 = -0.21$ in the top-left pixel. Written on the noise, guidance is $\hat\varepsilon_\text{unprompted} + w\,(\hat\varepsilon_\text{prompted} - \hat\varepsilon_\text{unprompted})$, the same as guiding the estimates, because the noise is a straight-line function of the estimate.

**Latent diffusion.** Stable Diffusion runs diffusion on the codes of an autoencoder (see [part 2](2.13-RP1-representation-learning-autoencoders-vaes-latent-spaces.md#lesson-autoencoder)), not on pixels. A 512 × 512 × 3 image becomes a 64 × 64 × 4 code: 16,384 numbers instead of 786,432, which is 48 times fewer. Only at the end does the decoder turn the final code into an image.

### Running example

#### Guidance

Start B from [Removing noise](#lesson-removing-noise) ends at RIGHT when there is no prompt. Now ask for "left". At step 500 of sampling, the two estimates are:

| Estimate | Image |
|---|---|
| Unprompted | (0.28, 0.72, 0.28, 0.72) |
| Prompted, "left" | (1, 0, 1, 0) |
| Guided, w = 1 | (1, 0, 1, 0) |
| Guided, w = 3 | (2.44, −1.44, 2.44, −1.44) |
| Guided, w = 7.5 | (5.68, −4.68, 5.68, −4.68) |

With the prompt, start B now ends at LEFT. Larger w pushes each pixel past valid values, which samplers clip back to the valid range. In real images, this shows up as oversaturated colors. Here the prompted estimate is already perfect, so w = 1 is enough. In real models the prompted estimate follows the prompt only loosely, and w above 1 strengthens it.

<ProblemCard id="gen-the-guided-value-at-step-500" />

#### Latent diffusion

The autoencoder of part 2 shows the latent idea at small scale: it turns each 4-pixel image into 1 number, LEFT into 1 and RIGHT into −1, so diffusion could work on 1 number instead of 4.

```python
import numpy as np

unprompted = np.array([0.28, 0.72, 0.28, 0.72])
prompted = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0])            # "left"

for w in (1, 3, 7.5):
    print(w, unprompted + w * (prompted - unprompted))
```

### Common questions

**Does guidance cost anything?** Yes. Every step runs the network twice, once with the prompt and once without, so sampling takes about twice the computation.

**What is a negative prompt?** Text describing what the image should avoid. Its estimate is used in place of the unprompted one, so the guided result is pushed away from it and towards the prompt.

**Why does $w$ above 1 help if $w = 1$ is the prompted estimate?** In real models the prompted estimate follows the prompt only loosely. Going past it, in the direction that separates it from the unprompted estimate, exaggerates what the prompt adds.

**Why run diffusion on codes?** It is 48 times fewer numbers at every step. The autoencoder handles fine detail, and diffusion only has to get the content and layout right.
