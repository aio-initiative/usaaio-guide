## Overview

A multilayer perceptron takes a fixed-size input. A sentence, a time series or a protein is a sequence, with a length that varies and a meaning that depends on order. A **recurrent neural network** reads a sequence one step at a time, carrying a **hidden state** that summarizes everything seen so far, and it uses the same weights at every step. That sharing is what lets one fixed set of parameters handle any length.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/rnn-unrolled.svg" alt="A chain of boxes h0, h1, h2, h3 joined left to right by arrows labeled W_hh, with an input x_t feeding each hidden box from below through W_xh and an output y_t leaving it above through W_hy.">
An RNN unrolled over three steps. One hidden state is passed along, and the three weight matrices are the same at every step.
</Figure>

Recurrent networks are not on the Round 1 syllabus. They are here because two things went wrong with them: gradients that vanish across long spans, and a fixed-size summary of the whole input. [Attention](2.34-TF14-transformer-qkv.md#lesson-q-k-v) and the Transformer were built to fix both, and that part of the syllabus makes more sense with the failure in view.

## Example: a recurrence by hand

A recurrent network with a one-dimensional hidden state computes $h_t = \tanh(w_h h_{t-1} + w_x x_t)$ with $w_h = 0.5$, $w_x = 1$ and $h_0 = 0$. It reads the sequence $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 0$, $x_3 = -1$.

**(a)** Compute $h_1$, $h_2$ and $h_3$.

**(b)** Compute $\partial h_3 / \partial h_0$ as a product of three local derivatives, one per step.

The forward pass is three applications of one formula. The derivative in part (b) is the quantity that decides whether the network can learn anything about the start of a sequence from a loss at its end, and the explanation computes both.

## Explanation

### The recurrence

At every step the network combines the previous hidden state with the current input, squashes the result with tanh, and reads an output off the new state:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{h}_t &= \tanh\!\left(W_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + W_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_h\right), \\
\mathbf{y}_t &= W_{hy}\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_y .
\end{aligned}
$$

A formula that gives each value from the one before it is called a **recurrence**, which is where the network gets its name. $W_{hh}$, $W_{xh}$ and $W_{hy}$ are the same matrices at every step. For the example, with one hidden unit and no bias,

$$
\begin{aligned}
h_1 &= \tanh(0.5 \cdot 0 + 1) = 0.7616, \\
h_2 &= \tanh(0.5 \cdot 0.7616 + 0) = 0.3634, \\
h_3 &= \tanh(0.5 \cdot 0.3634 - 1) = -0.6741.
\end{aligned}
$$

That is part (a). Each state carries a trace of every earlier input, and the trace fades: $x_1 = 1$ is still visible in $h_3$ only through the $0.5 \cdot 0.3634$ term.

### Backpropagation through time

Unrolled over $T$ steps, an RNN is a $T$-layer network whose layers share their weights, so training is ordinary backpropagation on that unrolled graph. It is called **backpropagation through time**, or BPTT. Two things differ from an MLP. A hidden state reaches the loss along two routes, through its own output $\mathbf{y}_t$ and through the next state $\mathbf{h}_{t+1}$, so its gradient is the sum of the two. And because each weight matrix is used at every step, its gradient is a sum of one term per step:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{hh}} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{\delta}_t\, \mathbf{h}_{t-1}^{\!\top} .
$$

Here $\boldsymbol{\delta}_t = \partial L / \partial \mathbf{z}_t$ is the error at step $t$, as in [Backpropagation by Hand](2.1-MA4-backpropagation-mechanics.md), with $\mathbf{z}_t$ the pre-activation at that step.

<ProblemCard id="rnn-loss-at-every-step" />

The backward pass needs every hidden state, so its memory grows with $T$. Long streams are trained with **truncated BPTT**: run the forward pass through the whole stream, but cut the backward pass every $k$ steps. No gradient then connects a loss to an input more than $k$ steps back. In code the cut is `h = h.detach()` between chunks.

<ProblemCard id="rnn-truncation-chunk-reach" />

### Why the gradients vanish

How much a loss at step $T$ can change the state at an earlier step $t$ is a product of one factor per step in between:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}
= \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{T-1}} \cdots
\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t+1}}{\partial \mathbf{h}_t} .
$$

Each factor is the tanh slope times the recurrent weight. For the example that is the number $(1 - h_i^2)\, w_h$, so

$$
\frac{\partial h_3}{\partial h_0} = (0.21)(0.434)(0.273) = 0.0249.
$$

That is part (b). Three steps in, a change at the start reaches the end at 2.5% of its size. The tanh slope is at most 1 and falls toward 0 as the hidden state nears $\pm 1$. With a weight below 1 every factor is below 1, and a product of many of them shrinks by a constant factor per step.

With a vector state each factor is a matrix of derivatives, called a **Jacobian**: the slopes times $W_{hh}$. The same thing happens. Suppose $W_{hh}$ can stretch a vector to at most 0.9 times its length. That largest stretch is the matrix's largest **singular value**, $\sigma_{\max}$, and it bounds the size of the whole product:

$$
\left\lVert \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_t} \right\rVert
\le \sigma_{\max}(W_{hh})^{\,T - t} .
$$

The gradient across 100 steps is then at most $0.9^{100} = 2.7 \times 10^{-5}$ of its size, and the measurement at the end of the module finds it far smaller than that. A plain RNN still trains, on patterns that span a few steps. The gradient from two steps back is healthy and the one from fifty is lost in rounding, so the network learns to look back perhaps ten to twenty steps and no further.

Factors above 1 make the product explode instead. That failure is loud, the loss goes to `nan`, and **gradient clipping** fixes it: when the length of the gradient exceeds a threshold $\tau$, scale the whole gradient down to length $\tau$. In PyTorch that is one line, `torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)`, placed between `loss.backward()` and `optimizer.step()`. Vanishing has no such fix, because there is no signal left to rescale. It takes a change of architecture. **Orthogonal initialization** of $W_{hh}$ sets $\sigma_{\max}(W_{hh})$ to exactly 1, placing the model on the stable knife-edge at the start. It delays the problem rather than solving it, since training moves the weights, but it is a meaningful improvement. PyTorch does not do it by default, so set it yourself: `nn.init.orthogonal_(rnn.weight_hh_l0)`.

### LSTM

The **long short-term memory** cell adds a second state, the **cell** $\mathbf{c}_t$, with a path through time that is not multiplied by a weight matrix. Three **gates** control it. A gate is a vector of sigmoid outputs, numbers between 0 and 1, that multiplies another vector entry by entry and so decides how much of each entry gets through. Write $[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t]$ for the previous state and the input joined into one vector, and $\odot$ for entry-by-entry multiplication:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma(W_f [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f), \\
\mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma(W_i [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i), \\
\mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma(W_o [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o), \\
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t &= \tanh(W_c [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_c), \\
\mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t, \\
\mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

Read each gate by what it multiplies. The **forget** gate $\mathbf{f}_t$ scales the old cell state, so 1 keeps a memory and 0 erases it. The **input** gate $\mathbf{i}_t$ scales how much of the candidate $\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t$ is written. The **output** gate $\mathbf{o}_t$ scales how much of the cell the rest of the network sees at this step.

The fifth line is the one that matters for gradients. Each entry of the new cell is the same entry of the old cell times its forget gate, plus what is written. So along the cell path the factor from one step to the next is the forget gate itself. Differentiating the additive term $\mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1}$ alone gives

$$
\frac{\partial (\mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1})}{\partial \mathbf{c}_{t-1}} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{f}_t),
$$

the matrix with $\mathbf{f}_t$ on its diagonal. No weight matrix and no tanh slope appear in it. When the forget gate sits near 1, the product over many steps stays near 1 and a gradient crosses hundreds of steps at nearly full size. The network can still learn to forget, by driving $\mathbf{f}_t$ toward 0, so keeping a memory is something the model chooses, not something the design rules out. For that reason the forget gate's bias is usually initialized positive in the literature, so memory is kept by default. PyTorch does not do this for you: `nn.LSTM` draws every bias from $\mathcal{U}(-1/\sqrt{H},\, 1/\sqrt{H})$, so set the forget bias yourself if you want memory kept by default. A residual connection gives a deep network the same additive path.

### GRU

The **gated recurrent unit** keeps the idea with two gates and no separate cell:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{z}_t &= \sigma(W_z [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_z), \\
\mathbf{r}_t &= \sigma(W_r [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_r), \\
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t &= \tanh(W [\mathbf{r}_t \odot \mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}), \\
\mathbf{h}_t &= (1 - \mathbf{z}_t) \odot \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{z}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t .
\end{aligned}
$$

The **reset** gate $\mathbf{r}_t$ decides how much of the past enters the candidate. The **update** gate $\mathbf{z}_t$ blends the old state with the candidate: 0 keeps the old state and 1 replaces it. That is the work the LSTM splits between its forget and input gates. Three weight blocks instead of four means about three quarters of the parameters, and in practice the two perform about the same. PyTorch's `nn.GRU` writes the update with $\mathbf{z}_t$ as the fraction kept, which is the same update with $\mathbf{z}_t$ and $1 - \mathbf{z}_t$ swapped. It also applies the reset gate after multiplying $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ by its weights, not before.

### From recurrence to attention

Stacked into an encoder and a decoder, recurrent networks were the standard for translation. The encoder reads the source sentence and the decoder writes the translation. They had one built-in flaw: the whole source sentence had to pass through the encoder's final state, one fixed-size vector, whatever the sentence's length. Attention removed that by letting the decoder look at every encoder state. The recurrence itself was then the remaining cost, $T$ steps that have to run one after another, and the Transformer dropped it.

## Implementation

### nn.RNN

PyTorch's recurrent layers take a whole batch of sequences at once. With `batch_first=True` the input is `(batch, steps, features)`, the output holds the hidden state at every step, and `h_n` is the final state on its own:

```python
import torch
from torch import nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
rnn = nn.RNN(input_size=3, hidden_size=4, batch_first=True)
x = torch.randn(2, 5, 3)                 # 2 sequences, 5 steps, 3 features each
out, h_n = rnn(x)
print(out.shape, h_n.shape)
for name, p in rnn.named_parameters():
    print(name, tuple(p.shape))
print(sum(p.numel() for p in rnn.parameters()))
```

```output
torch.Size([2, 5, 4]) torch.Size([1, 2, 4])
weight_ih_l0 (4, 3)
weight_hh_l0 (4, 4)
bias_ih_l0 (4,)
bias_hh_l0 (4,)
36
```

The leading 1 in `h_n` counts layers. The four parameter tensors are $W_{xh}$, $W_{hh}$ and two biases, $4 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot 4 + 4 + 4 = 36$ numbers, however long the sequence. The layer is the recurrence, and five lines reproduce it:

```python
W_xh, W_hh, b_xh, b_hh = [p.detach() for p in rnn.parameters()]
h = torch.zeros(2, 4)                    # h_0 for each sequence in the batch
states = []
for t in range(5):
    h = torch.tanh(x[:, t] @ W_xh.T + b_xh + h @ W_hh.T + b_hh)
    states.append(h)
print(torch.allclose(torch.stack(states, dim=1), out, atol=1e-6), torch.allclose(h, h_n[0], atol=1e-6))
```

The per-step states match `out` and the last one matches `h_n`:

```output
True True
```

### nn.LSTM and nn.GRU

Same input, same output shape, and the LSTM also returns its cell state. The parameter counts are four and three times the RNN's, one block per gate or candidate:

```python
lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=3, hidden_size=4, batch_first=True)
out, (h_n, c_n) = lstm(x)
print(out.shape, h_n.shape, c_n.shape, sum(p.numel() for p in lstm.parameters()))
gru = nn.GRU(input_size=3, hidden_size=4, batch_first=True)
out, h_n = gru(x)
print(out.shape, h_n.shape, sum(p.numel() for p in gru.parameters()))
```

$4 \times 36 = 144$ and $3 \times 36 = 108$:

```output
torch.Size([2, 5, 4]) torch.Size([1, 2, 4]) torch.Size([1, 2, 4]) 144
torch.Size([2, 5, 4]) torch.Size([1, 2, 4]) 108
```

For classification of a whole sequence, feed `h_n[0]` (or the LSTM's) to a linear layer. For a prediction at every step, feed `out`.

Three arguments come up in practice. `batch_first` defaults to `False`, which expects `(steps, batch, features)`, and batch-first data fed to it runs without an error on scrambled sequences. `num_layers=2` stacks a second recurrent layer on the outputs of the first. `bidirectional=True` adds a right-to-left pass and joins the two, so `out` has `2 * hidden_size` features. A bidirectional layer cannot generate, since its right-to-left pass reads tokens that have not been written yet. Sequences of different lengths are padded to one length, and `pack_padded_sequence` tells the layer where each one ends.

### Measuring the vanishing

The bound says the gradient across a span shrinks at least as fast as $\sigma_{\max}(W_{hh})^{\text{span}}$. Multiplying the per-step Jacobians out, with $W_{hh}$ scaled so that $\sigma_{\max} = 0.9$, shows how much faster it goes:

```python
import numpy as np

def jacobian_product_norm(W, steps, seed=0):
    """||prod diag(tanh'(z)) W|| across `steps` timesteps."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    d = W.shape[0]
    J = np.eye(d)
    h = rng.normal(size=d) * 0.1
    for _ in range(steps):
        z = W @ h
        h = np.tanh(z)
        J = (np.diag(1 - np.tanh(z) ** 2) @ W) @ J
    return np.linalg.norm(J, 2)

d = 32
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
A = rng.normal(size=(d, d))
W = 0.9 * A / np.linalg.norm(A, 2)        # force sigma_max(W) = 0.9

for steps in (10, 25, 50, 100):
    print(f"{steps:3d} steps: {jacobian_product_norm(W, steps):.3e}")
print("bound 0.9^100 =", f"{0.9 ** 100:.3e}")

rho = np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(W)).max()
print(f"W^100 alone, no tanh at all: {np.linalg.norm(np.linalg.matrix_power(W, 100), 2):.3e}")
print(f"spectral radius {rho:.3f}, and {rho:.3f}^100 = {rho ** 100:.1e}")
```

```output
 10 steps: 3.005e-03
 25 steps: 2.467e-07
 50 steps: 3.810e-14
100 steps: 9.090e-28
bound 0.9^100 = 2.656e-05
W^100 alone, no tanh at all: 9.099e-28
spectral radius 0.534, and 0.534^100 = 5.7e-28
```

By 50 steps the gradient is $10^{-14}$, below what float32 can add to a gradient from recent steps. The measured decay is far faster than the bound. The largest singular value is the most a matrix can stretch a vector in one multiplication, and no vector gets that stretch every time. Over many multiplications the rate that counts is the size of the matrix's largest eigenvalue, called its **spectral radius**. Here that is 0.534, well below 0.9.
