> **Goal:** A neural operator learns the solution operator mapping a coefficient function or initial condition to the solution.
> **Stage:** Camp, Advanced preparation.

## Operator learning

A PINN learns **one solution** to **one problem instance**, so changing the initial condition means retraining. A **neural operator** learns the solution *operator* $\mathcal{G}: a \mapsto u$ mapping a coefficient function or initial condition to the solution.

Formally, the input and output are *functions*, $a \in \mathcal{A}$, $u\in\mathcal{U}$, both infinite-dimensional, so this is regression between function spaces rather than between vectors. That framing is what distinguishes an operator from an ordinary network trained on discretized fields, and it is where the resolution-transfer property comes from.

Training is supervised: generate pairs $(a_i, u_i)$ with a classical solver, and fit. The data cost is real and is the method's main drawback.

## DeepONet

DeepONet uses a branch network encoding the input function at sensor locations and a trunk network encoding the query point, combined by an inner product:

$$
\mathcal{G}(a)(y) \approx \sum_{k=1}^{p}b_k(a)\,t_k(y).
$$

The branch network sees $a$ sampled at a fixed set of $m$ sensor locations, and the trunk sees a single query point $y$ at which the solution is wanted. Their inner product gives the value there, so the output can be evaluated at **any** $y$ without retraining: the trunk is a learned basis and the branch supplies the coefficients.

**The theorem behind it.** The universal approximation theorem for operators (Chen and Chen, 1995) says exactly this form, a finite sum of products of a function of $a$ and a function of $y$, can approximate any continuous nonlinear operator to arbitrary accuracy. DeepONet is that construction with both factors parameterized by networks, which is why the architecture looks arbitrary and is not.

The sensor locations are fixed at training time, so the *input* discretization is not free, and only the output query points are.

## Fourier Neural Operator

FNO parameterizes the operator in Fourier space:

$$
(\mathcal{K}v)(x) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(R\cdot\mathcal{F}(v)\big)(x),
$$

learning a complex multiplier $R$ on the lowest $k$ modes. Truncating to $k$ modes makes it cheap and acts as a smoothness prior.

A full FNO layer is $v \mapsto \sigma(\mathcal{K}v + Wv)$: a global convolution in the frequency domain plus a pointwise linear term, which supplies the high-frequency content the truncation removed. Stacking four such layers is the standard architecture.

**Why the frequency domain.** By the convolution theorem, multiplication in Fourier space is convolution in real space with a *global* kernel: every point influences every other, in $O(n\log n)$ via the FFT rather than the $O(n^2)$ a dense global convolution would cost. That is the same trick that makes spectral PDE solvers fast, imported into a learned model.

**Truncation as a prior.** Keeping only the lowest $k$ modes (typically 12–32) means the learned operator cannot represent structure finer than that scale. For the smooth solutions of diffusion-dominated PDEs this is exactly right. For shocks and turbulence it is a real limitation, and it is why FNO struggles on hyperbolic problems.

## Discretization invariance

Because the operator is defined in function space, FNO is **discretization-invariant**: train on a coarse grid and evaluate on a fine one.

The mechanism is that the learned parameters $R$ are indexed by *frequency*, not by grid position. A $64\times64$ grid and a $256\times256$ grid share their low frequencies, so the same $R$ applies: the FFT simply returns more modes, and the ones beyond $k$ are discarded either way.

An ordinary CNN or MLP trained on discretized fields has no such property: its weights are indexed by pixel, so a change of resolution changes what every weight means. Retraining is required, and this is precisely the difference between learning an operator and learning a function of a particular discretization.

The practical payoff is **zero-shot super-resolution**: train on cheap coarse simulations and evaluate at a resolution you never simulated. The caveat is that accuracy at the finer resolution is bounded by what the retained modes can represent: you get the coarse solution evaluated finely, not new fine-scale physics.

## The trade

Neural operators require training data, solved instances from a classical solver, where a PINN needs none. But once trained, inference is a single forward pass, typically $10^3$–$10^5$× faster than the solver that generated the training data.

That amortization is the point: worth it if you will solve the same family of problems many times (uncertainty quantification, design optimization, real-time control), not worth it for a one-off.

The break-even calculation is straightforward and worth doing before committing: if the solver costs $T_s$ per instance, generating $N_{\text{train}}$ examples costs $N_{\text{train}}T_s$, and the operator repays that after $N_{\text{train}}$ inferences (ignoring training compute, which is usually smaller than the data generation). With $N_{\text{train}} = 1000$ and a downstream task requiring $10^6$ solves, the amortization is overwhelming, and with 10 solves it is absurd.

## Variants and limits

**Physics-informed neural operators** combine both, adding a PDE residual to the operator's training loss, which reduces the data requirement, sometimes to zero for the residual-only case, at the cost of inheriting the PINN's optimization difficulties.

**Graph neural operators** replace the FFT with message passing on a mesh, which handles irregular geometries the FNO's uniform grid cannot ([Graph Neural Networks](/module/?id=graph-neural-networks)). **Transformer-based operators** use attention as the global kernel, trading the FFT's speed for flexibility.

**The honest limits.** Neural operators interpolate within the distribution of training instances and extrapolate poorly outside it: a model trained on Reynolds numbers up to 1000 should not be trusted at 5000. They provide no error bound, unlike a classical solver with a convergence guarantee. And they inherit the resolution limits of their truncation, so fine-scale phenomena absent from the training data will be absent from the predictions.

## From Scratch

```python
import numpy as np


def deeponet(branch_out, trunk_out):
    """G(a)(y) = sum_k b_k(a) t_k(y). The trunk is a LEARNED BASIS over query
    points, and the branch supplies its coefficients from the input function."""
    return branch_out @ trunk_out.T                  # (batch_a, n_query)


def sample_at_sensors(a_fn, sensors):
    """DeepONet's input discretization is FIXED at the sensor locations --
    only the OUTPUT query points are free."""
    return np.array([a_fn(s) for s in sensors])


def spectral_conv_1d(v, R, k_modes):
    """FNO layer core: FFT, multiply the lowest k modes by learned complex
    weights, inverse FFT. Multiplication in frequency = GLOBAL convolution in
    real space, at O(n log n) instead of O(n^2)."""
    v_hat = np.fft.rfft(v, axis=0)
    out_hat = np.zeros_like(v_hat)
    out_hat[:k_modes] = v_hat[:k_modes] * R[:k_modes]     # truncation = smoothness prior
    return np.fft.irfft(out_hat, n=v.shape[0], axis=0)


def fno_layer(v, R, W, k_modes, activation=np.tanh):
    """sigma(K v + W v): the spectral term is global and smooth, the pointwise
    linear term restores the high frequencies the truncation removed."""
    return activation(spectral_conv_1d(v, R, k_modes) + v @ W)


def discretization_invariance_check(v_coarse, v_fine, R, k_modes):
    """The SAME R applies at both resolutions, because it is indexed by
    FREQUENCY, not by grid position. A CNN's weights are indexed by pixel and
    have no such property."""
    return (spectral_conv_1d(v_coarse, R, k_modes),
            spectral_conv_1d(v_fine, R, k_modes))


def mode_truncation_error(u, k_modes):
    """What the retained modes cannot represent. Small for diffusion-dominated
    problems, large for shocks -- which is FNO's main limitation."""
    u_hat = np.fft.rfft(u)
    kept = np.zeros_like(u_hat)
    kept[:k_modes] = u_hat[:k_modes]
    return float(np.linalg.norm(u - np.fft.irfft(kept, n=len(u))) / np.linalg.norm(u))


def amortization_breakeven(solver_seconds, operator_seconds, n_train, train_hours=0.0):
    """Data generation costs n_train solver runs. The operator repays that
    after roughly n_train inferences -- do this sum BEFORE committing."""
    setup = n_train * solver_seconds + train_hours * 3600
    per_query_saving = solver_seconds - operator_seconds
    return {"setup_seconds": setup,
            "breakeven_queries": setup / max(per_query_saving, 1e-12),
            "speedup": solver_seconds / max(operator_seconds, 1e-12)}


def relative_l2(pred, true):
    """The standard operator-learning metric: relative L2 over the field,
    averaged across test instances."""
    return float(np.mean(np.linalg.norm(pred - true, axis=-1)
                         / np.linalg.norm(true, axis=-1)))


def physics_informed_operator_loss(pred, true, residual_fn, lam=1.0):
    """Adding the PDE residual reduces the data requirement -- and reintroduces
    the PINN's weighting difficulties."""
    return relative_l2(pred, true) + lam * float(np.mean(residual_fn(pred) ** 2))
```

## Using the Library

```python
import torch, torch.nn as nn


class SpectralConv1d(nn.Module):
    """The FNO core. Weights are indexed by MODE, which is what makes the
    model discretization-invariant."""

    def __init__(self, in_ch, out_ch, modes):
        super().__init__()
        self.modes = modes
        scale = 1 / (in_ch * out_ch)
        self.weights = nn.Parameter(
            scale * torch.rand(in_ch, out_ch, modes, dtype=torch.cfloat))

    def forward(self, x):                       # x: (B, C, N)
        x_ft = torch.fft.rfft(x)
        out_ft = torch.zeros(x.size(0), self.weights.size(1), x_ft.size(-1),
                             dtype=torch.cfloat, device=x.device)
        out_ft[..., :self.modes] = torch.einsum(
            "bix,iox->box", x_ft[..., :self.modes], self.weights)
        return torch.fft.irfft(out_ft, n=x.size(-1))


# Train at 64 points, evaluate at 256 -- no retraining, no interpolation.
model = SpectralConv1d(64, 64, modes=16)
model(torch.randn(8, 64, 64)).shape             # trained resolution
model(torch.randn(8, 64, 256)).shape            # zero-shot super-resolution

# Libraries: neuraloperator (FNO, TFNO), deepxde (DeepONet and PINNs together).
#   pip install neuraloperator deepxde
```

## Worked Example

**Part A: the amortization, computed.** A CFD solver takes 30 seconds per instance. An FNO takes 5 ms. Training data: 1000 solved instances.

$$
\text{data generation} = 1000\times30\text{ s} = 8.3\text{ hours}.
$$
$$
\text{training} \approx 2\text{ hours on one GPU}.
$$
$$
\text{setup total} \approx 10.3\text{ hours}.
$$

Each subsequent query saves $30 - 0.005 = 29.995$ s, so the break-even is

$$
\frac{10.3\times3600}{29.995} = 1236 \text{ queries}.
$$

| downstream task | queries needed | verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| one design evaluation | 1 | absurd |
| a parameter sweep | 200 | not worth it |
| uncertainty quantification (Monte Carlo) | $10^5$ | 80× saving |
| real-time control at 10 Hz | $8.64\times10^5$/day | the only option |

The speedup is 6000×, but it only counts after 1236 uses. That sum is the first thing to do.

**Part B: DeepONet's factorization, in miniature.** Take $p = 3$ basis functions. The trunk network learns $t(y) = (\sin\pi y,\ \sin2\pi y,\ \sin3\pi y)$. Say it rediscovers the Fourier basis, which for a heat equation on $[0,1]$ with Dirichlet boundaries it plausibly would.

Given an initial condition $a(x) = 2\sin\pi x - 0.5\sin3\pi x$ sampled at the sensors, the branch network must output the coefficients that produce the solution at the queried time. For the heat equation with $\alpha=0.1$ at $t=1$, the exact solution is

$$
u(x,1) = 2e^{-0.1\pi^2}\sin\pi x - 0.5e^{-0.9\pi^2}\sin3\pi x = 0.745\sin\pi x - 0.0000694\sin3\pi x.
$$

So the branch should output $b \approx (0.745,\ 0,\ -0.0000694)$. The operator has been factored into "which basis functions" (trunk, independent of the input) and "how much of each" (branch, independent of the query point), which is exactly the Chen and Chen construction, and it is why a finite sum of such products suffices.

**Part C: FNO's spectral layer, step by step.** A 1-D field on 64 points, keeping 16 modes.

1. `rfft` gives 33 complex coefficients (modes 0 to 32).
2. Multiply modes 0–15 by the learned complex weights $R$ and zero the rest.
3. `irfft` back to 64 points.

The parameter count is $16$ complex weights per input–output channel pair, so for 64 channels it is $64\times64\times16 = 65{,}536$ complex numbers, or 131k real parameters per layer. Compare a dense global convolution on 64 points: $64\times64\times64 = 262{,}144$ real weights. The frequency parameterization is 2× smaller and has global reach.

**Part D: discretization invariance, verified.** Train on a 64-point grid. At evaluation on 256 points:

- `rfft` now returns 129 modes instead of 33.
- The learned $R$ still applies to modes 0–15, which mean the same thing physically: mode 3 is three oscillations across the domain, regardless of how finely it is sampled.
- Modes 16–128 are zeroed, as they were in training.
- `irfft` returns 256 points.

**Nothing needed to change.** Compare a CNN with a $5\times1$ kernel: at $4\times$ resolution the same kernel covers a quarter of the physical distance, so every learned filter now means something different and the model is simply wrong.

The caveat: the 256-point output contains no structure above mode 15, so it is the coarse solution evaluated finely. If the true fine-grid solution has content at mode 40, the model cannot produce it.

**Part E: the truncation, and where it fails.** Typical relative $L^2$ error from keeping only the lowest 16 modes (order-of-magnitude, from standard test fields):

| solution | truncation error |
| --- | --- |
| diffused Gaussian ($t$ large) | 0.02% |
| smooth wave (band-limited below mode 16) | ~0% |
| Burgers with a forming shock | 14% |
| turbulent field | 38% |

The first two are what FNO was demonstrated on and where it excels. The last two are where the smoothness prior becomes a wall: a shock is a discontinuity, and a discontinuity has energy at every frequency, so no finite mode truncation represents it. That is a structural limitation of the architecture rather than a training problem, and it is why hyperbolic PDEs remain hard for this family.

**Part F: operator versus PINN, on the same problem.** Solve the 1-D heat equation for 500 different initial conditions.

| | PINN | FNO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| training data | none | 1000 solved instances |
| setup cost | none | 8.3 h solver + 2 h training |
| cost per new instance | ~4 min (retrain) | 5 ms |
| 500 instances, total | 33 hours | 10.3 hours + 2.5 s |
| accuracy | $10^{-3}$ relative | $10^{-2}$ relative |

The FNO wins on time by a factor of three at 500 instances and by a factor of more than 600 at $10^5$. The PINN wins on accuracy and needs no solver at all. The choice is entirely about how many instances you have, which is the honest summary of when operator learning is worth its data cost.

## Intuition

A neural operator learns one mapping across varying coefficients, forcing terms, or boundary conditions. Its training cost may be amortized over many future solves. Claims of discretization transfer require care: input representation, quadrature, Fourier truncation, and training resolutions still impose numerical choices.
