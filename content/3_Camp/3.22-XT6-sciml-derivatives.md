## The problem

The residual of Step 1 is $dy/dt + y$. The network of Step 2 gives $y(t)$. The other half, $dy/dt$, is the slope of the network's output as its input $t$ changes, and it is needed at every chosen point.

For example, the slope of $1 - t$ is −1, which can be read off by hand. The network has 13 weights and no such shortcut. The residual at $t = 1$ needs its slope there as a number, which for the untrained network turns out to be 0.0538.

The obvious way to get a slope is to measure it: evaluate the network at $t$ and at $t + h$, and divide the difference by $h$. The result depends on the step $h$, and no step is right:

From the right, the error falls steadily as h shrinks, to about 0.000000001 near h = 0.0000001. For smaller steps it rises again, jaggedly, to about 0.001 at the smallest step. The right side is labeled step too large: the line cuts across the curve. The left side is labeled step too small: rounding errors.">
The error of a measured slope for the untrained network. It is smallest for a middling step and grows on both sides.
</Figure>

A large step measures the slope of a line that cuts across the curve. A very small step subtracts two nearly equal numbers, and rounding errors take over. The slope has to be accurate, because the whole loss is built from it. Training will also need to know how the slope changes when the weights change.

## Earlier approaches

### Finite differences

Estimate the slope from two nearby values: (y(t + h) − y(t)) ÷ h. For $e^{-t}$ at t = 1 with h = 0.1, this gives −0.350 against the true −0.368 (the figure below uses the larger step h = 0.5, so the gap between the line and the curve is visible). A smaller h is more accurate, until h is so small that rounding errors in the computer's arithmetic take over.

### Symbolic differentiation

Computer algebra systems differentiate a formula exactly, by the rules of calculus. For a deep network the formula is enormous, and its derivative is larger still.

## Automatic differentiation

**The idea.** **Automatic differentiation** applies the chain rule to each operation as the network computes, which gives exact derivatives, apart from ordinary rounding. It is the same machinery as backpropagation, applied to the input $t$ instead of the weights.

**For this network.** The derivative of $\tanh(u)$ is $1 - \tanh^2(u)$, so by the chain rule each hidden unit $\tanh(w t + b)$ has slope $w \, (1 - \tanh^2(w t + b))$. The slope of the whole network is the weighted sum of those:

$$
\frac{dy}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^{4} v_i \, w_i \, \big(1 - \tanh^2(w_i t + b_i)\big).
$$

For example, a unit with $w = 1$ and $b = 0$ at $t = 1$ has $\tanh(1) = 0.7616$, so its slope is $1 - 0.7616^2 = 0.420$.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-finite-difference.svg" alt="A plot of e to the minus t near t = 1. A solid straight line touches the curve at t = 1. Its slope, the true slope, is -0.368. A dashed line passes through the curve at t = 1 and at t = 1.5. Its slope, the finite difference with h = 0.5, is -0.289, less steep than the true slope.">
A finite difference measures the slope of a line through two points of the curve. The chain rule gives the slope at the point itself.
</Figure>

**In code.** In PyTorch it is one call, `torch.autograd.grad(y, t, torch.ones_like(y), create_graph=True)[0]`. The `torch.ones_like(y)` gives the slope at every point of `t` at once, `[0]` takes it out of the tuple the call returns, and `create_graph=True` keeps it differentiable so that the loss can later be differentiated with respect to the weights. [Step 4](3.23-XT7-sciml-physics-loss.md) runs it on the whole loss.

### Second derivatives

**By the chain rule.** The equation in this chapter needs only $dy/dt$, but many equations in science also contain a second derivative. Differentiating each unit's slope once more, and writing $a_i = \tanh(w_i t + b_i)$ for the output of unit $i$, gives

$$
\frac{d^2y}{dt^2} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^{4} v_i \, w_i^2 \, a_i \, (1 - a_i^2).
$$

For a single unit with $v = 1$, $w = 1$ and $b = 0$ at $t = 1$, this is $-2 \times 0.762 \times 0.420 = -0.640$. For the untrained network at $t = 1$ it is 0.040426. In PyTorch it takes a second call of `torch.autograd.grad`, this time on the slope, which works only because the first call used `create_graph=True`.

**By finite differences.** The **second difference** estimates it from three nearby values:

$$
\frac{d^2y}{dt^2} \approx \frac{y(t + h) - 2\,y(t) + y(t - h)}{h^2}.
$$

It divides by $h^2$ instead of $h$, so rounding errors take over at a much larger step. For the untrained network at $t = 1$:

| h | Second difference | Error |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.040466 | 0.00004 |
| 0.001 | 0.040426 | 0.000000004 |
| 0.000001 | 0.040412 | 0.000014 |
| 0.00000001 | 0 | 0.040426 |

The **two-sided slope** $(y(t + h) - y(t - h)) \div 2h$ uses the same two outer points but divides by $2h$. At $h = 0.000001$ its error is 0.0000000001, while the second difference is already off by 0.000014. At $h = 0.00000001$ the change the second difference measures is smaller than the rounding in $y$, and it comes out as 0.

## Running example

For the untrained network from Step 2 at t = 1, the chain rule gives dy/dt = 0.053829. Finite differences with different step sizes:

| h | Finite difference | Error |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.055771 | 0.0019 |
| 0.001 | 0.053850 | 0.00002 |
| 0.000001 | 0.053829 | 0.00000002 |
| 0.000000000001 | 0.053735 | 0.000095 |

The error shrinks as h shrinks, then grows again at the smallest h, where rounding takes over. The chain rule needs no h at all.

```python
import numpy as np

p = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 1, 13)
w, b, v, c = p[0:4], p[4:8], p[8:12], p[12]
net = lambda t: np.tanh(w * t + b) @ v + c

slope = (w * (1 - np.tanh(w * 1.0 + b) ** 2)) @ v      # chain rule at t = 1
print(round(slope, 6))                                   # 0.053829
for h in (0.1, 1e-3, 1e-6, 1e-12):
    print(h, (net(1.0 + h) - net(1.0)) / h)
```

## Common questions

**Why not use finite differences in training?** They need a step $h$, and no choice is right: 0.1 gives an error of 0.0019 and $10^{-12}$ gives 0.000095 from rounding. Automatic differentiation has no $h$, and its result can itself be differentiated with respect to the weights, which training needs.

**Why is the second difference more accurate than the slope's estimate at h = 0.1?** Its three points sit on both sides of $t$, so the largest errors from the two sides cancel, and what is left shrinks like $h^2$ rather than $h$: an error of 0.00004 against 0.0019 for the one-sided estimate in the table above. The two-sided slope gains in the same way, which is why the figure compares the second difference with it.

**Is this the same as backpropagation?** It is the same chain rule. Backpropagation asks how the loss changes with each weight, and here the question is how the output changes with the input $t$.
