## Derivatives

### The problem

The residual from part 1 is $dy/dt + y$. The network from part 1 gives $y(t)$. The other half, $dy/dt$, is the slope of the network's output as its input $t$ changes, and it is needed at every chosen point.

For example, the slope of $1 - t$ is −1, which can be read off by hand. The network has 13 weights and no such shortcut. The residual at $t = 1$ needs its slope there as a number, which for the untrained network turns out to be 0.0538.

The obvious way to get a slope is to measure it: evaluate the network at $t$ and at $t + h$, and divide the difference by $h$. The result depends on the step $h$, and no step is right:

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-problem-derivatives.svg" alt="A plot of the error of the measured slope at t = 1 against the step h, both on logarithmic scales. From the right, the error falls steadily as h shrinks, to about 0.000000001 near h = 0.0000001. For smaller steps it rises again, jaggedly, to about 0.001 at the smallest step. The right side is labeled step too large: the line cuts across the curve. The left side is labeled step too small: rounding errors.">
The error of a measured slope for the untrained network. It is smallest for a middling step and grows on both sides.
</Figure>

A large step measures the slope of a line that cuts across the curve. A very small step subtracts two nearly equal numbers, and rounding errors take over. The slope has to be accurate, because the whole loss is built from it. Training will also need to know how the slope changes when the weights change.

### Earlier approaches

#### Finite differences

Estimate the slope from two nearby values: (y(t + h) − y(t)) ÷ h. For $e^{-t}$ at t = 1 with h = 0.1, this gives −0.350 against the true −0.368 (the figure below uses the larger step h = 0.5, so the gap between the line and the curve is visible). A smaller h is more accurate, until h is so small that rounding errors in the computer's arithmetic take over.

#### Symbolic differentiation

Computer algebra systems differentiate a formula exactly, by the rules of calculus. For a deep network the formula is enormous, and its derivative is larger still.

### Automatic differentiation

**The idea.** **Automatic differentiation** applies the chain rule to each operation as the network computes, which gives exact derivatives, apart from ordinary rounding. It is the same machinery as backpropagation, applied to the input $t$ instead of the weights.

**For this network.** The derivative of $\tanh(u)$ is $1 - \tanh^2(u)$, so by the chain rule each hidden unit $\tanh(w t + b)$ has slope $w \, (1 - \tanh^2(w t + b))$. The slope of the whole network is the weighted sum of those:

$$
\frac{dy}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^{4} v_i \, w_i \, \big(1 - \tanh^2(w_i t + b_i)\big).
$$

For example, a unit with $w = 1$ and $b = 0$ at $t = 1$ has $\tanh(1) = 0.7616$, so its slope is $1 - 0.7616^2 = 0.420$.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-finite-difference.svg" alt="A plot of e to the minus t near t = 1. A solid straight line touches the curve at t = 1. Its slope, the true slope, is -0.368. A dashed line passes through the curve at t = 1 and at t = 1.5. Its slope, the finite difference with h = 0.5, is -0.289, less steep than the true slope.">
A finite difference measures the slope of a line through two points of the curve. The chain rule gives the slope at the point itself.
</Figure>

**In code.** In PyTorch it is one call, `torch.autograd.grad(y, t, torch.ones_like(y), create_graph=True)[0]`. The `torch.ones_like(y)` gives the slope at every point of `t` at once, `[0]` takes it out of the tuple the call returns, and `create_graph=True` keeps it differentiable so that the loss can later be differentiated with respect to the weights. [Physics loss](#lesson-physics-loss) runs it on the whole loss.

#### Second derivatives

**By the chain rule.** The equation in this chapter needs only $dy/dt$, but many equations in science also contain a second derivative. Differentiating each unit's slope once more, and writing $a_i = \tanh(w_i t + b_i)$ for the output of unit $i$, gives

$$
\frac{d^2y}{dt^2} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^{4} v_i \, w_i^2 \, a_i \, (1 - a_i^2).
$$

For a single unit with $v = 1$, $w = 1$ and $b = 0$ at $t = 1$, this is $-2 \times 0.762 \times 0.420 = -0.640$. For the untrained network at $t = 1$ it is 0.040426. In PyTorch it takes a second call of `torch.autograd.grad`, this time on the slope, which works only because the first call used `create_graph=True`.

**By finite differences.** The **second difference** estimates it from three nearby values:

$$
\frac{d^2y}{dt^2} \approx \frac{y(t + h) - 2\,y(t) + y(t - h)}{h^2}.
$$

It divides by $h^2$ instead of $h$, so rounding errors take over at a much larger step. For the untrained network at $t = 1$:

| h | Second difference | Error |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.040466 | 0.00004 |
| 0.001 | 0.040426 | 0.000000004 |
| 0.000001 | 0.040412 | 0.000014 |
| 0.00000001 | 0 | 0.040426 |

The **two-sided slope** $(y(t + h) - y(t - h)) \div 2h$ uses the same two outer points but divides by $2h$. At $h = 0.000001$ its error is 0.0000000001, while the second difference is already off by 0.000014. At $h = 0.00000001$ the change the second difference measures is smaller than the rounding in $y$, and it comes out as 0.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-second-difference.svg" alt="A plot of the error at t = 1 against the step h, both on logarithmic scales. The two-sided slope's error, a gray line, falls as h shrinks to about 0.000000000005 near h = 0.00002, then rises jaggedly for smaller steps. The second difference's error, a heavy line, is smallest, about 0.0000000009, near h = 0.0006. For smaller steps it climbs steeply, spikes above 1 near h = 0.00000002, and stays at 0.04, all of the true value, from h = 0.00000001 down.">
The error of the two-sided slope and of the second difference for the untrained network at $t = 1$. Dividing by $h^2$ moves the smallest error to a larger step and makes it larger: about 0.0000000009 near $h = 0.0006$ for the second difference, against about 0.000000000005 near $h = 0.00002$ for the slope.
</Figure>

### Running example

For the untrained network from part 1 at t = 1, the chain rule gives dy/dt = 0.053829. Finite differences with different step sizes:

| h | Finite difference | Error |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.055771 | 0.0019 |
| 0.001 | 0.053850 | 0.00002 |
| 0.000001 | 0.053829 | 0.00000002 |
| 0.000000000001 | 0.053735 | 0.000095 |

The error shrinks as h shrinks, then grows again at the smallest h, where rounding takes over. The chain rule needs no h at all.

```python
import numpy as np

p = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 1, 13)
w, b, v, c = p[0:4], p[4:8], p[8:12], p[12]
net = lambda t: np.tanh(w * t + b) @ v + c

slope = (w * (1 - np.tanh(w * 1.0 + b) ** 2)) @ v      # chain rule at t = 1
print(round(slope, 6))                                   # 0.053829
for h in (0.1, 1e-3, 1e-6, 1e-12):
    print(h, (net(1.0 + h) - net(1.0)) / h)
```

### Common questions

**Why not use finite differences in training?** They need a step $h$, and no choice is right: 0.1 gives an error of 0.0019 and $10^{-12}$ gives 0.000095 from rounding. Automatic differentiation has no $h$, and its result can itself be differentiated with respect to the weights, which training needs.

**Why is the second difference more accurate than the slope's estimate at h = 0.1?** Its three points sit on both sides of $t$, so the largest errors from the two sides cancel, and what is left shrinks like $h^2$ rather than $h$: an error of 0.00004 against 0.0019 for the one-sided estimate in the table above. The two-sided slope gains in the same way, which is why the figure compares the second difference with it.

**Is this the same as backpropagation?** It is the same chain rule. Backpropagation asks how the loss changes with each weight, and here the question is how the output changes with the input $t$.

The next section, [Physics loss](#lesson-physics-loss), uses this derivative in the loss.

## Physics loss

### The problem

Training needs a loss: one number that says how wrong the network is. The usual loss compares the network with known answers.

Here there are none. For example, the natural loss would be the squared difference between the network and the true solution, $(y(t) - e^{-t})^2$. That needs $e^{-t}$, which is the answer. For an equation with no known formula, there would be nothing to put in its place.

A few measured values would not be enough either. Suppose $y$ had been measured at $t = 0.5$, 1 and 2. Many curves pass through three points, and only one of them solves the equation:

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-problem-loss.svg" alt="A plot of y against t with three measured values marked at t = 0.5, 1 and 2. Three different curves pass through all three points: the true solution e to the minus t, one that starts much higher at t = 0, and one that starts much lower.">
Three curves that fit the same three measurements. The measurements alone cannot say which is right.
</Figure>

So the loss has to measure how well the network satisfies the equation and the starting value, using only the network itself.

### Earlier approaches

#### Fitting measurements

If y has been measured at a few times, the network can be fit to those values like any regression. But between and beyond the measurements it can do anything, because nothing tells it about the equation. Through three measured points pass infinitely many curves.

#### Trial solutions

Lagaris et al. (1998) built the starting value into the formula, for example y = 1 + t × (network output), which equals 1 at t = 0 whatever the network does, and trained only on the equation. This works for simple starting and boundary conditions, but such formulas are hard to build for complicated shapes.

### The physics loss

**Two requirements, one loss.** A **physics-informed neural network** (PINN) adds the two requirements as two terms of one loss. Pick a set of **collocation points**, here $t = 0, 0.25, 0.5, \dots, 2$, and compute the residual at each:

$$
L = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \frac{dy}{dt}(t_i) + y(t_i) \right)^2 + \big( y(0) - 1 \big)^2
$$

**The equation part.** The first term is the mean squared residual. For example, the line $1 - t$ has residual $-t$, so its equation part is the average of $t^2$ over the 9 points: (0 + 0.0625 + 0.25 + 0.5625 + 1 + 1.5625 + 2.25 + 3.0625 + 4) ÷ 9 = 1.417.

**The starting-value part.** The second term is the squared distance of $y(0)$ from 1. A candidate that starts at 0.8 pays $(0.8 - 1)^2 = 0.04$.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-residual.svg" alt="A plot of the residual at each of the 9 collocation points from t = 0 to t = 2. For e to the minus t the residual is 0 at every point. For 1 minus t the residual is minus t: 0 at the first point and falling steadily to -2 at the last.">
The residuals of the two candidates at the 9 collocation points.
</Figure>

**No labels.** The collocation points need no labels, so any number of them can be chosen anywhere. Measurements, when there are some, add a third term, as in [part 3](3.24-XT8-sciml-training-and-checking.md).

**Balance.** The terms can differ in size by orders of magnitude. In harder problems they are usually given weights so that one does not swamp the other.

#### Beyond one variable

**The heat equation.** Heat flowing along a rod of length 1 follows an equation in two variables, position $x$ and time $t$:

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \, \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}.
$$

Here $u(x, t)$ is the temperature, $\alpha$ is how fast the material spreads heat, and each $\partial$ is a derivative in one variable with the other held fixed, written $u_t$ and $u_{xx}$ for short. The ends are held at 0, $u(0, t) = u(1, t) = 0$, and the rod starts as $u(x, 0) = \sin(\pi x)$. The network takes $x$ and $t$ as inputs, and two autograd calls in $x$ give $u_{xx}$, as in [Derivatives](#lesson-derivatives).

**The loss.** The residual is now $u_t - \alpha \, u_{xx}$, and each condition gets its own term, checked at its own points: the equation inside the rod, the ends along $x = 0$ and $x = 1$, and the start along $t = 0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
L ={} & \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} \big( u_t - \alpha \, u_{xx} \big)^2_{(x_i, \, t_i)} \\
& + \frac{1}{M} \sum_{j} \big( u(0, t_j)^2 + u(1, t_j)^2 \big) \\
& + \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k} \big( u(x_k, 0) - \sin(\pi x_k) \big)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-heat-domain.svg" alt="The rod's position x from 0 to 1 across, and time t from 0 to 0.4 upwards. Circles scattered inside mark where the equation is checked. Squares along the left and right edges mark where u must be 0. Triangles along the bottom edge mark where u must equal sin of pi x. A larger ring at x = 0.5, t = 0.1 marks the point of the check below.">
Where each term of the heat-equation loss is checked.
</Figure>

**A check.** Take $\alpha = 0.5$ and two candidates: the solution $e^{-\alpha \pi^2 t} \sin(\pi x)$, and $e^{-\pi^2 t} \sin(\pi x)$, which cools twice as fast. Both are 0 at both ends, since $\sin 0 = \sin \pi = 0$, and both start at $\sin(\pi x)$, so their end and start terms are 0. Only the residual tells them apart, here at $x = 0.5$, $t = 0.1$:

| Value | Solution | Twice as fast |
|---|---|---|
| $u$ | 0.6105 | 0.3727 |
| $u_t$ | −3.013 | −3.6785 |
| $\alpha \, u_{xx}$ | −3.013 | −1.8392 |
| Residual | 0 | −1.8392 |

For the second candidate, $u_t = -\pi^2 u = -9.86960 \times 0.37271 \approx -3.6785$ and $\alpha \, u_{xx} = -0.5 \times 9.86960 \times 0.37271 \approx -1.8392$, so the residual is $\approx -1.8392$, as in the table. The [USAAIO 2025 Round 2 paper](https://forum.beaver-edge.ai/c/ai-olympiads/2025-usa-na-aio-round-2/9) opened with a problem on this kind of loss, for heat along a rod held at 0 at both ends and starting from a sine-shaped profile.

### Running example

Using the 9 collocation points:

| Candidate | Equation part | Starting-value part | Loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| $e^{-t}$ | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1 − t | 1.417 | 0 | 1.417 |
| Untrained network | 8.131 | 15.415 | 23.546 |

For 1 − t, the residual is −t, so the equation part is the mean of t² over the points. The starting value is right. The untrained network starts at about −2.9262, so its starting-value part is (−2.9262 − 1)² = 15.415, and its residuals are about −2.8 to −2.9 at every point. Of the three candidates in the table, only the true solution has a loss of 0.

```python
import numpy as np

t = np.linspace(0, 2, 9)                 # collocation points 0, 0.25, ..., 2

def physics_loss(y, dy_dt, y0):
    return np.mean((dy_dt + y) ** 2) + (y0 - 1) ** 2

print(physics_loss(np.exp(-t), -np.exp(-t), 1.0))          # e^-t: 0
print(round(physics_loss(1 - t, -np.ones_like(t), 1.0), 3)) # 1 - t: 1.417
```

#### In PyTorch

The same loss in PyTorch, with automatic differentiation in place of the hand-written slope. The weights are the 13 random starting weights from part 1, so the derivatives at $t = 1$ match [Derivatives](#lesson-derivatives) and the loss at step 0 matches the untrained network's 23.546. The collocation points get `requires_grad=True` so that autograd can differentiate with respect to $t$.

```python
import numpy as np
import torch

p = torch.tensor(np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 1, 13), requires_grad=True)

def net(t):
    w, b, v, c = p[0:4], p[4:8], p[8:12], p[12]
    return torch.tanh(t[:, None] * w + b) @ v + c         # y at every t

t = torch.linspace(0, 2, 9, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)

def physics_loss():
    y = net(t)
    dy_dt = torch.autograd.grad(y, t, torch.ones_like(y), create_graph=True)[0]
    y0 = net(torch.zeros(1, dtype=torch.float64))[0]
    return torch.mean((dy_dt + y) ** 2) + (y0 - 1) ** 2

t1 = torch.ones(1, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
y = net(t1)
dy = torch.autograd.grad(y, t1, torch.ones_like(y), create_graph=True)[0]
d2y = torch.autograd.grad(dy, t1, torch.ones_like(dy))[0]   # a second call
print(f"at t = 1: dy/dt {dy.item():.6f}, d2y/dt2 {d2y.item():.6f}")

opt = torch.optim.Adam([p], lr=0.05)
for step in range(3001):
    loss = physics_loss()
    if step % 1000 == 0:
        print(f"step {step:4d}  loss {loss.item():.6f}")
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()            # reaches the weights through dy/dt as well
    opt.step()

ts = torch.tensor([0.0, 1.0, 2.0], dtype=torch.float64)
print("network", net(ts).detach().numpy().round(4))
print("exact  ", np.exp(-ts.numpy()).round(4))
```

```output
at t = 1: dy/dt 0.053829, d2y/dt2 0.040426
step    0  loss 23.545892
step 1000  loss 0.000411
step 2000  loss 0.000151
step 3000  loss 0.000034
network [0.9998 0.3668 0.1357]
exact   [1.     0.3679 0.1353]
```

Adam needs thousands of steps to get close, where the L-BFGS run of [part 3](3.24-XT8-sciml-training-and-checking.md) takes 100 iterations, which is why PINNs usually finish with L-BFGS.

### Common questions

**How many collocation points are needed?** Enough to cover the range closely. This page uses 9. Real problems use thousands, often drawn at random.

**Can the loss be 0 for a wrong function?** In principle, yes: the loss only checks the collocation points, and a function could satisfy the equation at those 9 points and not between them. More points make this harder, and [part 3](3.24-XT8-sciml-training-and-checking.md) checks the result at other points.

**Why is the starting-value part needed?** Without it, $2e^{-t}$ would also have a loss of 0, since its residual is 0 everywhere. The second term costs it $(2 - 1)^2 = 1$.

**Where is the data?** There is none. The network is never shown a value of the solution. The equation and the starting value are the only information it gets.
