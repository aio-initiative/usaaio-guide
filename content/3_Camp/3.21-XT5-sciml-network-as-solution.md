## The problem

The answer to Step 1 is a function, $y(t)$. To search for it, the model needs a way to write down candidate functions with numbers it can adjust.

For example, the straight line $a + b\,t$ is a family of functions with two adjustable numbers. Setting $a = 1$ and $b = -1$ gives $1 - t$, the candidate of Step 1. But no choice of $a$ and $b$ gives $e^{-t}$. The best-fit straight line in the least-squares sense, between $t = 0$ and $t = 2$, is $0.84 - 0.41\,t$, which is already off by 0.16 at $t = 0$.

The dashed line 1 minus t leaves it almost at once and goes below zero. The dotted line 0.84 minus 0.41 t, the best-fit straight line, crosses the curve twice but cannot follow its bend.">
No straight line follows the curve.

So the family has to be flexible enough to contain the answer, or something very close to it. It also has to be usable in two ways:

- **Evaluated at any t**, not only at the points of a grid.
- **Differentiated at any t**, because the equation is about $dy/dt$.

## Earlier approaches

### Values on a grid

Euler's method and mesh-based solvers store y only at grid points. With more dimensions the grid grows fast: 100 points along each of three space directions and time is 100⁴ = 100 million values.

### Fixed formulas

Another approach writes y as a fixed sum of global basis functions, such as sinusoids or Chebyshev polynomials, and chooses the coefficients so the sum satisfies the equation. These **spectral methods** are very accurate for smooth problems on simple shapes, but struggle with sharp features and complicated geometry.

## A small network

**A function of t.** A neural network takes $t$ as input and outputs $y(t)$. It is defined for every $t$, not just at grid points, and more dimensions only mean more inputs, such as $(x, y, z, t)$, rather than a bigger grid.

**The network.** The network in this chapter has one hidden layer of 4 units:

$$
y(t) = c + v_1 \tanh(w_1 t + b_1) + v_2 \tanh(w_2 t + b_2) + v_3 \tanh(w_3 t + b_3) + v_4 \tanh(w_4 t + b_4).
$$

Each hidden unit computes $\tanh(w t + b)$ with its own $w$ and $b$, and the output is $c$ plus a weighted sum of the 4 hidden units. That is 4 + 4 + 4 + 1 = 13 weights.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-network.svg" alt="The input t on the left, with arrows to four hidden units, each computing tanh of w times t plus b with its own w and b. Arrows labeled v1 to v4 lead from the hidden units to the output y(t), which is c plus the weighted sum.">
The 13-weight network: 4 values of $w$, 4 of $b$, 4 of $v$, and $c$.
</Figure>

**One unit.** A single unit $\tanh(w t + b)$ is a smooth step, from −1 up to 1 when $w$ is positive and from 1 down to −1 when $w$ is negative. For example, with $w = 1$ and $b = 0$ it is 0 at $t = 0$, 0.762 at $t = 1$ and 0.964 at $t = 2$. The weight $w$ sets how steep the step is and $b$ where it sits. The network builds its curve by adding four such steps.

**Why tanh.** The tanh function is smooth, so the network has derivatives of every order, which Step 3 needs. ReLU would be a poor choice here: its second derivative is 0 almost everywhere, which breaks equations that involve second derivatives.

## Running example

The 13 weights start as random numbers. At that point the network is far from the answer:

| t | 0 | 0.5 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Untrained network | −2.926 | −2.924 | −2.903 | −2.872 | −2.835 |
| Exact, $e^{-t}$ | 1 | 0.607 | 0.368 | 0.223 | 0.135 |

It is nearly flat, slightly rising, and starts at −2.926 instead of 1. Training, in Steps 4 and 5, changes the 13 weights until the network matches the right column.

```python
import numpy as np

p = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 1, 13)    # 13 random starting weights
w, b, v, c = p[0:4], p[4:8], p[8:12], p[12]

def net(t):
    return np.tanh(np.multiply.outer(t, w) + b) @ v + c

print(net(np.array([0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2])).round(3))
```

## Common questions

**Why 4 hidden units?** It is enough for a smooth curve like $e^{-t}$ and small enough to write out. A harder problem needs more units and more layers.

**Why is the untrained network nearly flat?** Its 13 weights are standard-normal random numbers, and the four units' slopes mostly cancel, so the sum changes only slowly with $t$. The constant $c$ is −2.325, so the output sits near −2.9 across the whole range.

**Is this different from fitting a curve to data?** The network is the same. The difference is the loss: there are no data points to fit. Steps 4 and 5 train the network to satisfy the equation.
