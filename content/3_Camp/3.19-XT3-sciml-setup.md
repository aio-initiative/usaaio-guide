## Setup

### The problem

Science and engineering describe many systems by how they change: heat spreading through a metal plate, air flowing over a wing, a drug clearing from the blood. These descriptions are **differential equations**, and solving them is how engineers predict what a system will do.

Classical solvers handle this well, but each new shape or setting needs a new run. For example, a drug that clears at a rate $k$ follows $dy/dt = -k\,y$, and $k$ differs from patient to patient. A solver gives the curve for one value of $k$. A different $k$ means starting again.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-problem-setup.svg" alt="A plot of the amount left against time for three values of k. Run 1, with k = 0.5, falls slowly. Run 2, with k = 1, falls faster. Run 3, with k = 2, falls fastest. Each curve is a separate run of the solver.">
One equation, three settings, three separate solver runs.
</Figure>

Classical solvers also do not easily combine the equation with measured data. Scientific machine learning trains neural networks with the equation itself built into the loss, so a network can learn from physics, from data, or from both.

### History

| Year | Method | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| 1768 | Euler's method | Solving an equation step by step from a starting value |
| 1950s to 1960s | Finite element method | Solving on a mesh over complex shapes. Still the engineering standard |
| 1998 | Lagaris et al. | A neural network as the solution of a differential equation |
| 2019 | Physics-informed neural networks (Raissi et al.) | The equation as a loss term, computed with automatic differentiation |
| 2020 | Fourier Neural Operator (Li et al.) | Learning a whole family of solutions, so a new case takes one pass |
| 2023 | GraphCast (Lam et al.) | A learned weather model beats the leading physics-based forecast on 90% of the targets tested |

The 1998 idea became practical at scale once deep learning libraries made automatic differentiation and GPUs easy to use.

### The intuition

#### The equation as the teacher

A classifier learns from labeled examples. Here the equation does the teaching: wherever the network's output breaks the equation, the loss penalizes it. For example, an equation saying "the rate of change equals minus the value" asks for a slope of −0.5 wherever the value is 0.5, so a function whose value at some moment is 0.5 while its slope there is −0.2 is breaking it.

#### An adjustable function

The network is a smooth function of time, or of position and time, with weights that can be adjusted. Training adjusts them until the equation holds at many chosen points.

#### Data where it exists

Measurements, when there are some, go into the same loss as an extra term. Unknown constants in the equation, such as a decay rate, can then be learned along with the solution.

## The equation

### The problem

A differential equation says how a quantity changes, not what it is. This chapter uses

$$
\frac{dy}{dt} = -y, \qquad y(0) = 1
$$

The equation says that $y$ shrinks at a rate equal to its current value. When $y$ is 1 it falls at a rate of 1, and when it has dropped to 0.5 it falls at a rate of 0.5, so it slows down as it goes. A radioactive sample decaying, and the difference between a hot drink's temperature and the room, behave this way: each falls at a rate proportional to how far it still has to go.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-problem-equation.svg" alt="A plot of y against t. Short lines all over the plot show the slope the equation gives at each point: steep and falling where y is large, nearly flat where y is small. A dot marks the start, y(0) = 1, and one heavy curve leaves it and follows the slopes downwards.">
What the equation gives, and what it leaves to be found.
</Figure>

At every point the equation gives a slope, the short lines in the figure. It does not give the curve. Many curves follow the slopes, one through every starting height. The starting value $y(0) = 1$ picks out exactly one of them. The task is to find that function.

### Earlier approaches

#### Solving by hand

Some equations can be solved with algebra and calculus. This one has the solution $y = e^{-t}$. Most equations met in practice, such as those for air flowing over a wing, have no such formula.

#### Step-by-step solvers

**Euler's method** starts at the known value and repeatedly steps forward by a small time $h$, using the equation to get the slope:

$$
y_{\text{next}} = y + h \times \frac{dy}{dt} = y - h \, y = (1 - h) \, y.
$$

For example, with $h = 0.5$ every step halves $y$: 1, 0.5, 0.25. Smaller steps are more accurate but take more of them. Modern solvers use better step rules, and for problems in space they use a mesh, but the idea is the same.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-euler.svg" alt="A plot of y against t from 0 to 2. The exact solution, e to the minus t, falls smoothly from 1 to 0.135. Euler's method with h = 0.1 follows it closely, slightly below. Euler's method with h = 0.5 has only four steps and falls well below the exact curve, ending at 0.0625.">
Euler's method against the exact solution. Smaller steps follow the curve more closely.
</Figure>

### The residual

**Checking a candidate.** A candidate function can be checked by putting it into the equation. Moving everything to one side, the equation says $dy/dt + y = 0$. The **residual** is how far a candidate is from that:

$$
\text{residual}(t) = \frac{dy}{dt} + y.
$$

**Two candidates.** For $y = e^{-t}$, the slope is $-e^{-t}$, so the residual is $-e^{-t} + e^{-t} = 0$ at every $t$. For the straight line $y = 1 - t$, the slope is −1, so the residual is $-1 + (1 - t) = -t$, which is 0 only at $t = 0$.

**Why it matters.** The residual needs no knowledge of the answer, only the candidate and its slope. For every solution of the equation it is 0 at every $t$, and for any other function it is not. [Physics loss](3.22-XT6-sciml-derivatives.md#lesson-physics-loss), in part 2, turns this into a loss.

### Running example

#### Euler's method

Euler's method against the exact solution:

| t | Exact, $e^{-t}$ | Euler, h = 0.5 | Euler, h = 0.1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 0.5 | 0.6065 | 0.5 | 0.5905 |
| 1 | 0.3679 | 0.25 | 0.3487 |
| 1.5 | 0.2231 | 0.125 | 0.2059 |
| 2 | 0.1353 | 0.0625 | 0.1216 |

With h = 0.5, each step multiplies by 0.5, and the error at t = 2 is more than half the true value. With h = 0.1, it takes 20 steps but the error is about 10%.

<ProblemCard id="sm-six-steps-to-reach-t-six" />

#### The residual

Now check two candidates with the residual. The straight line y = 1 − t starts at the right value, but its slope is −1, so its residual is −1 + (1 − t) = −t:

| t | 0 | 0.5 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Residual of 1 − t | 0 | −0.5 | −1 | −1.5 | −2 |
| Residual of $e^{-t}$ | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The line satisfies the equation only at t = 0. The exponential satisfies it everywhere, because its slope, $-e^{-t}$, is exactly minus its value.

```python
import numpy as np

y, h = 1.0, 0.1
for _ in range(10):          # 10 steps of 0.1 reach t = 1
    y = y - h * y
print(round(y, 4), round(np.exp(-1), 4))    # 0.3487 0.3679

t = np.array([0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2])
print(-1 + (1 - t))                          # residual of 1 - t
```

### Common questions

**Why is the starting value needed?** The equation alone has many solutions. $2e^{-t}$ and $5e^{-t}$ both shrink at a rate equal to their value. Only $y(0) = 1$ picks out $e^{-t}$.

**How does Euler's error depend on the step?** Roughly in proportion to it. At $t = 2$ the error is 0.073 with $h = 0.5$ and 0.014 with $h = 0.1$: a step 5 times smaller gives an error about 5 times smaller, for 5 times the work.

**Can the residual be 0 at some points for a wrong function?** Yes. The line $1 - t$ has a residual of 0 at $t = 0$. A solution must have a residual of 0 at every $t$, which is why the physics loss checks many points.

**Why use a network if Euler's method works?** For this equation there is no reason to. The chapter uses a problem with a known answer so that every step can be checked. [Part 3](3.24-XT8-sciml-training-and-checking.md) says where the approach pays off.

The next section, [A network as the solution](#lesson-a-network-as-the-solution), represents the unknown function with a network.

## A network as the solution

### The problem

The answer to the equation is a function, $y(t)$. To search for it, the model needs a way to write down candidate functions with numbers it can adjust.

For example, the straight line $a + b\,t$ is a family of functions with two adjustable numbers. Setting $a = 1$ and $b = -1$ gives $1 - t$, the candidate from [The equation](#lesson-the-equation). But no choice of $a$ and $b$ gives $e^{-t}$. The best-fit straight line in the least-squares sense, between $t = 0$ and $t = 2$, is $0.84 - 0.41\,t$, which is already off by 0.16 at $t = 0$.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-problem-network.svg" alt="A plot of y against t from 0 to 2. The curve e to the minus t bends as it falls from 1 to 0.135. The dashed line 1 minus t leaves it almost at once and goes below zero. The dotted line 0.84 minus 0.41 t, the best-fit straight line, crosses the curve twice but cannot follow its bend.">
No straight line follows the curve.
</Figure>

So the family has to be flexible enough to contain the answer, or something very close to it. It also has to be usable in two ways:

- **Evaluated at any t**, not only at the points of a grid.
- **Differentiated at any t**, because the equation is about $dy/dt$.

### Earlier approaches

#### Values on a grid

Euler's method and mesh-based solvers store y only at grid points. With more dimensions the grid grows fast: 100 points along each of three space directions and time is 100⁴ = 100 million values.

#### Fixed formulas

Another approach writes y as a fixed sum of global basis functions, such as sinusoids or Chebyshev polynomials, and chooses the coefficients so the sum satisfies the equation. These **spectral methods** are very accurate for smooth problems on simple shapes, but struggle with sharp features and complicated geometry.

### A small network

**A function of t.** A neural network takes $t$ as input and outputs $y(t)$. It is defined for every $t$, not just at grid points, and more dimensions only mean more inputs, such as $(x, y, z, t)$, rather than a bigger grid.

**The network.** The network in this chapter has one hidden layer of 4 units:

$$
y(t) = c + v_1 \tanh(w_1 t + b_1) + v_2 \tanh(w_2 t + b_2) + v_3 \tanh(w_3 t + b_3) + v_4 \tanh(w_4 t + b_4).
$$

Each hidden unit computes $\tanh(w t + b)$ with its own $w$ and $b$, and the output is $c$ plus a weighted sum of the 4 hidden units. That is 4 + 4 + 4 + 1 = 13 weights.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/sciml-network.svg" alt="The input t on the left, with arrows to four hidden units, each computing tanh of w times t plus b with its own w and b. Arrows labeled v1 to v4 lead from the hidden units to the output y(t), which is c plus the weighted sum.">
The 13-weight network: 4 values of $w$, 4 of $b$, 4 of $v$, and $c$.
</Figure>

**One unit.** A single unit $\tanh(w t + b)$ is a smooth step, from −1 up to 1 when $w$ is positive and from 1 down to −1 when $w$ is negative. For example, with $w = 1$ and $b = 0$ it is 0 at $t = 0$, 0.762 at $t = 1$ and 0.964 at $t = 2$. The weight $w$ sets how steep the step is and $b$ where it sits. The network builds its curve by adding four such steps.

**Why tanh.** The tanh function is smooth, so the network has derivatives of every order, which [part 2](3.22-XT6-sciml-derivatives.md#lesson-derivatives) needs. ReLU would be a poor choice here: its second derivative is 0 almost everywhere, which breaks equations that involve second derivatives.

### Running example

The 13 weights start as random numbers. At that point the network is far from the answer:

| t | 0 | 0.5 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Untrained network | −2.926 | −2.924 | −2.903 | −2.872 | −2.835 |
| Exact, $e^{-t}$ | 1 | 0.607 | 0.368 | 0.223 | 0.135 |

It is nearly flat, slightly rising, and starts at −2.926 instead of 1. Training, in parts 2 and 3, changes the 13 weights until the network matches the right column.

```python
import numpy as np

p = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 1, 13)    # 13 random starting weights
w, b, v, c = p[0:4], p[4:8], p[8:12], p[12]

def net(t):
    return np.tanh(np.multiply.outer(t, w) + b) @ v + c

print(net(np.array([0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2])).round(3))
```

### Common questions

**Why 4 hidden units?** It is enough for a smooth curve like $e^{-t}$ and small enough to write out. A harder problem needs more units and more layers.

**Why is the untrained network nearly flat?** Its 13 weights are standard-normal random numbers, and the four units' slopes mostly cancel, so the sum changes only slowly with $t$. The constant $c$ is −2.325, so the output sits near −2.9 across the whole range.

**Is this different from fitting a curve to data?** The network is the same. The difference is the loss: there are no data points to fit. Parts 2 and 3 train the network to satisfy the equation.
