> **Goal:** The data distribution, three approaches and the roadmap.

## The problem

A classifier maps an image to a label. A **generative model** goes the other way: after seeing many examples, it produces new ones that look like they came from the same source, without copying any of them. The examples can be images, audio or molecules. This chapter uses images.

The difficulty is that almost every combination of pixel values is noise. For example, a 64 × 64 color image is 12,288 numbers, and filling them in at random gives static, never a face. (The figure uses a small grid to show the idea.)

Real images occupy a tiny part of all possible images. The model has to learn where that part is, and how to pick new points inside it.

## History

| Year | Model | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | VAE (Kingma and Welling) | An autoencoder whose codes fill a region, so a code drawn at random from it decodes to a new image |
| 2014 | GAN (Goodfellow et al.) | A generator trained against a network that judges real from fake |
| 2015 | Diffusion (Sohl-Dickstein et al.) | Generation as the reverse of gradually adding noise |
| 2018 | StyleGAN (Karras et al.) | Photorealistic 1024 × 1024 faces |
| 2020 | DDPM (Ho et al.) | Diffusion reaches the image quality of GANs |
| 2022 | Classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans) | Steering diffusion toward a prompt without a separate classifier |
| 2022 | Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al.) | Text-to-image on a single consumer GPU, with public weights |

GANs produced the most realistic images from about 2015 to 2020. Since then, most image generators, including Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 2 and Imagen, have been diffusion models.

## The intuition

### The data distribution

The training images are samples from an unknown **distribution**: some images are likely, most are essentially impossible. A generative model learns enough about this distribution to draw new samples from it. In this chapter the training images have just 4 pixels, and only two of them exist: one with the left column bright and one with the right column bright. An image such as (0.3, 0.9, 0.7, 0.1), read left to right and top to bottom, looks like neither.

### Starting from randomness

Every method here starts from random numbers, which a computer can produce cheaply and endlessly, and learns a function that turns them into realistic images. Different random numbers give different images.

### Three approaches

| Approach | Idea | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autoencoders and VAEs | Compress images to short codes, then decode codes back to images | Fast and stable to train | VAEs blur, and a plain autoencoder's random codes do not decode to images at all |
| GANs | A generator learns to fool a judge that compares its images with real ones | Sharp images, fast to sample | Unstable training, little variety |
| Diffusion | Learn to remove noise, then start from pure noise | Sharp, varied images and stable training | Slow to sample, many steps per image |
