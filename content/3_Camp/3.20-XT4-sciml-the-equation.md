## The problem

A differential equation says how a quantity changes, not what it is. This chapter uses

$$
\frac{dy}{dt} = -y, \qquad y(0) = 1
$$

The equation says that $y$ shrinks at a rate equal to its current value. When $y$ is 1 it falls at a rate of 1, and when it has dropped to 0.5 it falls at a rate of 0.5, so it slows down as it goes. A radioactive sample decaying, and the difference between a hot drink's temperature and the room, behave this way: each falls at a rate proportional to how far it still has to go.

At every point the equation gives a slope, the short lines in the figure. It does not give the curve. Many curves follow the slopes, one through every starting height. The starting value $y(0) = 1$ picks out exactly one of them. The task is to find that function.

## Earlier approaches

### Solving by hand

Some equations can be solved with algebra and calculus. This one has the solution $y = e^{-t}$. Most equations met in practice, such as those for air flowing over a wing, have no such formula.

### Step-by-step solvers

**Euler's method** starts at the known value and repeatedly steps forward by a small time $h$, using the equation to get the slope:

$$
y_{\text{next}} = y + h \times \frac{dy}{dt} = y - h \, y = (1 - h) \, y.
$$

For example, with $h = 0.5$ every step halves $y$: 1, 0.5, 0.25. Smaller steps are more accurate but take more of them. Modern solvers use better step rules, and for problems in space they use a mesh, but the idea is the same.

## The residual

**Checking a candidate.** A candidate function can be checked by putting it into the equation. Moving everything to one side, the equation says $dy/dt + y = 0$. The **residual** is how far a candidate is from that:

$$
\text{residual}(t) = \frac{dy}{dt} + y.
$$

**Two candidates.** For $y = e^{-t}$, the slope is $-e^{-t}$, so the residual is $-e^{-t} + e^{-t} = 0$ at every $t$. For the straight line $y = 1 - t$, the slope is −1, so the residual is $-1 + (1 - t) = -t$, which is 0 only at $t = 0$.

**Why it matters.** The residual needs no knowledge of the answer, only the candidate and its slope. For every solution of the equation it is 0 at every $t$, and for any other function it is not. Step 4 turns this into a loss.

## Running example

### Euler's method

Euler's method against the exact solution:

| t | Exact, $e^{-t}$ | Euler, h = 0.5 | Euler, h = 0.1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 0.5 | 0.6065 | 0.5 | 0.5905 |
| 1 | 0.3679 | 0.25 | 0.3487 |
| 1.5 | 0.2231 | 0.125 | 0.2059 |
| 2 | 0.1353 | 0.0625 | 0.1216 |

With h = 0.5, each step multiplies by 0.5, and the error at t = 2 is more than half the true value. With h = 0.1, it takes 20 steps but the error is about 10%.

### The residual

Now check two candidates with the residual. The straight line y = 1 − t starts at the right value, but its slope is −1, so its residual is −1 + (1 − t) = −t:

| t | 0 | 0.5 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Residual of 1 − t | 0 | −0.5 | −1 | −1.5 | −2 |
| Residual of $e^{-t}$ | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The line satisfies the equation only at t = 0. The exponential satisfies it everywhere, because its slope, $-e^{-t}$, is exactly minus its value.

```python
import numpy as np

y, h = 1.0, 0.1
for _ in range(10):          # 10 steps of 0.1 reach t = 1
    y = y - h * y
print(round(y, 4), round(np.exp(-1), 4))    # 0.3487 0.3679

t = np.array([0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2])
print(-1 + (1 - t))                          # residual of 1 - t
```

## Common questions

**Why is the starting value needed?** The equation alone has many solutions. $2e^{-t}$ and $5e^{-t}$ both shrink at a rate equal to their value. Only $y(0) = 1$ picks out $e^{-t}$.

**How does Euler's error depend on the step?** Roughly in proportion to it. At $t = 2$ the error is 0.073 with $h = 0.5$ and 0.014 with $h = 0.1$: a step 5 times smaller gives an error about 5 times smaller, for 5 times the work.

**Can the residual be 0 at some points for a wrong function?** Yes. The line $1 - t$ has a residual of 0 at $t = 0$. A solution must have a residual of 0 at every $t$, which is why Step 4 checks many points.

**Why use a network if Euler's method works?** For this equation there is no reason to. The chapter uses a problem with a known answer so that every step can be checked. Step 5 says where the approach pays off.
