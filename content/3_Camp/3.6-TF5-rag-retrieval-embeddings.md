## Overview

Ask a language model when your school library closes on Saturday. It has never seen your school's handbook, so it either refuses or makes up a plausible time. Making things up like this is called **hallucination**. A person would look it up first and then answer from what they found. Can a model do the same?

**Retrieval-augmented generation** (RAG) does that. It finds the passages most related to the question, pastes them into the prompt and lets the model answer from them. RAG is not on the USAAIO syllabus. Its core step ranks vectors by how close they are, which is how k-nearest neighbors works and how word embeddings are compared. Both of those topics are on the syllabus, and the [2026 Round 1](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-1-problems.pdf) paper had a problem on each (Problem 9 is the k-nearest neighbors task, Problem 5 the word-embedding one).

## Example: answer from the right two notes

A school handbook has four notes. We made up a vector of length 1 for each note so the arithmetic stays small. Read the three entries as rough scores for how much a note is about the library, about closing time and about food:

| Note | Text | Vector (library, closing, food) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| d1 | The library opens at 8 am. | $(1, 0, 0)$ |
| d2 | The library closes at 9 pm on weekdays. | $(0.8, 0.6, 0)$ |
| d3 | Weekend hours are 10 am to 6 pm. | $(0, 1, 0)$ |
| d4 | The cafeteria serves lunch from 11 to 1. | $(0, 0.6, 0.8)$ |

The question "When does the library close on Saturday?" gets the vector $(0.6, 0.8, 0)$. The prompt has room for two notes. Which two should go in, and what should the model answer?

## Explanation

### Two kinds of memory

A language model stores what it learned in its weights. The weights are frozen when training ends, and the model cannot tell you where a fact came from. A folder of documents is a second kind of memory. You can add a note the day the hours change, and you can point to the note an answer came from. RAG uses both. The documents hold the facts, and the model reads them and writes the answer.

Looking things up takes work. The handbook might have thousands of notes, but the prompt has room for only a few. We need a fast way to score how related each note is to the question.

### Measuring "related" with cosine similarity

Suppose we could turn every piece of text into a list of numbers, an **embedding**, so that texts about similar things get vectors pointing in similar directions. Then a small angle between two vectors means the texts are related. You met this idea for single tokens in [Build a Transformer: Embed](2.30-TF10-transformer-setup.md#lesson-embed).

The **cosine similarity** is the cosine of that angle:

$$
\cos(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{d}) = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\lVert \mathbf{q} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{d} \rVert} .
$$

Here $\mathbf{q}$ is the question's vector and $\mathbf{d}$ is a note's vector. The dot product $\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}$ multiplies matching entries and adds them up. $\lVert \mathbf{q} \rVert$ is the length of $\mathbf{q}$, the square root of the sum of its squared entries. The cosine is 1 when the vectors point the same way, 0 when they are perpendicular and $-1$ when they point in opposite directions. Dividing by both lengths means only the direction counts. Doubling a vector does not change its cosine with anything.

When every vector has length 1, both lengths in the denominator are 1, so the cosine equals the dot product. The focus problem's vectors have length 1, so there we only need dot products.

### Where the vectors come from: TF-IDF

The simplest embedding counts words. List every word that appears in the notes, ignoring capital letters and punctuation. That list is the **vocabulary**. A note's vector has one entry per vocabulary word, holding how many times the word appears in the note. That count is the **term frequency** (tf).

Raw counts have a problem. "The" appears in d1, d2 and d4, so a question containing "the" matches three notes, even though "the" says nothing about what a note is about. A word that appears in few notes tells us more. So we multiply each count by a weight that grows as the word gets rarer. With $n$ notes, let the **document frequency** $\mathrm{df}(w)$ be the number of notes that contain the word $w$. The weight is the **inverse document frequency**:

$$
\mathrm{idf}(w) = \ln \frac{n}{\mathrm{df}(w)} + 1 .
$$

A word in every note gets $\ln 1 + 1 = 1$. Without the $+1$ it would get 0 and drop out of every score. The vector with entries tf times idf, scaled to length 1, is the **TF-IDF** embedding. scikit-learn's `TfidfVectorizer` uses this formula when you pass `smooth_idf=False`.

In the four handbook notes, $n = 4$. "The" is in 3 notes, so its idf is $\ln(4/3) + 1 \approx 1.29$. "Weekend" is only in d3, so its idf is $\ln 4 + 1 \approx 2.39$.

### When word counts fail

Now run TF-IDF on the focus question, "When does the library close on Saturday?". Only "the", "library" and "on" are in the vocabulary. To TF-IDF, "close" is a different word from "closes", and "Saturday" has nothing to do with "weekend". The cosine similarities come out as 0.30 for d1, 0.56 for d2, 0 for d3 and 0.08 for d4. The note with the weekend hours ranks last, with a score of 0, because it shares no word with the question.

A better embedding would know that Saturday is a weekend day. Many systems use a **dense** embedding instead. A Transformer encoder reads the text and outputs a few hundred numbers (768 in the Dense Passage Retrieval paper), most of them nonzero. It is trained on pairs of a question and the passage that answers it. A contrastive loss pulls each question's vector toward its passage and away from the other passages in the batch (see [Contrastive Learning and CLIP](2.14-RP2-contrastive-learning.md)). After training, a question and its answer get similar vectors even when they share no words. The made-up vectors in the focus problem behave like a dense embedding. They put the Saturday question close to the weekend-hours note.

TF-IDF vectors are called **sparse**, because almost every entry is 0. Sparse scores catch rare keywords and names that the question repeats word for word, so many systems add a sparse score to a dense one. A popular sparse score is BM25, a version of TF-IDF that also adjusts for the length of each note and gives each repeat of a word less extra weight.

### The pipeline

1. **Chunk** the documents into passages of a few sentences each.
2. **Embed** every chunk once and store the vectors.
3. When a question arrives, **embed** it with the same method.
4. **Retrieve** the $k$ chunks with the highest cosine similarity. Here $k$ counts retrieved chunks, not clusters as in k-means.
5. **Prompt** the model with the chunks and the question, with an instruction to answer only from them. If the chunks do not contain the answer, the model should say so rather than guess.

Chunk size is a trade-off. Big chunks carry more context, but they mix several topics into one vector that matches none of them well. Small chunks are precise, but they can lose their subject. Suppose the handbook said "The library is open on weekends. Hours are 10 am to 6 pm." Chunked one sentence at a time, the second chunk no longer says "library". Note d3 looks like such a chunk.

To test retrieval on its own, collect test questions and mark the chunk that holds each answer. **Recall@k** is the fraction of test questions whose answer-bearing chunk appears in the top $k$ retrieved. If 3 of 4 test questions have their answer in the top 2, recall@2 is $3/4$. If retrieval misses the right chunk, even a perfect model cannot answer. Retrieval metrics are necessary but not sufficient: the answer can still be unsupported, incomplete, or contradicted by the retrieved text. End-to-end answer accuracy can hide compensating failures, such as a model answering from memory despite poor retrieval. Here each question has one answer-bearing chunk. When a question has several, information-retrieval books define recall@k as the fraction of them in the top $k$. Some papers call our version top-k accuracy.

### Solving the focus problem

The vectors have length 1, so each cosine is a dot product with $\mathbf{q} = (0.6, 0.8, 0)$:

- d1: $0.6 \cdot 1 = 0.6$
- d2: $0.6 \cdot 0.8 + 0.8 \cdot 0.6 = 0.96$
- d3: $0.8 \cdot 1 = 0.8$
- d4: $0.8 \cdot 0.6 = 0.48$

The top two are d2 and d3, so the prompt is:

```text
Answer using only the notes below.
- The library closes at 9 pm on weekdays.
- Weekend hours are 10 am to 6 pm.
Question: When does the library close on Saturday?
Answer:
```

Saturday is a weekend day, and d3 says weekend hours end at 6 pm, so the model should answer 6 pm. d3 holds the answer, but it never says "library". The model has to assume these are the library's weekend hours. d2 at least shows that the notes are about the library.

d3 is the answer-bearing note, and it ranks second. For this one question, recall@1 is 0 and recall@2 is 1. TF-IDF ranked d3 last, so with TF-IDF even recall@3 would be 0.

## Implementation

This code puts the four note vectors in the rows of a matrix `D`, computes the cosine of each with the question and prints the two best notes. It divides by the lengths, so it also works for vectors that are not length 1.

```python
import numpy as np

D = np.array([[1.0, 0.0, 0.0],      # d1: The library opens at 8 am.
              [0.8, 0.6, 0.0],      # d2: The library closes at 9 pm on weekdays.
              [0.0, 1.0, 0.0],      # d3: Weekend hours are 10 am to 6 pm.
              [0.0, 0.6, 0.8]])     # d4: The cafeteria serves lunch from 11 to 1.
q = np.array([0.6, 0.8, 0.0])       # "When does the library close on Saturday?"
sims = D @ q / (np.linalg.norm(D, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(q))
print("cosine similarities:", np.round(sims, 2))
print("top 2 documents:", [f"d{i + 1}" for i in np.argsort(-sims)[:2]])
```

```output
cosine similarities: [0.6  0.96 0.8  0.48]
top 2 documents: ['d2', 'd3']
```

The scores match the hand computation, 0.96 for d2 and 0.8 for d3.

Next is a full retriever on six real sentences. It builds the vocabulary, computes idf with the formula above and scales each note's TF-IDF vector to length 1. It then retrieves the top 2 notes for three test questions, counts recall@2 and prints the prompt for the first question.

```python
import re
import numpy as np

docs = [
    "The library opens at 8 am and closes at 9 pm on weekdays.",
    "On weekends the library is open from 10 am to 6 pm.",
    "The cafeteria serves lunch from 11 am to 1 pm.",
    "Students may borrow up to five library books at a time.",
    "Late library books cost 25 cents per day.",
    "The gym is open to students after classes until 7 pm.",
]
questions = [
    ("When does the library open on weekends?", 1),
    ("How much do late books cost?", 4),
    ("When is lunch served?", 2),
]
tokenize = lambda s: re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower())
vocab = sorted({w for d in docs for w in tokenize(d)})
col = {w: j for j, w in enumerate(vocab)}

def counts(text):
    v = np.zeros(len(vocab))
    for w in tokenize(text):
        if w in col:
            v[col[w]] += 1
    return v

tf = np.array([counts(d) for d in docs])
df = (tf > 0).sum(axis=0)
idf = np.log(len(docs) / df) + 1                  # rare words count more
doc_vecs = tf * idf
doc_vecs /= np.linalg.norm(doc_vecs, axis=1, keepdims=True)

def retrieve(question, k=2):
    v = counts(question) * idf
    v /= np.linalg.norm(v)
    scores = doc_vecs @ v                          # cosine similarity, since all rows have length 1
    return np.argsort(-scores)[:k], scores

hits = 0
for question, answer_doc in questions:
    top, scores = retrieve(question)
    hits += answer_doc in top
    print(f"{question:42} -> docs {top.tolist()}  scores {np.round(scores[top], 2).tolist()}")
print(f"recall@2 = {hits}/{len(questions)}")

top, _ = retrieve(questions[0][0])
context = "\n".join(f"- {docs[i]}" for i in top)
prompt = f"Answer using only the notes below.\n{context}\nQuestion: {questions[0][0]}\nAnswer:"
print(prompt)
```

```output
When does the library open on weekends?    -> docs [1, 0]  scores [0.63, 0.21]
How much do late books cost?               -> docs [4, 3]  scores [0.61, 0.12]
When is lunch served?                      -> docs [2, 1]  scores [0.31, 0.18]
recall@2 = 3/3
Answer using only the notes below.
- On weekends the library is open from 10 am to 6 pm.
- The library opens at 8 am and closes at 9 pm on weekdays.
Question: When does the library open on weekends?
Answer:
```

Each question's answer note ranks first, so recall@2 is 3/3. The scores stay well below 1 because each question and its note share only a few words. The lunch question scores only 0.31, since "lunch" is the one word it shares with its note. The last five lines are the prompt that would go to the language model, with the two retrieved notes above the question.

The code has a gap. A question that shares no word with any note, such as "When can I work out?", gets a vector of all zeros. Dividing by its length 0 makes NumPy return `nan` for every score. A dense embedding still gives that question a nonzero vector, and a well-trained one would place it near the note about the gym.

<Warning>

If you skip normalization and rank by raw dot products, long documents win
because they contain more words. A page that says "library" twenty times
beats the one sentence that answers the question. Divide every vector by its
length first, or use cosine similarity directly, so that only the direction
counts.

</Warning>

<Optional title="Searching millions of vectors">

Exact search compares the question with all $n$ stored vectors of dimension $d$, which costs $\mathcal{O}(nd)$ per question. With millions of chunks that is slow. **Approximate nearest-neighbor** search gives up a little accuracy for speed, since it may miss a few of the true top results. One method groups the vectors into clusters ahead of time and searches only the clusters nearest the question. Another, HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world), links each vector to its near neighbors and walks the links toward the question. The FAISS library implements both, and the RAG paper used FAISS with HNSW.

Many systems then **rerank**. A fast first stage retrieves a long list of candidates. A slower, more accurate model reads the question and each candidate together and scores the pair. The best few go into the prompt. In the BERT reranker of Nogueira and Cho (2019), the first stage was BM25 and the list was 1,000 passages long.

</Optional>
