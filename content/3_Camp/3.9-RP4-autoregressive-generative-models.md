## Overview

An autoregressive model represents a joint distribution as a sequence of conditional predictions. For images, audio, or discrete latent codes, we must choose an order in which components are generated.

This yields tractable likelihoods and simple ancestral sampling, while generation remains sequential. We will connect factorization order, teacher-forced training, decoding, and evaluation across different data types.

For four variables in raster order, write $p(x)=p(x_1)p(x_2\mid x_1)p(x_3\mid x_{1:2})p(x_4\mid x_{1:3})$. Training observes every true prefix (this is teacher forcing) and can score all four factors in parallel with a causal mask.

Sampling must first choose $x_1$, then condition on that chosen value for $x_2$, and continue. This sequential dependency is exact for the chosen factorization rather than an implementation accident.

## Language Modelling and Decoding

### Perplexity

The chain-rule factorization and the cross-entropy training objective are taught in [GPT and Causal Language Modeling](2.44-TF24-gpt-causal-lm.md). Its loss, $\mathcal{L}$, is the mean negative log-likelihood per token, and perplexity is $\exp(\mathcal{L})$:

$$
\boxed{\;\text{PPL} = \exp\!\left(-\frac1N\sum_{t}\log p(w_t\mid w_{<t})\right) = \exp(\mathcal{L})\;}
$$

**Interpretation: the effective branching factor.** If a model assigns uniform probability $1/k$ to $k$ equally likely continuations at every step, then $\mathcal{L} = \log k$ and $\text{PPL} = k$. So a perplexity of 20 means the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 20 options at each token.

Bounds: $\text{PPL} \ge 1$ (perfect prediction), and $\text{PPL} \le |V|$ for a model that never does worse than uniform. A uniform model over 50,000 tokens has $\text{PPL} = 50{,}000$. A good modern language model on English text scores 10–20. GPT-2 small scored about 37.5 on WikiText-103, and current large models are around 8–10.

**Perplexity is not comparable across tokenizers.** A character-level model has far lower perplexity per token than a word-level one simply because each decision is easier, with no implication about quality. Compare only within a fixed tokenization, or convert to bits per byte:

$$
\text{BPB} = \frac{\mathcal{L}\cdot N_{\text{tokens}}}{\ln 2\cdot N_{\text{bytes}}},
$$

which is tokenizer-independent and is what modern papers report.

### $n$-gram models and smoothing

Approximate the full history by the last $n-1$ tokens:

$$
p(w_t\mid w_{<t}) \approx p(w_t\mid w_{t-n+1},\dots,w_{t-1}),
\qquad
\hat p_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{C(w_{t-n+1:t})}{C(w_{t-n+1:t-1})}.
$$

**The zero problem.** Any $n$-gram not seen in training gets probability 0, so the whole sequence gets probability 0 and perplexity is infinite. With a 50k vocabulary there are $1.25\times10^{14}$ possible trigrams, so almost all of them are unseen no matter how large the corpus. This is not an edge case. It is the normal situation.

**Add-$k$ (Laplace) smoothing.**

$$
\hat p = \frac{C(w_{t-n+1:t}) + k}{C(w_{t-n+1:t-1}) + k|V|}.
$$

Simple and badly calibrated: with $|V| = 50{,}000$ and $k=1$, a context seen 10 times has its probability mass overwhelmingly reassigned to the 50,000 unseen continuations, since the denominator becomes $10 + 50{,}000$.

**Backoff and interpolation** are better. Interpolated:

$$
\hat p(w_t\mid w_{t-2},w_{t-1}) = \lambda_3\hat p_{\text{MLE}}(w_t\mid w_{t-2},w_{t-1}) + \lambda_2\hat p_{\text{MLE}}(w_t\mid w_{t-1}) + \lambda_1\hat p_{\text{MLE}}(w_t),
$$

with $\sum\lambda_i = 1$ fit on held-out data. Katz backoff instead falls back only when the higher-order count is zero, discounting the observed counts to free up mass.

**Kneser–Ney** is the best of the classical methods. Its insight: the unigram fallback should not be the raw frequency but the number of *distinct contexts* a word appears in. "Francisco" is frequent, but almost always after "San", so as a fallback prediction it should be rare. Absolute discounting subtracts a fixed $d$ from every count and redistributes it according to that continuation probability.

$n$-gram models are historical, but they make the sparsity problem concrete, and the reason neural models won is exactly that embeddings share statistical strength across similar contexts, so an unseen trigram is not a catastrophe.

### Decoding

Given $p(w_t\mid w_{<t})$, produce text. This is a search problem, and the choice of method changes the output more than most people expect. Greedy decoding, sampling, temperature, top-$k$ and top-$p$ are taught in [Generate](2.43-TF23-transformer-generate.md) and [Pre-training & Fine-tuning](3.14-NL3-pre-training-fine-tuning.md).

**Beam search.** Keep the $B$ highest-scoring partial sequences at each step. With length normalization:

$$
\text{score}(y) = \frac{1}{|y|^\alpha}\sum_t\log p(y_t\mid y_{<t}), \qquad \alpha\approx0.6\text{–}1.0 .
$$

Without normalization, beam search systematically prefers **short** sequences, because every additional token multiplies by a probability less than 1 and so lowers the total log-probability.

**The beam-search curse.** For open-ended generation, larger beams produce *worse* text: flatter, more repetitive, more generic. The reason: the most probable sequence under a language model is not the most human-like one. Human text is surprising, with a roughly constant information rate, and maximizing probability systematically selects for the bland. Beam search is right for translation and summarization, where there is a correct answer, and wrong for story generation and dialogue.

**Repetition penalties.** Divide the logits of already-generated tokens by a factor (1.1–1.2), or forbid repeating any $n$-gram. Necessary because greedy and beam decoding fall into loops: once a phrase repeats twice, its continuation becomes the highest-probability option, which is self-reinforcing.

### Failure modes

**Repetition loops.** Deterministic decoding degenerates into "I think that I think that I think..." The mechanism is self-reinforcing: the model's context now contains the repeated phrase, which raises the probability of continuing the pattern. Fixes: sampling, repetition penalty, or $n$-gram blocking.

**Exposure bias** is the standard failure of teacher-forced training, and is covered in [Pre-training & Fine-tuning](3.14-NL3-pre-training-fine-tuning.md).

### From Scratch

```python
import math
from collections import Counter, defaultdict
import numpy as np


def bits_per_byte(total_nll_nats, n_bytes):
    return total_nll_nats / (math.log(2) * n_bytes)


class NGramLM:
    """Interpolated smoothing: mix the trigram, bigram, and unigram estimates
    so an unseen high-order context falls back rather than giving probability 0."""

    def __init__(self, n=3, lambdas=(0.1, 0.3, 0.6)):
        self.n, self.lambdas = n, lambdas
        self.counts = [Counter() for _ in range(n)]
        self.contexts = [Counter() for _ in range(n)]
        self.vocab = set()

    def fit(self, tokens):
        self.vocab = set(tokens)
        for order in range(self.n):
            for i in range(order, len(tokens)):
                gram = tuple(tokens[i - order:i + 1])
                self.counts[order][gram] += 1
                self.contexts[order][gram[:-1]] += 1
        return self

    def prob(self, word, context):
        p = 0.0
        for order, lam in zip(range(self.n), self.lambdas):
            gram = tuple(context[len(context) - order:]) + (word,) if order else (word,)
            ctx = gram[:-1]
            c = self.counts[order][gram]
            d = self.contexts[order][ctx]
            p += lam * (c / d if d else 1.0 / max(len(self.vocab), 1))
        return p


def kneser_ney_continuation(counts):
    """The KN insight: a word's fallback probability should be the number of
    DISTINCT contexts it follows, not its raw frequency. 'Francisco' is common
    but only ever after 'San', so it is a poor fallback prediction."""
    contexts_per_word = defaultdict(set)
    for (prev, word) in counts:
        contexts_per_word[word].add(prev)
    total = sum(len(s) for s in contexts_per_word.values())
    return {w: len(s) / total for w, s in contexts_per_word.items()}


def beam_search(step_fn, start, beam=5, max_len=20, alpha=0.7, eos=None):
    """Length normalization matters: without dividing by |y|^alpha, every extra
    token lowers the total log-prob, so short sequences win automatically."""
    beams = [(0.0, list(start))]
    finished = []
    for _ in range(max_len):
        cands = []
        for score, seq in beams:
            if eos is not None and seq[-1] == eos:
                finished.append((score, seq))
                continue
            logp = np.log(step_fn(seq) + 1e-12)
            for tok in np.argsort(logp)[::-1][:beam]:
                cands.append((score + logp[tok], seq + [int(tok)]))
        if not cands:
            break
        beams = sorted(cands, key=lambda t: t[0] / len(t[1]) ** alpha, reverse=True)[:beam]
    finished += beams
    return max(finished, key=lambda t: t[0] / len(t[1]) ** alpha)[1]


def repetition_penalty(logits, generated, penalty=1.1):
    """Divide the logits of already-used tokens. Necessary because a repeated
    phrase raises its own continuation probability -- the loop is self-reinforcing."""
    logits = np.array(logits, float)
    for t in set(generated):
        logits[t] = logits[t] / penalty if logits[t] > 0 else logits[t] * penalty
    return logits

```

### Using the Library

```python
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM
import torch

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2")
inputs = tok("The capital of France is", return_tensors="pt")

# Nucleus sampling: the sensible default for open-ended generation.
out = model.generate(
    **inputs,
    do_sample=True, top_p=0.92, temperature=0.8,
    repetition_penalty=1.1, max_new_tokens=50,
)

# Beam search: right for translation/summarization, wrong for open-ended text.
out = model.generate(**inputs, num_beams=5, length_penalty=0.8,
                     no_repeat_ngram_size=3, early_stopping=True)

# Perplexity on a held-out text.
with torch.no_grad():
    loss = model(inputs.input_ids, labels=inputs.input_ids).loss
ppl = torch.exp(loss)
```

`generate` defaults to **greedy** (`do_sample=False`), so if your output looks flat and repetitive, check that first.

## Generative Model Families

### What a generative model can offer

Given samples $x_1,\dots,x_n \sim p_{\text{data}}$, learn a model you can sample from. Three things you might want:

- **Sampling**: draw new $x\sim p_\theta$.
- **Density evaluation**: compute $p_\theta(x)$ for a given $x$ (needed for anomaly detection, compression, model comparison).
- **Latent inference**: recover a $z$ explaining an observed $x$ (needed for editing and controllable generation).

| Family | Density | Sampling | Latents |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Autoregressive | exact | slow ($O(d)$ passes) | none |
| Flow | exact | fast (1 pass) | exact, invertible |
| VAE | lower bound | fast (1 pass) | approximate |
| GAN | none | fast (1 pass) | none (unless inverted) |
| Diffusion | bound (or exact ODE) | slow (many steps) | yes |
| Energy-based | unnormalized | slow (MCMC) | no |

**Explicit versus implicit.** Explicit models define $p_\theta(x)$ and optimize it. Implicit models (GANs) define only a sampling procedure: you can draw from them but never evaluate a density, which is both their freedom and their main limitation.

### Autoregressive models

Factor by the chain rule ([Probability and Bayes](/module/?id=probability-foundations)):

$$
p(x) = \prod_{i=1}^{d}p(x_i\mid x_{<i}).
$$

This is exact for **any** ordering. No approximation is being made. Each conditional is a small classification or regression problem, so the whole thing is trained by maximum likelihood with the same cross-entropy machinery as everything else.

**Advantages.** Exact likelihood. Stable training (it is just supervised learning). Best-in-class quality on discrete data: text and code are entirely dominated by this family.

**The disadvantage is fundamental.** Sampling requires $d$ sequential forward passes, and $d$ is the number of dimensions: 1024 for a short text, but $3\times256\times256 = 196{,}608$ for an image. PixelCNN generates one pixel at a time, which is why autoregressive image models lost to diffusion despite competitive likelihoods.

**Ordering matters for images and not for text.** Text has a canonical order. Pixels do not, and the raster-scan order used by PixelCNN means a pixel cannot condition on the one below it, which is a real modelling handicap. Models like PixelCNN++ and Image Transformer work around it partially, and VQ-based models sidestep it by autoregressing over a much shorter sequence of discrete codes.

### VAEs, and why their samples are blurry

Derived in [VAE](3.10-RP3-vae-encoder-decoder-generation.md). The ELBO:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big] - D_{\text{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\Vert\,p(z)\big).
$$

Three contributing reasons for blur, worth separating:

1. **The likelihood term is usually a Gaussian**, so $\log p(x\mid z) \propto -\lVert x-\hat x\rVert^2$. Squared error's optimum is the **conditional mean**, and averaging over all plausible images consistent with a latent gives a blur. This is the same fact as "the constant minimizing MSE is the mean" ([Loss Functions](/module/?id=loss-functions) §4).
2. **Forward KL is mode-covering** ([Information Theory](/module/?id=information-theory-core) §4). Maximum likelihood penalizes missing mass, so the model spreads out over everything rather than committing, including regions between modes where no data lives.
3. **The bound is loose.** $\log p(x) = \text{ELBO} + D_{\text{KL}}(q\Vert p(z\mid x))$, and the gap is the posterior approximation error. Optimizing a loose bound is not optimizing the likelihood.

**Fixes.** A perceptual or adversarial reconstruction loss (VAE-GAN) replaces the pixel Gaussian. A richer posterior (normalizing flows on $q$) tightens the bound. Discrete latents (VQ-VAE) avoid the Gaussian entirely.

**VQ-VAE** deserves separate mention because it is the bridge to modern systems. Quantize the encoder output to the nearest of $K$ learned codebook vectors:

$$
z_q = \arg\min_{e_k}\lVert z_e - e_k\rVert .
$$

The argmin has no gradient, so use the **straight-through estimator**: forward-pass through the quantizer, backward-pass as if it were the identity, $\nabla_{z_e} \approx \nabla_{z_q}$. The codebook is trained by a separate commitment loss. The result is a *discrete* latent grid that an autoregressive model or a diffusion model can then be trained over, which is the design behind many current image and video generators. Codebook vectors that no encoder output ever selects are called dead codes and waste capacity.

### The trilemma

| | High quality | Fast sampling | Coverage / likelihood |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Autoregressive | ✓ | ✗ ($O(d)$ passes) | ✓ exact |
| VAE | ✗ blurry | ✓ | ✓ bound |
| GAN | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ mode collapse, no likelihood |
| Flow | ~ | ✓ | ✓ exact |
| Diffusion | ✓ | ✗ (many steps) | ✓ bound |

Every row is missing something, and the current research frontier is precisely about buying back the missing corner: consistency models and distillation give diffusion fast sampling, and adversarial fine-tuning of diffusion models trades a little coverage for a lot of speed.

### From Scratch

```python
import numpy as np


def autoregressive_sample(conditional_fn, d, rng):
    """Exact by the chain rule -- and d sequential passes. That cost is the
    reason this family lost the image domain despite better likelihoods."""
    x = np.zeros(d)
    for i in range(d):
        x[i] = conditional_fn(x[:i], rng)
    return x


def straight_through_quantize(z_e, codebook):
    """VQ-VAE: argmin has no gradient, so forward through the quantizer and
    backward as if it were the identity."""
    d2 = ((z_e[:, None, :] - codebook[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
    idx = d2.argmin(1)
    z_q = codebook[idx]
    return z_q, idx, z_e + (z_q - z_e)          # the STE, in numpy form


def vq_losses(z_e, z_q, beta=0.25):
    """Codebook loss moves the codes to the encoder output. Commitment loss
    moves the encoder output to the codes."""
    codebook = ((z_q - z_e) ** 2).mean()        # in torch, stop the gradient on z_e
    commit = ((z_e - z_q) ** 2).mean()          # in torch, stop the gradient on z_q
    return codebook + beta * commit

```

### Using the Library

```python
import torch

# VQ-VAE quantization with the straight-through estimator.
def quantize(z_e, codebook):
    d = (z_e.pow(2).sum(-1, keepdim=True) - 2 * z_e @ codebook.T
         + codebook.pow(2).sum(-1))
    idx = d.argmin(-1)
    z_q = codebook[idx]
    return z_e + (z_q - z_e).detach(), idx     # STE: identity on the backward pass
```

### Worked Example

**Part A: sampling cost across families.** Generate one $256\times256$ RGB image, $d = 196{,}608$.

| family | forward passes | relative time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GAN | 1 | 1× |
| VAE | 1 | 1× |
| flow | 1 | ~2× (invertibility constrains the architecture) |
| diffusion (DDPM) | 1000 | 1000× |
| diffusion (DDIM, 50 steps) | 50 | 50× |
| autoregressive (PixelCNN) | 196,608 | 196,608× |

At 10 ms per forward pass, the GAN takes 10 ms, DDIM takes 0.5 s, and PixelCNN takes **33 minutes** per image. That is not a tuning problem. It is the chain rule's structure, and it is why autoregressive image generation was abandoned even while autoregressive *text* generation took over the world. 1000 tokens is 1000 passes, which is fine.

**Part B: the VQ-VAE shortcut.** Compress a $256\times256$ image to a $32\times32$ grid of discrete codes. The autoregressive model now runs over $32^2 = 1024$ positions instead of 196,608 (a 192× reduction), bringing image generation back into the range where sequential sampling is affordable.

That is the architecture behind DALL·E 1 and Parti: a VQ autoencoder for compression plus a strong sequence model over the codes. The blur problem of the VAE section does not arise because the decoder is trained with a perceptual and adversarial loss rather than a pixel Gaussian.

## Intuition

The factorization order determines which dependencies are easy to express at each step. Raster order gives nearby image pixels useful predecessors but treats future directions differently. Alternative orders or latent tokenizations change the modelling problem.
