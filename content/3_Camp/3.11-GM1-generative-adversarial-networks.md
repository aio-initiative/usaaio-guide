## The problem

VAE images are blurry, and the cause is the loss. It compares the output with one target image, pixel by pixel.

That works when there is one right answer. In generation there are many, because several different images are all plausible. For example, suppose LEFT, (1, 0, 1, 0), and RIGHT, (0, 1, 0, 1), are equally likely to be the target:

- A model that outputs LEFT is exactly right half the time and wrong in every pixel the other half, an average squared error of 0.5.
- A model that outputs the gray square (0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5) is off by 0.5 in every pixel either way, an average squared error of $0.5^2 = 0.25$.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/generative-problem-gan.svg" alt="A plot of the average squared error against the image the model outputs, from RIGHT through the gray blend to LEFT. The curve is a U shape: 0.5 at RIGHT, 0.5 at LEFT, and lowest, 0.25, at the gray blend in the middle.">
The pixel loss when LEFT and RIGHT are equally likely targets. It is lowest for the gray blend.
</Figure>

So the loss is lowest for the gray square, an image that is neither LEFT nor RIGHT and that is not in the training set. Any pixel loss prefers the blur. The model needs a loss that rewards looking real, not matching one target pixel by pixel.

## Earlier approaches

### VAEs

As in [part 2](2.13-RP1-representation-learning-autoencoders-vaes-latent-spaces.md#lesson-vae), the VAE's codes can be sampled, but its pixel loss averages the images that share a code.

### Pixel losses in general

The problem is not specific to VAEs. If a model must output either LEFT or RIGHT and each is equally likely, outputting LEFT has a mean squared error of 0 half the time and 1 the other half, 0.5 on average. Outputting the gray average has an error of 0.25 either way, so any pixel loss prefers the blur.

## Generator and discriminator

**Two networks.** A **generative adversarial network** (GAN) trains two networks against each other. The **generator** turns random numbers into an image. The **discriminator** looks at an image and outputs $D$, the probability that it is real rather than generated.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/generative-gan.svg" alt="Random numbers go into the generator, which produces a generated image. The generated image and a real image both go into the discriminator, which outputs D, the probability the image is real. A dashed arrow from the discriminator back to the generator is labeled: the generator is trained to raise D on its images.">
The two networks of a GAN. The discriminator's score is the generator's training signal.
</Figure>

**The discriminator's loss.** It learns to score real images high and generated ones low:

$$
\text{loss}_D = -\ln D(\text{real}) - \ln\big(1 - D(\text{generated})\big).
$$

For example, scores of 0.9 on a real image and 0.1 on a generated one give $-\ln 0.9 - \ln 0.9 = 0.21$. Scores of 0.5 on both, a pure guess, give 1.39.

**The generator's loss.** It learns to make the discriminator score its images high:

$$
\text{loss}_G = -\ln D(\text{generated}).
$$

A score of 0.1 gives a loss of 2.30, and a score of 0.9 gives 0.11. A blurry image is easy to spot as fake, so the generator is pushed toward sharp images.

**The game.** Both losses come from one number, the **value** $V = \ln D(\text{real}) + \ln\big(1 - D(\text{generated})\big)$, averaged over real and generated images. The discriminator is trained to raise $V$. The generator affects only the second term, and in the original formulation it is trained to lower $V$. For LEFT and the early generated image of the running example introduced below, $V = \ln 0.881 + \ln(1 - 0.018) = -0.145$, the discriminator's loss with its sign changed. The generator loss $-\ln D$ above has the same aim and learns faster at the start (see Common questions).

**Training in turns.** One step improves the discriminator with the generator held fixed, and the next improves the generator with the discriminator held fixed.

**Results and problems.** GANs gave the most realistic images for years. StyleGAN (2018) generated 1024 × 1024 faces and improved the state of the art on the standard automatic measure of generated-image quality, the Fréchet inception distance. But the two networks must stay balanced, so training is unstable, and the generator often suffers **mode collapse**: it finds a few images that fool the discriminator and produces only those.

### The best discriminator

**Pointwise.** Hold the generator fixed. For an image $x$, let $a = p_\text{data}(x)$ be its probability under the data and $b = p_g(x)$ its probability under the generator. The discriminator's score $d = D(x)$ adds $a \ln d + b \ln(1 - d)$ to $V$, which is largest where its slope $a/d - b/(1 - d)$ is 0:

$$
D^*(x) = \frac{p_\text{data}(x)}{p_\text{data}(x) + p_g(x)}.
$$

In the running example the data is LEFT or RIGHT, half the time each. A collapsed generator that always makes LEFT gives $D^*(\text{LEFT}) = 0.5 \div (0.5 + 1) = 0.333$ and $D^*(\text{RIGHT}) = 0.5 \div (0.5 + 0) = 1$.

| The generator makes | $D^*$(LEFT) | $D^*$(RIGHT) | $V$ at $D^*$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| LEFT always | 0.333 | 1 | −0.955 |
| LEFT 3 times in 4 | 0.4 | 0.667 | −1.319 |
| LEFT and RIGHT, half each | 0.5 | 0.5 | −1.386 |

<Figure src="assets/module-media/generative-best-discriminator.svg" alt="Grouped bars for LEFT and RIGHT. For LEFT: the data probability 0.5, the generator's probability 1, and the best discriminator's score 0.333. For RIGHT: the data probability 0.5, the generator's probability 0, and the best discriminator's score 1, drawn darkest.">
The best discriminator against a generator that always makes LEFT. It distrusts LEFT and fully trusts RIGHT.
</Figure>

**The chase.** Against the collapsed generator, the best discriminator distrusts LEFT and fully trusts RIGHT, so the generator gains by switching to RIGHT. The discriminator then turns against RIGHT, and the two can chase each other between the images without the generator ever making both. In training, the discriminator takes only a few steps toward $D^*$ before the generator moves, so the generator chases a moving target. The related pathology is **oscillation**: $G$ collapses to mode A, $D$ learns to reject A, $G$ moves to mode B, $D$ learns B and forgets A, $G$ returns to A. The pair cycles indefinitely without converging, which is a generic behavior of simultaneous gradient descent on a minimax objective rather than a bug.

**What the generator minimizes.** With $D^*$ in place, adding and subtracting $\ln 2$ inside each logarithm gives

$$
V = \mathbb{E}_\text{data}\Big[\ln \frac{p_\text{data}}{p_\text{data} + p_g}\Big] + \mathbb{E}_{g}\Big[\ln \frac{p_g}{p_\text{data} + p_g}\Big] = -\ln 4 + 2\,\text{JSD}(p_\text{data} \,\|\, p_g).
$$

The **Jensen-Shannon divergence** $\text{JSD} = \tfrac{1}{2}\text{KL}(p_\text{data} \,\|\, m) + \tfrac{1}{2}\text{KL}(p_g \,\|\, m)$ compares each distribution with their average $m$, and it is 0 only when the two are equal. So the best generator reproduces the data, where $V = -\ln 4 = -1.386$ and $D^* = 0.5$ everywhere. For the collapsed generator, $V = 0.5 \ln(1/3) + 0.5 \ln 1 + \ln(2/3) = -0.955$ and $\text{JSD} = 0.216$, so $-1.386 + 2 \times 0.216 = -0.954$, the same up to rounding.

### The Wasserstein distance

JSD cannot always be improved. If the real and generated images never overlap, the two supports are disjoint and $\text{JSD} = \ln 2$ however far apart they are, so its gradient is 0. That is the usual situation, not a curiosity: a generator that turns a few hundred random numbers into a million pixels generally lands on a surface the real images never touch. In one dimension, with real data at $x = 0$ and generated data at $x = \theta$, both point masses, the mixture $m$ puts half its mass at each point and

$$
\text{JSD} = \tfrac12\Big(\ln\tfrac{1}{1/2}\Big) + \tfrac12\Big(\ln\tfrac{1}{1/2}\Big) = \ln 2
$$

for every $\theta \ne 0$. At $\theta = 10$ and at $\theta = 0.001$ the divergence is identical, so $\partial\text{JSD}/\partial\theta = 0$ and gradient descent has no idea which way to move. The **Wasserstein-1 distance**, the average distance the mass has to be moved, is $W = \lvert\theta\rvert$, whose derivative is $\operatorname{sign}(\theta)$, so descending it always moves $\theta$ toward 0. That single example is the whole argument for the Wasserstein GAN (WGAN).

### Conditional GANs

**Conditioning on a label.** Giving both networks a label $y$, as $G(z, y)$ and $D(x, y)$, makes the discriminator judge pairs: is this a real image of class $y$? The best discriminator is the same formula with every probability conditioned on $y$. In the running example, with $y$ naming LEFT or RIGHT, the data given "right" is always RIGHT, so a generator that answers "right" with LEFT meets $D^*(\text{LEFT}, \text{right}) = 0 \div (0 + 1) = 0$ and is caught at once. The label is usually appended to the generator's random numbers and to the discriminator's input. Conditioning does not prevent mode collapse within a class.

## Running example

### The discriminator

Midway through training, the discriminator has learned one thing about real images: their top row equals their bottom row. It adds up how far the rows differ, the **mismatch**, and gives the score D = sigmoid(2 − 6 × mismatch).

| Image | Mismatch | D (probability real) | Generator loss, −ln D |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early generated (0.6, 0.3, 0.1, 0.8) | 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0 | 0.018 | 4.018 |
| Later generated (0.7, 0.3, 0.6, 0.4) | 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.2 | 0.690 | 0.371 |
| LEFT (1, 0, 1, 0), real | 0 | 0.881 | 0.127 |

### The two losses

The generator's loss falls as its rows come to match. The discriminator's own loss, on LEFT and the early generated image, is −ln 0.881 − ln(1 − 0.018) = 0.145, low because it tells them apart easily.

### Mode collapse

Mode collapse is easy to see here. If the generator outputs LEFT for every random input, every image scores 0.881, as high as a real one, but RIGHT never appears. A sharp sample grid can hide it. Mitigations: **minibatch discrimination** (let $D$ see statistics across the batch, so it can detect that all samples are identical), **unrolled GANs** (differentiate through $D$'s future updates so $G$ cannot exploit a momentary weakness), and **WGAN-GP**.

```python
import numpy as np

def discriminator(x):
    mismatch = abs(x[0] - x[2]) + abs(x[1] - x[3])
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(2 - 6 * mismatch)))

for x in ([0.6, 0.3, 0.1, 0.8], [0.7, 0.3, 0.6, 0.4], [1, 0, 1, 0]):
    d = discriminator(x)
    print(x, round(d, 3), round(-np.log(d), 3))
```

### The two losses in PyTorch

The two losses above, in the code a contestant actually writes:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def d_loss(d_real, d_fake):
    return (F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(d_real, torch.ones_like(d_real))
            + F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(d_fake, torch.zeros_like(d_fake)))

def g_loss(d_fake):
    # MAXIMIZE log D(G(z)), not minimize log(1 - D(G(z))).
    return F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(d_fake, torch.ones_like(d_fake))
```

The DCGAN tutorial this lesson links uses Adam with `betas=(0.5, 0.999)` for both networks, following the DCGAN paper. The first moment can be reduced further, toward 0, if the losses oscillate, since momentum on a moving target can amplify the oscillation above.

## Common questions

**When is training finished?** In theory, when the generated images are as good as real ones and the discriminator can only guess, giving 0.5 to everything. In practice the two losses swing up and down and never settle, so people stop by looking at the images. What matters is that it is **stable and away from zero**: a discriminator loss falling toward 0 means it is telling real from generated apart easily, and the generator is losing. A **Wasserstein GAN** (WGAN) trains the discriminator to estimate the distance above instead of winning the game, and its loss is a meaningful learning curve, useful for debugging and hyperparameter searches, where the original GAN's losses are not: they only report who is currently winning.

**Why is the generator's loss $-\ln D$?** It gives a strong signal when the generator is losing badly. The discriminator computes $D = \text{sigmoid}(a)$ from a score $a$, and the generator learns through the slope with respect to $a$. For the early image, $D = 0.018$ and $-\ln D = 4.018$. The slope of $-\ln D$ is $-(1 - D) = -0.982$, while the slope of the original $\ln(1 - D)$ is $-D = -0.018$, 55 times smaller. Both losses have the same best generator.

**Does the generator ever see a real image?** No. It only receives the discriminator's score for its own images. Everything it learns about real images reaches it through the discriminator.

**Why does the real image score only 0.881?** This discriminator is a simple formula that gives 0.881 to any image whose rows match. A trained one would move toward 1 for real images.
