## Weighted sum

### The problem

After part 2, each token has a weight for every token it can see. For "sat" in head 1 the weights are 0.115 for "the", 0.709 for "cat" and 0.175 for itself. Each of those tokens also has a value from [Q, K, V](2.34-TF14-transformer-qkv.md#lesson-q-k-v).

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-problem-weighted-sum.svg" alt="A bar chart of the weights of sat on three tokens: 0.115 on the, 0.709 on cat and 0.175 on sat. Under each bar is the value of that token: (1.000, 1.000) for the, (0.841, 1.540) for cat and (0.909, -0.416) for sat.">
What "sat" has in head 1 after part 2: three weights and three values, and no result yet.
</Figure>

The weights say how much each token matters, but a weight is not information. "sat" still has to receive something. The three values have to be combined into one new vector for "sat" that carries what it found. The way of combining has to meet three needs:

- **Fixed size.** The result is the same size whether the token looked at 3 others or 3,000.
- **Follows the weights.** A token with a weight of 0.709 should count for far more than one with 0.115.
- **Smooth.** A small change in a weight gives a small change in the result, so that training can adjust it.

### Earlier approaches

#### Copying one token

Copying only the highest-weighted token ignores the rest and cannot be trained: "sat" would take the value of "cat" and drop everything else.

#### The context vector

Bahdanau et al. (2014) combined a translation model's states by a weighted sum, called a context vector: when translating "the black cat" into French, the word "chat" draws mostly on "cat". The Transformer keeps this idea.

### The weighted sum

**One token.** Each token's new vector is the **weighted average** of the values of the tokens it can see. Multiply each value by its weight and add:

$$
\text{output} = a_1 v_1 + a_2 v_2 + \dots
$$

where $a_j$ is the weight on token $j$ and $v_j$ is its value. For example, weights of 0.5 and 0.5 on the values (2, 0) and (0, 2) give 0.5 × (2, 0) + 0.5 × (0, 2) = (1, 1).

**What the weights do.** If one weight is close to 1, the output is close to that token's value: weights of 0.9 and 0.1 on (2, 0) and (0, 2) give (1.8, 0.2). If the weights are equal, the output is the plain average. Because the weights are positive and add up to 1, the output always lies between the values.

The statement that the output lies between the values applies to the averaging operation, rather than to the full transformer block. Later projections, residual additions, and feed-forward layers transform this result further, so the block as a whole is not confined to the hull of the values.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-weighted-sum.svg" alt="A plot of three arrows from the origin for the head 1 values of the, cat and sat: (1.000, 1.000) with weight 0.115, (0.841, 1.540) with weight 0.709 and (0.909, -0.416) with weight 0.175. A heavier arrow shows the output for sat, (0.872, 1.135), which lies between the three values and is built mostly from the value of cat.">
Head 1 of the running example. The output for "sat" lies between the three values, and the value of "cat", which has the largest weight, contributes the most to it.
</Figure>

**Every token at once.** With the weights as the rows of $A$ and the values as the rows of $V$, the outputs for all tokens are one matrix multiplication, $A V$. Queries, keys and values, the scores, the mask and softmax, and this weighted sum together are called **scaled dot-product attention**. In one line, with the mask from part 2 applied before the softmax:

$$
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V,
$$

where $d_k$ is the head size, 2 in the running example. For head 1, the softmax gives the 3 × 3 table of weights from [Mask and softmax](2.34-TF14-transformer-qkv.md#lesson-mask-and-softmax), and $V$ is the 3 × 2 table of values from [Q, K, V](2.34-TF14-transformer-qkv.md#lesson-q-k-v), so the output is a 3 × 2 table with one row per token.

#### Gradients

**One score.** Training changes attention through its scores. When the score $s_j$ of token $j$ rises by a small amount, its weight rises and the others fall (see [Mask and softmax](2.34-TF14-transformer-qkv.md#lesson-mask-and-softmax)), and the output moves toward that token's value:

$$
\frac{\partial \, \text{output}}{\partial s_j} = a_j \, (v_j - \text{output}).
$$

The move is large when the token already has a large weight and its value lies far from the output. For "sat" in head 1, whose output is (0.872, 1.135):

| Score of | Weight | Value − output | Weight × (value − output) |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | 0.115 | (0.128, −0.135) | (0.015, −0.016) |
| cat | 0.709 | (−0.031, 0.405) | (−0.022, 0.287) |
| sat | 0.175 | (0.037, −1.551) | (0.006, −0.271) |

Raising the score of "cat" pulls the output up, toward the value of "cat". Raising the score of "sat" pulls it down, toward the value of "sat". The three moves add up to (0, 0), because raising every score by the same amount changes no weight. Added up, the rows of the table give (−0.001, 0.000) rather than (0, 0), because each is rounded. At full precision they cancel exactly.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-gradient.svg" alt="A plot of the output for sat in head 1, at (0.872, 1.135), with dashed lines to the values of cat at (0.841, 1.540) and the at (1.000, 1.000), and a dashed line down toward the value of sat at (0.909, -0.416), below the frame. Two arrows start at the output and are drawn to scale: one labeled score of cat rises points up along the line to cat, and one labeled score of sat rises points down along the line to sat. The arrow for the is too short to see.">
How fast the output for "sat" in head 1 moves as each score rises. Each arrow points toward its token's value.
</Figure>

**The whole table.** Training starts from $dO$, how much the training loss (see [Predict](2.42-TF22-transformer-predict.md#lesson-predict)) changes with each number of the output, one row per token. Working back through $O = AV$, the softmax and $S = QK^\top / \sqrt{d_k}$:

$$
dV = A^\top dO, \qquad dS = A \odot \left(dO \, V^\top - r\right), \qquad dQ = \frac{dS \, K}{\sqrt{d_k}}, \qquad dK = \frac{dS^\top Q}{\sqrt{d_k}}.
$$

Here $\odot$ multiplies matching entries, and $r$ is a column with one number per row: the weighted average of that row of $dO \, V^\top$, using the row's weights, subtracted from every entry of the row. The $dS$ formula is the rule for one score applied to every row at once. For example, let the loss be the first number of the output of "sat", so $dO$ is 0 except for (1, 0) in the row of "sat". Then $dV$ gives the three values 0.115, 0.709 and 0.175 times (1, 0): each value is blamed in proportion to its weight. In the row of "sat", $dO \, V^\top$ holds the first number of each value, 1.000, 0.841 and 0.909, and their weighted average is the first number of the output, 0.872 (the rounded weights and values shown give 0.870). Subtracting 0.872 and multiplying by the weights gives 0.115 × 0.128 = 0.015, 0.709 × (−0.031) = −0.022 and 0.175 × 0.037 = 0.006, the first numbers of the table above.

<ProblemCard id="tf-which-value-takes-the-blame" />

**Only the outputs.** The weighted average subtracted in each row equals the dot product of that token's row of $dO$ with its row of the output, because the output is itself the weighted average of the values. In the example it is (1, 0) · (0.872, 1.135) = 0.872. So it can be found from $O$ and $dO$ alone, before any weight is known. [Efficient Transformers: FlashAttention](4.11-TF29-efficient-flashattention.md#lesson-flashattention) relies on this: it stores no table of weights, and in the backward pass it recomputes them a block at a time.

### Running example

#### One token

For "sat" in head 1, the weights from part 2 are 0.115, 0.709 and 0.175, and the values of head 1 come from [Q, K, V](2.34-TF14-transformer-qkv.md#lesson-q-k-v):

| Token | Weight | Value | Weight × value |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | 0.115 | (1.000, 1.000) | (0.115, 0.115) |
| cat | 0.709 | (0.841, 1.540) | (0.596, 1.092) |
| sat | 0.175 | (0.909, −0.416) | (0.159, −0.073) |
| | | **Sum** | **(0.870, 1.134)** |

At full precision it is (0.872, 1.135), mostly the value of "cat".

#### Every token in head 1

| Token | Head 1 output |
|---|---|
| the | (1.000, 1.000) |
| cat | (0.881, 1.405) |
| sat | (0.872, 1.135) |

#### Head 2

| Token | Weight | Value | Weight × value |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | 0.124 | (0.000, 1.000) | (0.000, 0.124) |
| cat | 0.105 | (1.010, 1.000) | (0.106, 0.105) |
| sat | 0.771 | (1.020, 2.000) | (0.786, 1.542) |
| | | **Sum** | **(0.892, 1.771)** |

At full precision it is (0.893, 1.771), close to the value of "sat" itself.

| Token | Head 2 output |
|---|---|
| the | (0.000, 1.000) |
| cat | (0.649, 1.000) |
| sat | (0.893, 1.771) |

```python
import numpy as np

A1 = np.array([[1.000, 0, 0], [0.250, 0.750, 0], [0.115, 0.709, 0.175]])
V1 = np.array([[1.000, 1.000], [0.841, 1.540], [0.909, -0.416]])

O1 = A1 @ V1
print(O1.round(3))   # last digits differ slightly: the inputs are rounded
```

The gradient formulas can be checked against small nudges to each input, a check worth running on any backward pass written by hand:

```python
import numpy as np

Q1 = np.array([[0.000, 1.000], [1.010, 1.000], [1.020, 2.000]])
K1 = np.array([[1.000, 0.000], [1.540, 1.010], [-0.416, 1.020]])
V1 = np.array([[1.000, 1.000], [0.841, 1.540], [0.909, -0.416]])
hidden = np.triu(np.ones((3, 3), dtype=bool), k=1)

def attention(Q, K, V):
    S = np.where(hidden, -np.inf, Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(2))
    A = np.exp(S - S.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    A /= A.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return A @ V, A

O, A = attention(Q1, K1, V1)
dO = np.zeros((3, 2))
dO[2, 0] = 1                     # loss: first number of the output of "sat"
dV = A.T @ dO
dS = A * (dO @ V1.T - (dO * O).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))
dQ, dK = dS @ K1 / np.sqrt(2), dS.T @ Q1 / np.sqrt(2)

def nudged(X, h=1e-6):
    """The same gradient from small nudges: raise and lower each number of X."""
    G = np.zeros_like(X)
    for i in np.ndindex(X.shape):
        X[i] += h
        up = attention(Q1, K1, V1)[0][2, 0]
        X[i] -= 2 * h
        down = attention(Q1, K1, V1)[0][2, 0]
        X[i] += h
        G[i] = (up - down) / (2 * h)
    return G

print([np.allclose(G, nudged(X)) for X, G in ((Q1, dQ), (K1, dK), (V1, dV))])
# [True, True, True]
```

### Common questions

**Does the output replace the token's vector?** No. It is what the token gathered from the others. [Residual and LayerNorm](2.39-TF19-transformer-residual-and-layernorm.md#lesson-residual-and-layernorm) adds it to the token's own vector, so nothing the token already had is lost.

**Why is the output of "the" just its own value?** "the" has a weight of 1 on itself and 0 on everything else, so its weighted sum is its own value: (1.000, 1.000) in head 1.

**Can the output be larger than every value?** No. The weights are positive and add up to 1, so each number of the output lies between the smallest and the largest of the values. In head 1 the first numbers of the values are 1.000, 0.841 and 0.909, and the output has 0.872.

**Why do the hand sums differ in the last digit?** The weights and values shown are rounded to three decimals. Adding the rounded products gives (0.870, 1.134), and full precision gives (0.872, 1.135).

The next section, [Multi-head](#lesson-multi-head), combines the two heads.

## Multi-head

### The problem

Attention so far gives each token one set of weights and one result. But a token often needs several things at once.

To predict what follows "the cat sat", the model needs the subject, "cat", and also the verb, "sat" itself. With one set of weights, the token has to split its attention between them. If it puts 0.5 on each, it gets the average of their two values, and an average is not either one. For example, if the value of "cat" were (1, 0) and the value of "sat" (0, 1), the result would be (0.5, 0.5).

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-problem-multi-head.svg" alt="Two arrows from the origin: the value of cat, (1, 0), along the horizontal axis, and the value of sat, (0, 1), along the vertical axis. A heavier arrow between them points to (0.5, 0.5), labeled weights 0.5 and 0.5, which is neither.">
One set of weights split between two tokens returns their average, which matches neither value.
</Figure>

Putting all the weight on one of them returns that value exactly and loses the other. What the token needs is to look at "cat" fully and at "sat" fully, at the same time, and to keep the two results apart.

### Earlier approaches

Attention before the Transformer used a single head. Making that head bigger does not help, because it still has only one set of weights per token: if it splits its weight evenly between "cat" and "sat", it returns the average of their values, not either one.

### Multi-head attention

**Several heads.** **Multi-head attention** runs several heads side by side. Each head has its own queries, keys and values, so each can look for something different. For example, one set of weights split evenly between the values (1, 0) and (0, 1) returns (0.5, 0.5), which matches neither. Two heads can return (1, 0) and (0, 1) separately.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-multi-head.svg" alt="Two rows, one per head, each showing the weights of 'sat' on 'the', 'cat' and 'sat'. In head 1 the weights are 0.115, 0.709 and 0.175, with cat outlined, and the head output is (0.872, 1.135). In head 2 they are 0.124, 0.105 and 0.771, with sat outlined, and the head output is (0.893, 1.771). Arrows join the two outputs into one row of four numbers, the row of sat in M: 0.872, 1.135, 0.893, 1.771.">
The two heads of the running example for "sat". Head 1 attends to "cat", head 2 to "sat" itself, and their outputs are placed side by side.
</Figure>

**Putting the heads together.** The outputs of the heads are placed side by side, which gives each token $d$ numbers again, and then multiplied by one more learned table of weights, the **output projection** $W_O$:

$$
M = [\,O_1 \;\; O_2 \;\; \dots\,] \; W_O.
$$

**Shapes.** Each of $W_Q$, $W_K$ and $W_V$ is a $d \times d$ table, and with $h$ heads, each head uses its own $d_k = d / h$ of the columns. For a text of $T$ tokens:

| Table | Shape | Running example | GPT-2 small |
|---|---|---|---|
| Input $H$ | $T \times d$ | 3 × 4 | up to 1,024 × 768 |
| $W_Q$, $W_K$, $W_V$, each | $d \times d$ | 4 × 4 | 768 × 768 |
| One head's columns of $W_Q$ | $d \times d_k$ | 4 × 2 | 768 × 64 |
| One head's queries, keys or values | $T \times d_k$ | 3 × 2 | up to 1,024 × 64 |
| One head's scores and weights | $T \times T$ | 3 × 3 | up to 1,024 × 1,024 |
| One head's output | $T \times d_k$ | 3 × 2 | up to 1,024 × 64 |
| The heads side by side | $T \times d$ | 3 × 4 | up to 1,024 × 768 |
| $W_O$ | $d \times d$ | 4 × 4 | 768 × 768 |
| $M$ | $T \times d$ | 3 × 4 | up to 1,024 × 768 |

**Cost.** Splitting into heads costs nothing extra. Twelve heads of 64 numbers use the same number of weights as one head of 768, because 12 × 64 = 768. The four tables hold $4d^2$ weights whatever the number of heads: 4 × 16 = 64 in the running example, and 4 × 768 × 768 = 2,359,296 in GPT-2 small, which with 3,072 offsets is the 2,362,368 counted in [Stack](2.39-TF19-transformer-residual-and-layernorm.md#lesson-stack). Computing the scores costs the same too: the running example's 2 heads each compute 9 scores of 2 products, 2 × 9 × 2 = 36 multiplications, as many as one head of 4 numbers, 9 × 4 = 36.

<ProblemCard id="tf-one-wide-head" />

**Thin heads.** The price of splitting is that each head compares fewer numbers. A head of one number can only prefer the largest or the smallest key. In the first number of head 1's keys, "the" has 1.000, "cat" 1.540 and "sat" −0.416. A positive query puts "cat" first, a negative one puts "sat" first, and no query puts "the" first. With two numbers it can: against the keys (1.000, 0.000), (1.540, 1.010) and (−0.416, 1.020), the query (1, −1) gives dot products of 1.000, 0.530 and −1.436, which divided by $\sqrt{2} = 1.4142$ are scores of 0.707, 0.375 and −1.015, so "the" comes first. Real models keep heads of 64 or 128 numbers.

**What heads learn.** In trained models, heads specialize (Voita et al., 2019): the most important and confident heads play consistent and often linguistically-interpretable roles.

### Running example

#### Several heads

The two heads look at different tokens for "sat":

| Head | the | cat | sat | Output for "sat" |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.115 | **0.709** | 0.175 | (0.872, 1.135) |
| 2 | 0.124 | 0.105 | **0.771** | (0.893, 1.771) |

Head 1 finds the subject. Head 2, whose queries and keys are identical, stays on the token itself, the flaw described in [Q, K, V](2.34-TF14-transformer-qkv.md#lesson-q-k-v). Placing the two outputs side by side gives each token four numbers again.

#### Putting the heads together

| Token | Head 1, first | Head 1, second | Head 2, first | Head 2, second |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| the | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.000 | 1.000 |
| cat | 0.881 | 1.405 | 0.649 | 1.000 |
| sat | 0.872 | 1.135 | 0.893 | 1.771 |

The running example's output projection leaves this unchanged, so this is the attention output, $M$. A trained projection would mix the two halves.

```python
import numpy as np

O1 = np.array([[1.000, 1.000], [0.881, 1.405], [0.872, 1.135]])
O2 = np.array([[0.000, 1.000], [0.649, 1.000], [0.893, 1.771]])
W_O = np.eye(4)                       # output projection

M = np.concatenate([O1, O2], axis=1) @ W_O
print(M.round(3))
```

### Common questions

**How many heads does a real model have?** GPT-2 small has 12 heads of 64 numbers in each of its 12 blocks. The width is fixed, so more heads means smaller heads: 768 numbers can be 12 heads of 64 or 6 heads of 128.

**Is each head told what to look for?** No. Every head starts with random weights, and what it does comes out of training. Some heads end up nearly redundant: Michel et al. (2019) found that a large percentage of a trained model's heads can be removed at test time with little effect on performance, and that some layers can even be reduced to a single head.

**Do the heads see each other's results?** Not inside one attention layer. They run side by side on the same input. Their outputs are mixed afterwards by the output projection, and later blocks can build on all of them.

**Why does head 2 attend to "sat" itself?** Its queries and keys were set to be identical in [Q, K, V](2.34-TF14-transformer-qkv.md#lesson-q-k-v). A token then tends to match itself, so head 2 gives "sat" 0.771 of its own weight. A trained head would not be built this way.

[Part 4](2.39-TF19-transformer-residual-and-layernorm.md#lesson-residual-and-layernorm) adds $M$ back to the block's input.
