## Overview

Labels are expensive and unlabeled data is nearly free. **Semi-supervised learning** starts from a small labeled set and a large unlabeled set from the same distribution, and makes the unlabeled examples carry some of the load. Two classical methods are within reach of any contestant: **pseudo-labeling** on top of any classifier, and **label propagation** on top of a neighborhood graph.

[2026 Round 2, Day 2](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-2-day-2-problems.pdf), Problem 4 handed out 200 labeled images and 800 unlabeled ones, banned pretrained weights of any kind, and scored by macro-F1. That is the setting pseudo-labeling was made for, and the explanation measures how the loop fails when it trusts itself too much.

## Example: five points, one shortcut

Five points form a graph with the edges $1\!-\!2$, $2\!-\!3$, $3\!-\!4$, $4\!-\!5$ and a shortcut $2\!-\!4$, all of weight 1. Point 1 is labeled $A$ and point 5 is labeled $B$. Label propagation gives every unlabeled point the weighted average of its neighbors' values, with the labeled points held fixed. What probability of class $A$ does each of points 2, 3 and 4 end with, which label does each receive, and what does the value at point 3 say? And if a random walk from point 2 moves to a neighbor with probability proportional to the edge weight, how likely is it to reach point 1 before point 5?

<NotebookCard id="semi-supervised-learning" />

Three unknowns and three equations, and the answer has a meaning that survives to graphs with a thousand points.

## Explanation

### What unlabeled data can tell you

An unlabeled point carries no information about its own label. A pile of them helps only through an assumption connecting the shape of the data to the labels. The standard one is the **cluster assumption**, also called the low-density assumption. Points in the same dense cluster tend to share a label, so a good decision boundary passes through sparse regions, not through the middle of a cluster. Unlabeled data then maps where the clusters are. Six labeled points cannot show the shape of two interleaved crescents. The 994 unlabeled ones trace both completely, and the labels only say which is which.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/label-propagation-moons.svg" alt="Two panels of the two-moons dataset. Left: the 1000 points of the two-moons dataset, 994 gray unlabeled and six starred labeled. Right: every point colored by label propagation.">
Six labels, 994 unlabeled points: label propagation colors both crescents from the data's own shape.
</Figure>

The assumption can be false. If the two classes share the same density, two identical overlapping Gaussians split by a noisy boundary, the data's shape says nothing about the labels and no amount of unlabeled data helps. Worse, a method that trusts the cluster structure will confidently learn the wrong boundary.

### Pseudo-labeling and confirmation bias

Pseudo-labeling, or self-training, is the obvious idea:

1. Train a classifier on the labeled set.
2. Predict on the unlabeled set and keep the most confident predictions.
3. Add them, with their predicted **pseudo-labels**, to the training set. Retrain and repeat.

The obvious version fails. The implementation runs it on the digits with 4 labels per class, 40 in all. The supervised SVM scores 0.853 on held-out data. Absorb the 100 globally most confident predictions each round and after ten rounds the score is 0.695, with 308 wrong pseudo-labels among the 1,040 training rows. Over eight seeds the naive loop ends below the supervised baseline every time, averaging 0.668 against 0.841, and on the worst seed it falls to 0.457.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/pseudo-label-rounds.svg" alt="Test accuracy by round for two pseudo-labeling loops starting from the same 40 labels. The naive loop falls from 0.853 to 0.695 over ten rounds. The careful loop rises to 0.937 over fifteen rounds. A dashed line marks the supervised score.">
The same classifier, the same 40 labels. The naive loop absorbs its own mistakes and sinks. The careful loop climbs past the supervised score.
</Figure>

The mechanism is **confirmation bias**. A confident prediction is not a correct prediction. Once a wrong pseudo-label enters the training set it looks like ground truth. The retrained model learns the error and grows confident about more like it. Global most-confident selection makes it worse: easy classes flood the quota and tilt the class balance, and the most confident points sit deep in regions the model already knows, so they add little. Two cheap fixes make the same loop work:

- **Per-class quotas.** Each round, take at most $k$ new points *per predicted class*, not $k$ overall.
- **A confidence floor.** Only absorb predictions whose margin clears a threshold. When nothing clears it, stop.

With a quota of 8 per class and a margin floor of 0.5, the same seed climbs to 0.937 after fifteen rounds, with 103 wrong pseudo-labels among 1,166 rows. Over the eight seeds the careful loop averages 0.891 and never ends below the supervised baseline.

### Label propagation

Pseudo-labeling trusts a model's outputs. Label propagation trusts the data's geometry directly. Build a similarity graph over all points, labeled and unlabeled, connecting near neighbors and weighting edges by closeness, with a kNN graph or an RBF kernel $w_{ij} = e^{-\gamma \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert^2}$. Then let each labeled point push its label along the edges until the assignment stops changing. Labels flow through dense regions and stall across sparse gaps, which is the cluster assumption turned into an algorithm.

The update rule is short. Normalize each row of the weight matrix, $T = D^{-1} W$ with $D$ the diagonal of row sums, so row $i$ holds the fractions of point $i$'s total similarity going to each neighbor. Put the labels in a matrix $F$ with one row per point and one column per class, one-hot for the labeled points and zero elsewhere. Then repeat two steps:

1. $F \leftarrow TF$: every point takes the similarity-weighted average of its neighbors' label distributions.
2. Reset the labeled rows to their one-hot labels, **clamping** the known answers.

At the fixed point nothing changes, so splitting the points into labeled $L$ and unlabeled $U$ gives $F_U = T_{UU} F_U + T_{UL} Y_L$, a linear system with the closed form

$$
F_U = (I - T_{UU})^{-1} T_{UL} Y_L .
$$

For the example, with $f_1 = 1$ and $f_5 = 0$ fixed, each unlabeled point equals the average of its neighbors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_2 &= \frac{1 + f_3 + f_4}{3}, \\[4pt]
f_3 &= \frac{f_2 + f_4}{2}, \\[4pt]
f_4 &= \frac{f_2 + f_3 + 0}{3} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Reversing the path swaps the two labels and the points 2 and 4 while fixing point 3, so $f_3 = 1/2$ and $f_4 = 1 - f_2$. Substituting, $3 f_2 = 1 + 1/2 + 1 - f_2$, so $f_2 = 5/8$ and $f_4 = 3/8$. Point 2 goes to $A$ and point 4 to $B$. Point 3 sits at exactly $1/2$: the graph is symmetric about it, so neither label is favored, and an implementation breaks the tie by whichever class comes first. The implementation reaches the same fixed point by iterating, after 98 sweeps at a tolerance of $10^{-12}$, and by the linear solve.

Each unlabeled row of $F_U$ is a probability distribution with a clean meaning. Start a random walk at point $i$ that moves to neighbor $j$ with probability $T_{ij}$. Entry $c$ of row $i$ is the probability that the walk reaches a labeled point of class $c$ before a labeled point of any other class. So a walk from point 2 reaches point 1 first with probability $f_2 = 5/8 = 0.625$, and 100,000 simulated walks give 0.623. Labels win the points they can reach through dense regions, which is the cluster assumption in one sentence.

scikit-learn ships `LabelPropagation` and `LabelSpreading` in `sklearn.semi_supervised`, with unlabeled points marked −1. `LabelSpreading` uses the symmetric normalization $D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}$ and, instead of clamping, blends toward the original labels each step, $F \leftarrow \alpha S F + (1 - \alpha) Y$, so a mislabeled point can be outvoted by its neighbors. The kernel decides the result: too wide an RBF blurs the classes together, and too few kNN neighbors fragments the graph so that propagation stops there. With the RBF kernel the graph is dense, every point linked to every other through an $n \times n$ matrix, so it only scales to a few thousand points. The kNN kernel keeps $k$ neighbors per point and scales much further. On images, distances between raw pixels are a poor similarity. Propagate over a representation in which similar images are close, from a model trained on the labeled part or from [contrastive learning](2.14-RP2-contrastive-learning.md).

### Consistency regularization

Deep methods add a third idea: a model should give the same answer for a data point and a lightly corrupted copy of it. Let the model predict a pseudo-label from a weakly augmented copy of an unlabeled image, a small crop or flip, then train it to predict that label from a strongly augmented copy. That is FixMatch, a simplification of earlier recipes such as MixMatch and long the reference recipe for image classification with tens of labels. It keeps a pseudo-label only when the prediction on the weak copy clears a confidence threshold, 0.95 in the paper, so the confidence floor is still there. Both halves matter: a way to generate labels, and a brake on how fast the model believes itself.

### The contest problem

Read the Round 2 problem as a design exercise. No pretrained models, so any built-in knowledge about images has to come from the architecture and from augmentation. There are only 200 labels, so every example held back for validation is one the model never trains on. A split that small also gives a noisy read on the threshold, the quota, or the stopping round. Designing that split is part of what the setters said they were measuring. Little else warns you when the loop starts absorbing its own mistakes, so choose every knob conservatively in advance: a fixed small quota, a high threshold, a fixed round budget. [Early stopping](1.21-DL4-optimization-adam-sgd.md) is available in principle, but it spends labels you can barely spare.

## Implementation

### The example in NumPy

The graph as a weight matrix, the iterate-and-clamp loop, the closed form, and the random walk:

```python
import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)

W = np.zeros((5, 5))
for i, j in [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 4), (1, 3)]:        # the path 1-2-3-4-5 and the shortcut 2-4
    W[i, j] = W[j, i] = 1.0
T = W / W.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)                        # each row: a point's similarity as fractions
labeled, unlabeled = np.array([0, 4]), np.array([1, 2, 3])
Y = np.zeros((5, 2))                                        # one-hot labels for points 1 and 5
Y[0, 0] = Y[4, 1] = 1.0

F = Y.copy()
for sweep in range(1, 1001):
    F_next = T @ F                                          # every point averages its neighbors
    F_next[labeled] = Y[labeled]                            # the known labels are clamped back
    if sweep in (1, 2, 10):
        print(f"after {sweep:>2} sweeps, P(A) = {F_next[:, 0]}")
    if np.abs(F_next - F).max() < 1e-12:
        break
    F = F_next
print(f"fixed point after {sweep} sweeps, P(A) = {F[:, 0]}")

F_U = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(3) - T[np.ix_(unlabeled, unlabeled)], T[np.ix_(unlabeled, labeled)] @ Y[labeled])
print("closed form, points 2, 3, 4:", F_U[:, 0], "= 5/8, 1/2, 3/8")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                              # random walks from point 2, stopped at a labeled point
exits = 0
for _ in range(100000):
    i = 1
    while i not in (0, 4):
        i = rng.choice(5, p=T[i])
    exits += i == 0
print(f"walks from point 2 that exit at point 1: {exits / 100000:.4f}")
```

```output
after  1 sweeps, P(A) = [1.     0.3333 0.     0.     0.    ]
after  2 sweeps, P(A) = [1.     0.3333 0.1667 0.1111 0.    ]
after 10 sweeps, P(A) = [1.     0.5925 0.4577 0.3425 0.    ]
fixed point after 98 sweeps, P(A) = [1.    0.625 0.5   0.375 0.   ]
closed form, points 2, 3, 4: [0.625 0.5   0.375] = 5/8, 1/2, 3/8
walks from point 2 that exit at point 1: 0.6228
```

### Six labels, two crescents

The same loop on a thousand points with an RBF kernel, against the closed form and against scikit-learn. The RBF kernel gives each point a weight of 1 on itself, so a little of each point's own current value carries over into the average as well, and scikit-learn's `LabelPropagation` builds its RBF graph the same way:

```python
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.semi_supervised import LabelPropagation

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=1000, noise=0.08, random_state=0)
labeled = np.concatenate([np.flatnonzero(y == c)[:3] for c in (0, 1)])   # three labels per class
U = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(X)), labeled)                              # everything else

W = np.exp(-20.0 * ((X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1))       # RBF weights, gamma = 20
T = W / W.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)                                     # each row sums to 1

Y = np.zeros((len(X), 2))
Y[labeled, y[labeled]] = 1.0
F = Y.copy()
for _ in range(1000):
    F = T @ F                                        # every point takes its neighbors' average
    F[labeled] = Y[labeled]                          # then the known labels are clamped back
pred_iter = F.argmax(axis=1)

# The fixed point in closed form: F_U = T_UU F_U + T_UL Y_L.
F_U = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(len(U)) - T[np.ix_(U, U)], T[np.ix_(U, labeled)] @ Y[labeled])
pred_closed = F_U.argmax(axis=1)

y_partial = np.full(len(X), -1)
y_partial[labeled] = y[labeled]
pred_sk = LabelPropagation(kernel="rbf", gamma=20, max_iter=1000).fit(X, y_partial).transduction_

print(f"accuracy on the {len(U)} unlabeled points, from 6 labels")
print(f"  iterate and clamp : {np.mean(pred_iter[U] == y[U]):.3f}")
print(f"  closed form       : {np.mean(pred_closed == y[U]):.3f}")
print(f"  scikit-learn      : {np.mean(pred_sk[U] == y[U]):.3f}")
print(f"iteration agrees with the closed form on {np.mean(pred_iter[U] == pred_closed):.1%} of points, "
      f"with scikit-learn on {np.mean(pred_iter == pred_sk):.1%}")
```

```output
accuracy on the 994 unlabeled points, from 6 labels
  iterate and clamp : 0.999
  closed form       : 0.999
  scikit-learn      : 0.999
iteration agrees with the closed form on 100.0% of points, with scikit-learn on 100.0%
```

A few lines of NumPy, one linear solve, and the library land on the same labels: `LabelPropagation` is this loop.

### Pseudo-labeling, naive and careful

The digits with 4 labels per class. Confidence is the gap between the top two entries of the SVM's `decision_function`. The naive loop absorbs the 100 most confident predictions a round for ten rounds. The careful loop absorbs at most 8 per predicted class with a margin of at least 0.5, for up to fifteen rounds, and stops when nothing qualifies:

```python
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)

def split(seed):
    X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=600, random_state=seed, stratify=y)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    seed_rows = np.concatenate([rng.choice(np.where(y_train == c)[0], 4, replace=False) for c in range(10)])
    return X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test, seed_rows

def pseudo_label(seed, naive, rounds, per_class=8, floor=0.5, top=100):
    X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test, seed_rows = split(seed)
    y_work = np.full(len(y_train), -1)
    y_work[seed_rows] = y_train[seed_rows]
    labeled = y_work != -1
    history = []
    for r in range(rounds + 1):
        clf = SVC(kernel="rbf", gamma="scale").fit(X_train[labeled], y_work[labeled])
        history.append((clf.predict(X_test) == y_test).mean())
        if r == rounds:
            break
        cand = np.where(~labeled)[0]
        scores = clf.decision_function(X_train[cand])
        top2 = np.sort(scores, axis=1)[:, -2:]
        conf = top2[:, 1] - top2[:, 0]                       # the margin between the top two classes
        pred = clf.predict(X_train[cand])
        if naive:                                            # the most confident, whatever their class
            take = np.argsort(-conf)[:top]
            y_work[cand[take]] = pred[take]
            labeled[cand[take]] = True
        else:                                                # at most per_class each, margin at least floor
            added = 0
            for c in range(10):
                m = (pred == c) & (conf >= floor)
                idx = cand[m][np.argsort(-conf[m])[:per_class]]
                y_work[idx] = c
                labeled[idx] = True
                added += len(idx)
            if added == 0:
                break
    wrong = int((y_work[labeled] != y_train[labeled]).sum())
    return np.array(history), int(labeled.sum()), wrong

naive_hist, n_naive, wrong_naive = pseudo_label(3, True, 10)
careful_hist, n_careful, wrong_careful = pseudo_label(3, False, 15)
print(f"supervised, 40 labels:    {naive_hist[0]:.3f}")
print(f"naive, after 10 rounds:   {naive_hist[-1]:.3f}   training rows {n_naive}, wrong pseudo-labels {wrong_naive}")
print(f"careful, after 15 rounds: {careful_hist[-1]:.3f}   training rows {n_careful}, wrong pseudo-labels {wrong_careful}")
print("naive by round:  ", np.round(naive_hist, 3))
print("careful by round:", np.round(careful_hist, 3))
```

```output
naive, after 10 rounds:   0.695   training rows 1040, wrong pseudo-labels 308
careful, after 15 rounds: 0.937   training rows 1166, wrong pseudo-labels 103
naive by round:   [0.853 0.825 0.822 0.828 0.802 0.782 0.777 0.76  0.728 0.717 0.695]
careful by round: [0.853 0.832 0.873 0.873 0.877 0.868 0.875 0.88  0.88  0.89  0.91  0.92
 0.928 0.935 0.938 0.937]
```

### scikit-learn's SelfTrainingClassifier

The library version of the loop takes a base classifier with `predict_proba` and a probability `threshold`. Its other mode, `criterion="k_best"`, absorbs the $k$ most confident rows each round, which is the naive loop. An SVM has no probabilities of its own, so they are fitted by a sigmoid on cross-validated decision values, and with 40 labeled rows that calibration has almost nothing to fit on:

```python
from sklearn.calibration import CalibratedClassifierCV
from sklearn.semi_supervised import SelfTrainingClassifier

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test, seed_rows = split(3)
y_semi = np.full(len(y_train), -1)
y_semi[seed_rows] = y_train[seed_rows]
for threshold in (0.6, 0.8, 0.95):
    base = CalibratedClassifierCV(SVC(kernel="rbf", gamma="scale"), cv=3, ensemble=False)   # sigmoid probabilities from 3-fold CV
    st = SelfTrainingClassifier(base, threshold=threshold, max_iter=15).fit(X_train, y_semi)
    print(f"threshold {threshold}: test accuracy {(st.predict(X_test) == y_test).mean():.3f}, "
          f"labeled by the end {(st.transduction_ != -1).sum()} of {len(y_train)}, rounds {st.n_iter_}")
base = CalibratedClassifierCV(SVC(kernel="rbf", gamma="scale"), cv=3, ensemble=False).fit(X_train[seed_rows], y_train[seed_rows])
print("largest probability on any unlabeled row, from the 40 labels:", base.predict_proba(X_train[y_semi == -1]).max().round(3))
```

```output
threshold 0.6: test accuracy 0.632, labeled by the end 40 of 1197, rounds 1
threshold 0.8: test accuracy 0.632, labeled by the end 40 of 1197, rounds 1
threshold 0.95: test accuracy 0.632, labeled by the end 40 of 1197, rounds 1
largest probability on any unlabeled row, from the 40 labels: 0.477
```

The largest calibrated probability on any unlabeled row is 0.477, so no threshold is ever cleared, nothing is absorbed, and the calibrated classifier itself scores 0.632 against the plain SVM's 0.853. The confidence measure has to mean something before a floor on it can. The margin loop above thresholds the decision function directly, so it is written out and not imported.
