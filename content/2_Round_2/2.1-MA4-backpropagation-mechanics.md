## Overview

Training adjusts the weights to lower a loss, which needs the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight. **Backpropagation** is the chain rule organized so that one sweep backward through the network produces all of them, at about the cost of the forward pass. It is what `loss.backward()` runs in PyTorch, and the contest asks for it by hand: given a small network and its forward pass, compute the gradients. [2025 Round 1](https://forum.beaver-edge.ai/c/ai-olympiads/usa-north-america-ai-olympiad/8), Problem 2 trained its network by gradient descent, and every gradient in that update is one of the formulas below.

## Example: the backward pass by hand

The network from the forward propagation module has just been run on $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)^\top$ with true class 0:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W^{(1)} &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, &
\mathbf{b}^{(1)} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}, &
\mathbf{z}^{(1)} &= \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, &
\mathbf{a}^{(1)} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \\[6pt]
W^{(2)} &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, &
\mathbf{b}^{(2)} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, &
\hat{\mathbf{y}} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0.1192 \\ 0.8808 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The hidden activation is ReLU, the output is a softmax, and the loss is cross-entropy. What are the output error $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} = \partial L / \partial \mathbf{z}^{(2)}$, the gradients $\partial L / \partial W^{(2)}$ and $\partial L / \partial \mathbf{b}^{(2)}$, the hidden error $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}$, and the gradients $\partial L / \partial W^{(1)}$ and $\partial L / \partial \mathbf{b}^{(1)}$?

<NotebookCard id="backpropagation-by-hand" />

Six quantities, in four lines once the right intermediate is in hand: the output error, one layer's gradients, the step back through a layer, then the next layer's gradients. The explanation derives the lines and fills in the numbers.

## Explanation

### The error signal

Backpropagation tracks one quantity per layer: how much the loss changes when that layer's pre-activation changes. It is called the layer's **error**, written

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l)}} ,
$$

with one entry per unit. Every weight and bias gradient is a short product involving $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$, and $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ comes from $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}$ by one rule. The backward pass is: find the error at the output, then move it back one layer at a time, collecting gradients as it passes.

### The output error

With a softmax output and cross-entropy loss, the error at the logits is

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y} ,
$$

predicted minus true. The derivation is three lines. Write $c$ for the true class, so the loss is $L = -\log \hat y_c$. Substitute the softmax and split the logarithm:

$$
L = -\log \frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} = -z_c + \log \sum_j e^{z_j} .
$$

Now differentiate with respect to one logit $z_i$. The first term gives −1 when $i = c$ and 0 otherwise, which is $-y_i$. The second gives $e^{z_i} / \sum_j e^{z_j}$, which is $\hat y_i$. Together, $\partial L / \partial z_i = \hat y_i - y_i$. The logarithm in the loss undoes the exponential in the softmax. That is why the two are paired. [Calculus and Gradients](1.9-MA3-calculus-gradients.md) has the same derivation at more length. For the example, $\mathbf{y} = (1, 0)^\top$, so

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1192 - 1 \\ 0.8808 - 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} -0.8808 \\ 0.8808 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entries sum to zero, as they always do, because the predicted and the true probabilities each sum to 1.

### Weight gradients are outer products

Take one weight, $W^{(2)}_{ij}$, which connects hidden unit $j$ to output $i$. It appears in one place only, the term $W^{(2)}_{ij}\, a^{(1)}_j$ inside $z^{(2)}_i$. Nudging the weight moves $z^{(2)}_i$ at the rate $a^{(1)}_j$, and $z^{(2)}_i$ moves the loss at the rate $\delta^{(2)}_i$. The chain rule multiplies the two:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}_{ij}} = \delta^{(2)}_i \, a^{(1)}_j .
$$

A weight's gradient is the error at the unit it feeds times the activation it reads. All of them at once form a matrix:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} \big(\mathbf{a}^{(1)}\big)^{\!\top},
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(2)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} .
$$

A column times a row is called an **outer product**. Its entry $(i, j)$ is $\delta_i a_j$, so it has the same shape as $W^{(2)}$. A gradient always has the shape of its parameter, which settles any doubt about which factor is transposed. The bias gradient is the error itself, because the bias is added to $z_i$ with slope 1. For the example,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(2)}} &=
\begin{pmatrix} -0.8808 \\ 0.8808 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
&= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1.7616 & -0.8808 \\ 0 & 1.7616 & 0.8808 \end{pmatrix}, \\[6pt]
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(2)}} &= \begin{pmatrix} -0.8808 \\ 0.8808 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first column is zero because the first hidden unit is off: $a^{(1)}_1 = 0$, so no change to the weights reading it can change the loss.

### Back through a layer

Next, move the error from the output back to the hidden layer. Hidden unit $j$ affects the loss in two stages.

1. Its activation $a^{(1)}_j$ feeds every output unit $p$ through the weight $W^{(2)}_{pj}$. Nudging $a^{(1)}_j$ changes the loss at the rate $\sum_p \delta^{(2)}_p W^{(2)}_{pj}$: each output's error times the weight that connects it to unit $j$, added up.
2. Its activation is $\varphi(z^{(1)}_j)$. Nudging $z^{(1)}_j$ moves the activation at the rate $\varphi'(z^{(1)}_j)$, the slope of the activation function.

Multiply the two stages:

$$
\delta^{(1)}_j = \Big( \sum_p \delta^{(2)}_p W^{(2)}_{pj} \Big)\, \varphi'(z^{(1)}_j) .
$$

The sum is entry $j$ of $(W^{(2)})^{\!\top} \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)}$, so for the whole layer

$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} = \big( (W^{(2)})^{\!\top} \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} \big) \odot \varphi'(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}) ,
$$

where $\odot$ multiplies entry by entry. In words: multiply by the transpose of the next layer's weight matrix, which sends each output's error back along the weights it arrived by, then multiply by the activation's slope. The same rule gives $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)}$ from $\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l+1)}$ at every hidden layer, however deep the network. For ReLU the slope is 1 where $z > 0$ and 0 elsewhere, so for the example

$$
\begin{aligned}
(W^{(2)})^{\!\top} \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(2)} &=
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} -0.8808 \\ 0.8808 \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
&= \begin{pmatrix} -0.8808 \\ 0.8808 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \\[6pt]
\varphi'(\mathbf{z}^{(1)}) &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0.8808 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Two of the three entries are zero for two different reasons. The first unit's pre-activation was negative, so ReLU's slope there is 0 and no error passes through a unit that was off. The third unit feeds both outputs with weight 1, and the two errors cancel exactly, so moving it would not change the loss.

### The first layer

The same outer product, with the input as the incoming activation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(1)}} &= \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)} \mathbf{x}^{\!\top} =
\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0.8808 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt]
&= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0.8808 & 1.7616 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \\[6pt]
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(1)}} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0.8808 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Only one row of $W^{(1)}$ gets any gradient on this example. In general, at every layer,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(l)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} \big(\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}\big)^{\!\top},
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(l)}} = \boldsymbol{\delta}^{(l)} ,
$$

and a gradient descent step subtracts the learning rate times each of these from its parameter.

<ProblemCard id="bp-input-from-first-layer-gradient" />

### Batches

For $N$ examples the loss is the mean of the per-example losses, so each gradient is the mean of the per-example gradients. With the examples as rows, the outer products become one matrix product that sums over the batch, and the bias gradient is a column sum. Dividing the output error by $N$ once, at the start, makes everything the gradient of the mean.

<ProblemCard id="bp-rank-of-output-gradient" />

### Vanishing and exploding gradients

To reach an early layer from the output, the error is multiplied by one weight matrix and one set of activation slopes at every layer in between. Multiplying by many factors acts like raising a number to a power. If the factors are mostly below 1, the gradient shrinks by a constant factor per layer and the early layers stop learning, which is the **vanishing gradient**. If they are mostly above 1, it grows by a constant factor per layer, the **exploding gradient**.

The sigmoid's slope is at most $\tfrac14$, so a stack of $L$ sigmoid layers shrinks the gradient by at least $4^{L-1}$ from the slopes alone. The deep sigmoid network in the [MLP activation experiment](1.18-DL1-neural-networks-perceptrons-mlps-activations.md#card-mlp-activations) never learns at all. ReLU's slope is exactly 1 wherever a unit is on, which is the main reason it replaced sigmoid between layers. The weight factors are handled by initialization, in [Optimizers & Training Tricks](1.21-DL4-optimization-adam-sgd.md), and the exploding case by gradient clipping, in [Recurrent Networks](3.5-NL2-sequence-models-transfer-learning.md).

## Implementation

The forward pass from the previous module, repeated so this runs on its own, then the backward pass as a loop over the cache:

```python
import numpy as np

np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)

def relu(Z):
    return np.maximum(0.0, Z)

def softmax(Z):
    Z = Z - Z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    E = np.exp(Z)
    return E / E.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def forward(X, params, acts):
    cache = {"A0": X}
    A = X
    for l, ((W, b), phi) in enumerate(zip(params, acts), start=1):
        Z = A @ W + b
        A = phi(Z)
        cache[f"Z{l}"] = Z
        cache[f"A{l}"] = A
    return A, cache

def backward(cache, params, Y):
    """Y: one-hot labels, (N, c). Returns [(dW, db)] per layer, for the mean loss."""
    N, L = Y.shape[0], len(params)
    grads = [None] * L
    delta = (cache[f"A{L}"] - Y) / N                            # output error, softmax + cross-entropy
    for l in range(L, 0, -1):
        A_prev = cache[f"A{l-1}"]
        W, b = params[l - 1]
        grads[l - 1] = (A_prev.T @ delta, delta.sum(axis=0))    # outer products summed over the batch
        if l > 1:
            delta = (delta @ W.T) * (cache[f"Z{l-1}"] > 0)      # back through W, then ReLU'
    return grads
```

`backward` is the four steps of the explanation. The output error is one line, each layer's gradients are one line, and the step back through a layer is one line, with the transposes flipped because the code keeps examples in rows. On the example, printed in the layout of the derivation:

```python
W1 = np.array([[1., 0., 1.], [-1., 1., 1.]])    # the derivation's W1, transposed
b1 = np.array([0., 0., -2.])
W2 = np.array([[1., 0.], [0., 1.], [1., 1.]])
b2 = np.array([0., 0.])
params = [(W1, b1), (W2, b2)]
X = np.array([[1., 2.]])
Y = np.array([[1., 0.]])                        # one-hot label for class 0

out, cache = forward(X, params, [relu, softmax])
grads = backward(cache, params, Y)
for l, (dW, db) in enumerate(grads, start=1):
    print(f"dW{l}\n{np.round(dW.T, 4) + 0.0}\ndb{l} {np.round(db, 4) + 0.0}")
```

```output
dW1
[[0.     0.    ]
 [0.8808 1.7616]
 [0.     0.    ]]
db1 [0.     0.8808 0.    ]
dW2
[[ 0.     -1.7616 -0.8808]
 [ 0.      1.7616  0.8808]]
db2 [-0.8808  0.8808]
```

Every entry matches the gradients worked out by hand. Check any hand-derived gradient numerically, with the central difference from [Why PyTorch](1.24-PY8-why-pytorch-not-tensorflow.md): nudge one weight up and down by a small $h$ and compare the slope of the loss. On $W^{(1)}_{22}$, whose gradient is 1.7616:

```python
def loss_of(params):
    out, _ = forward(X, params, [relu, softmax])
    return -np.log(out[0, 0])

h = 1e-5
W1_plus, W1_minus = W1.copy(), W1.copy()
W1_plus[1, 1] += h
W1_minus[1, 1] -= h
numeric = (loss_of([(W1_plus, b1), (W2, b2)]) - loss_of([(W1_minus, b1), (W2, b2)])) / (2 * h)
print(f"{numeric:.6f} {grads[0][0][1, 1]:.6f}")
```

```output
1.761594 1.761594
```

PyTorch's autograd agrees too. The same forward pass written in torch, with `loss.backward()` in place of the loop, puts the same matrix in `W1.grad`:

```python
import torch

x = torch.tensor([1., 2.])
W1t = torch.tensor([[1., -1.], [0., 1.], [1., 1.]], requires_grad=True)
b1t = torch.tensor([0., 0., -2.], requires_grad=True)
W2t = torch.tensor([[1., 0., 1.], [0., 1., 1.]], requires_grad=True)
b2t = torch.tensor([0., 0.], requires_grad=True)

a1 = torch.relu(W1t @ x + b1t)
loss = -torch.log_softmax(W2t @ a1 + b2t, dim=0)[0]      # cross-entropy for class 0
loss.backward()
print(torch.allclose(W1t.grad, torch.tensor(grads[0][0].T, dtype=torch.float32), atol=1e-6))
```

```output
True
```
