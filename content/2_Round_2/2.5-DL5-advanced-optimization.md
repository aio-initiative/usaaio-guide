## Overview

Gradient descent fits a linear model to 200 houses. With unscaled features it needs 12,500 steps. Rescale the features, and the same fit takes 7 steps. Why? How big should each step be? And when can you be sure the bottom you reached is the lowest point there is?

For least squares and logistic regression, convex optimization answers all three questions. Newton's method on the logistic loss is in [Advanced Optimization](2.6-DL5-advanced-optimization-methods.md). The [USAAIO syllabus](https://usaaio.org/syllabus) lists convex optimization, "e.g., gradient descent algorithms and duality", among its math foundations.

## Example: gradient descent on an elongated bowl

Minimize $f(x, y) = x^2 + 10y^2$ with gradient descent, starting from $(10, 1)$ with a fixed learning rate $\eta$.

1. Which values of $\eta$ make gradient descent converge?
2. Which $\eta$ is fastest, and how many steps does it need to get within 0.01 of the minimum at $(0, 0)$?

Before reading on, guess: is the largest safe $\eta$ set by the $x^2$ term or by the $10y^2$ term?

## Explanation

### Gradient descent, one step at a time

Start in one dimension with $f(w) = (w - 3)^2$. Its minimum is at $w = 3$, and its derivative is $f'(w) = 2(w - 3)$. Gradient descent repeats

$$
w \leftarrow w - \eta\,f'(w) ,
$$

where the learning rate $\eta > 0$ sets the size of each step.

With $\eta = 0.1$ and $w_0 = 0$: $f'(0) = -6$, so $w_1 = 0 + 0.6 = 0.6$. Then $f'(0.6) = -4.8$, so $w_2 = 1.08$. Watch the distance to the minimum: 3, then 2.4, then 1.92. Each step multiplies it by 0.8. To see why, write $e = w - 3$ for the error. One step gives

$$
e \leftarrow e - \eta \cdot 2e = (1 - 2\eta)\,e .
$$

So the error shrinks by the same factor every step, as long as $|1 - 2\eta| < 1$, which means $0 < \eta < 1$. With $\eta = 0.5$ the factor is 0 and one step lands on the minimum. With $\eta = 1.1$ the factor is $-1.2$. Each step jumps past 3 to the other side and lands 1.2 times farther away, so $w$ runs off to infinity.

For a general quadratic $f(w) = \tfrac{a}{2}(w - c)^2$, with its minimum at $w = c$, the factor is $1 - \eta a$. Here $a = f''$ is the **curvature**. Gradient descent converges when $0 < \eta < 2/a$, and $\eta = 1/a$ lands on the minimum in one step.

### Solving the focus problem: learning rates on the bowl

$f(x, y) = x^2 + 10y^2$ has gradient $(2x,\ 20y)$, so one step updates each coordinate on its own:

$$
x \leftarrow (1 - 2\eta)\,x, \qquad y \leftarrow (1 - 20\eta)\,y .
$$

This is the one-dimensional picture twice, with curvatures 2 and 20. Both coordinates shrink only if $|1 - 2\eta| < 1$ and $|1 - 20\eta| < 1$. The second condition is stricter, so gradient descent converges for **$0 < \eta < 0.1$**. The $y$-direction, with curvature 20, sets the largest step for both coordinates.

Which $\eta$ is fastest? Raising $\eta$ shrinks $|1 - 2\eta|$. Once $\eta > 1/20$, it grows $|1 - 20\eta|$. The slower coordinate decides the speed, so we want the larger of the two sizes to be as small as possible. That happens where the two sizes meet. There $1 - 2\eta$ is still positive and $1 - 20\eta$ is already negative, so

$$
1 - 2\eta = -(1 - 20\eta), \qquad\text{which gives}\qquad \eta = \tfrac{2}{22} = \tfrac{1}{11} .
$$

Both factors then have size $1 - 2/11 = 9/11 \approx 0.818$. Each coordinate is multiplied by $\pm 9/11$ every step, so the distance to $(0, 0)$ is multiplied by $9/11$ too. The start $(10, 1)$ is at distance $\sqrt{101} \approx 10.05$. We need $(9/11)^k \cdot 10.05 \le 0.01$, so $k \ge \ln(1005)/\ln(11/9) \approx 34.4$. The first whole number of steps that works is **$k = 35$**.

The same argument works for any quadratic, a function whose Hessian $H$ (the matrix of second derivatives) is constant. Along each eigenvector of $H$, the coordinate is multiplied by $1 - \eta\lambda_i$ per step, where the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ is the curvature in that direction. Write $\lambda_{\max}$ and $\lambda_{\min}$ for the largest and smallest eigenvalues. Their ratio $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the **condition number**. With $\lambda_{\min} > 0$:

- Gradient descent converges if and only if $0 < \eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$.
- The best fixed step is $\eta = 2/(\lambda_{\min} + \lambda_{\max})$, giving the factor $\dfrac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}$.
- The simpler step $\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max}$ multiplies the flattest direction by $1 - 1/\kappa$. Since $(1 - 1/\kappa)^k \approx e^{-k/\kappa}$, cutting the error by a factor of 1,000 takes about $\kappa \ln 1000 \approx 7\kappa$ steps.

Here $\lambda_{\max} = 20$ and $\lambda_{\min} = 2$, so $\kappa = 10$ and $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1) = 9/11$, as we found. A large condition number means slow training.

For a loss that is not quadratic, the curvature changes from point to point. If no direction anywhere has curvature above $\lambda_{\max}$, the step $\eta \le 1/\lambda_{\max}$ still lowers the loss at every step (proof in the Optional box below).

### Feature scaling

In practice, a large condition number often comes from features on different scales. Take a data matrix $X$ with one house per row, $n$ rows and $d$ feature columns. The mean squared error of a linear model has Hessian $\tfrac{2}{n}X^\top X$, derived in [Calculus and Gradients](1.9-MA3-calculus-gradients.md). In the Implementation below, the area spreads with standard deviation about 60 m² and the number of rooms with about 1.4. After centering, the area column's entries are about 42 times larger. The Hessian's eigenvalues then come out about $42^2 \approx 1{,}800$ times apart. The loss surface is a long, narrow valley, and gradient descent needs 12,500 steps.

**Standardizing** each feature, subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation, puts every column on the same scale. If the features are not strongly correlated, $X^\top X$ is then close to a multiple of the identity and $\kappa$ is close to 1. Here $\kappa$ drops to 1.3, and the same fit takes 7 steps. The model makes the same predictions, with rescaled parameters. In the focus problem, substituting $y' = \sqrt{10}\,y$ gives $x^2 + y'^2$, a round bowl with $\kappa = 1$, which $\eta = 1/2$ solves in one step.

In scikit-learn, put a `StandardScaler`, fit on the training set, before any model trained by gradient steps.

### Convex functions

Gradient descent found $(0, 0)$ for $x^2 + 10y^2$, where the gradient is zero. How do we know no lower point hides somewhere else? Gradient descent only sees the local slope, so the function must have no other valleys to get stuck in. **Convex** functions are the ones with that guarantee.

A function is convex if the straight segment between any two points on its graph never dips below the graph:

$$
f\big(t\mathbf{u} + (1 - t)\mathbf{v}\big) \le t f(\mathbf{u}) + (1 - t) f(\mathbf{v})
\qquad\text{for all } t \in [0, 1] .
$$

As $t$ goes from 0 to 1, the point $t\mathbf{u} + (1 - t)\mathbf{v}$ runs along the segment between two inputs $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$. The left side is the graph's height there, and the right side is the chord's height. Examples: $x^2$, $e^x$, $-\ln x$ (for $x > 0$), $|x|$, and the mean squared error of a linear model.

In practice, use one of these tests instead of the definition. The first two need the inputs to range over a **convex set**, one where the segment between any two points stays inside, such as all of $\mathbb{R}^d$ or an interval.

- **Second-derivative test.** A twice-differentiable $f$ is convex if and only if its Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere: all eigenvalues $\ge 0$ at every point. In one dimension, the condition is $f'' \ge 0$ at every point.
- **Tangent-line test.** A differentiable $f$ is convex if and only if it lies above every tangent line (or plane): $f(\mathbf{v}) \ge f(\mathbf{u}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{u}) \cdot (\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{u})$ for every pair of inputs.
- **Building blocks.** Sums of convex functions, positive multiples of one, the maximum of several, and a convex function of a linear-plus-constant input, $f(A\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{b})$, are all convex. For example, $\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2$ is convex (its Hessian is $2I$), so $\lVert X\theta - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2$ is convex too. Adding the penalty $\alpha\lVert\theta\rVert^2$ with $\alpha > 0$ gives the ridge regression loss, still convex.

**Why local means global.** Suppose $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^\star) = \mathbf{0}$ for a convex $f$. The tangent-line test at $\mathbf{x}^\star$ says $f(\mathbf{v}) \ge f(\mathbf{x}^\star) + \mathbf{0} \cdot (\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{x}^\star) = f(\mathbf{x}^\star)$ for every $\mathbf{v}$. So any point with zero gradient is a global minimum. If $f$ is **strictly** convex (the segment lies strictly above the graph between its ends), that minimum is unique. A Hessian with all eigenvalues positive everywhere makes $f$ strictly convex. In the focus problem the Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(2, 20)$ at every point, so $(0, 0)$ is the one and only minimum.

<ProblemCard id="cvx-which-loss-is-not-convex" />

**Jensen's inequality** extends the definition from two points to any number. For convex $f$ and weights $w_i \ge 0$ that sum to 1,

$$
f\Big(\sum_i w_i x_i\Big) \le \sum_i w_i f(x_i) .
$$

The left side is $f$ of the weighted average, and the right side is the weighted average of $f$. For a strictly convex $f$, equality holds only when all the $x_i$ with positive weight are equal.

A function is **concave** when $-f$ is convex, like $\ln x$, whose graph bends downward. For a concave $f$ the inequality flips. With $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 4$ and equal weights, Jensen on $\ln$ gives $\ln 2.5 \ge (\ln 1 + \ln 4)/2 = \ln 2$. So the average 2.5 is at least the geometric mean $\sqrt{1 \cdot 4} = 2$. This is the **AM-GM inequality**: the arithmetic mean is at least the geometric mean. The same argument on $-\ln$ shows that KL divergence is never negative, a fact [Information Theory for AI](3.1-PS4-information-theory-core.md) uses.

### Constraints and the Lagrangian

Which point with $x + y \ge 1$ is closest to the origin? And can you find a number that is provably a lower bound on its squared distance, without first finding the point? A hard-margin support vector machine solves a problem with **constraints** like this. It minimizes the size of its weights subject to classifying every training point correctly.

Write a constrained problem as

$$
\min_{\mathbf{x}} f_0(\mathbf{x}) \quad\text{subject to}\quad f_i(\mathbf{x}) \le 0 \ \text{ for } i = 1, \dots, m .
$$

Here $f_0$ is the function to minimize, and each $f_i(\mathbf{x}) \le 0$ is one constraint. A point is **feasible** if it satisfies every constraint. Call the smallest value of $f_0$ over feasible points $p^\star$.

The idea is to charge a price for breaking each constraint. Give constraint $i$ a **multiplier** $\mu_i \ge 0$ and form the **Lagrangian**

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol\mu) = f_0(\mathbf{x}) + \sum_i \mu_i f_i(\mathbf{x}) .
$$

For an equality constraint $h(\mathbf{x}) = 0$, add $\nu\,h(\mathbf{x})$ with a multiplier $\nu$ of any sign. For several equality constraints $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, use one multiplier per row and add $\boldsymbol\nu^\top(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b})$, whose gradient in $\mathbf{x}$ is $A^\top\boldsymbol\nu$.

Minimizing $\mathcal{L}$ over all $\mathbf{x}$ for fixed multipliers gives the **dual function** $g(\boldsymbol\mu)$. For any feasible $\mathbf{x}$,

$$
g(\boldsymbol\mu) \le \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol\mu) = f_0(\mathbf{x}) + \sum_i \mu_i f_i(\mathbf{x}) \le f_0(\mathbf{x}) .
$$

The first inequality holds because $g$ is the minimum over all $\mathbf{x}$. The second holds because each $\mu_i \ge 0$ and each $f_i(\mathbf{x}) \le 0$. So every value $g(\boldsymbol\mu)$ is a lower bound on $p^\star$ (**weak duality**). The **dual problem** looks for the best such bound, $d^\star = \max_{\boldsymbol\mu \ge \mathbf{0}} g(\boldsymbol\mu)$.

Call the problem **convex** when $f_0$ and every $f_i$ are convex and every equality constraint is linear. For a convex problem, $d^\star = p^\star$ (**strong duality**) when some point satisfies every inequality constraint strictly. When every constraint is linear, any feasible point will do.

**Worked example.** Back to the half-plane: minimize $x^2 + y^2$ subject to $x + y \ge 1$. Write the constraint as $1 - x - y \le 0$. Then

$$
\mathcal{L} = x^2 + y^2 + \mu(1 - x - y) .
$$

Setting $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial x = 2x - \mu = 0$ and $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial y = 2y - \mu = 0$ gives $x = y = \mu/2$. Substituting, $g(\mu) = \tfrac{\mu^2}{4} + \tfrac{\mu^2}{4} + \mu(1 - \mu) = \mu - \tfrac{\mu^2}{2}$. We want the best lower bound, so maximize $g$: $g'(\mu) = 1 - \mu = 0$ gives $\mu = 1$ and $d^\star = g(1) = \tfrac12$. The matching point is $x = y = \tfrac12$, with squared distance $\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$. That equals $d^\star$, so strong duality holds.

When strong duality holds and the functions are differentiable, the solution $\mathbf{x}^\star$ and the best multipliers satisfy four conditions, the **KKT conditions** (after Karush, Kuhn and Tucker):

1. The gradient of $\mathcal{L}$ in $\mathbf{x}$ is zero at $\mathbf{x}^\star$.
2. Every constraint holds at $\mathbf{x}^\star$.
3. Every $\mu_i \ge 0$.
4. **Complementary slackness**: $\mu_i f_i(\mathbf{x}^\star) = 0$ for every $i$.

Condition 4 says each constraint either holds with equality at $\mathbf{x}^\star$ (it is **active**) or has multiplier 0. Here $\mu = 1 > 0$, so the constraint must be active, and indeed $x + y = 1$. In a hard-margin SVM (see [Support Vector Machines](1.15-ML3-kernel-methods-svms.md)), the training points with $\mu_i > 0$ sit on the margin and are called support vectors. Points with multiplier 0 do not affect the answer.

## Implementation

This code runs gradient descent on the focus problem from $(10, 1)$ for several learning rates. It counts the steps until the distance to $(0, 0)$ is at most 0.01.

```python
import numpy as np

def grad(p):
    x, y = p
    return np.array([2 * x, 20 * y])      # f(x, y) = x^2 + 10 y^2

def steps_to_reach(eta, tol=0.01, max_steps=1000):
    p = np.array([10.0, 1.0])
    for k in range(max_steps):
        if np.linalg.norm(p) <= tol:
            return k
        p = p - eta * grad(p)
        if np.linalg.norm(p) > 1e6:
            return "diverged"
    return "not yet"

for eta in (0.01, 0.05, 1 / 11, 0.099, 0.11):
    print(f"eta = {eta:.4f}   steps to within 0.01: {steps_to_reach(eta)}")
```

```output
eta = 0.0100   steps to within 0.01: 342
eta = 0.0500   steps to within 0.01: 66
eta = 0.0909   steps to within 0.01: 35
eta = 0.0990   steps to within 0.01: 228
eta = 0.1100   steps to within 0.01: diverged
```

$\eta = 1/11$ is fastest, at 35 steps, as predicted. $\eta = 0.099$ is slower than $\eta = 0.05$ because its $y$-factor is $1 - 20(0.099) = -0.98$. So $y$ flips sign every step and shrinks by only 2%. Above 0.1 the $y$-coordinate grows and the run diverges.

This code fits $\mathbf{y} \approx X\theta$ to 200 made-up houses twice. Both runs center the features. The first leaves them unscaled, and the second standardizes them. We center $\mathbf{y}$ as well, so no intercept is needed. Each run uses its own best step $2/(\lambda_{\min} + \lambda_{\max})$. It counts the steps until $\theta$ is within $10^{-6}$ (relative) of the least-squares answer.

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 200
area = rng.uniform(50, 250, n)              # square meters
rooms = rng.integers(1, 6, n).astype(float)
y = 3.0 * area + 20.0 * rooms + rng.normal(0, 10, n)
X_raw = np.column_stack([area, rooms])
X_centered = X_raw - X_raw.mean(axis=0)
X_std = X_centered / X_raw.std(axis=0)
y_c = y - y.mean()

def gd_steps(X, y, tol=1e-6, max_steps=100000):
    H = 2 / len(y) * X.T @ X                 # Hessian of the MSE
    lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)
    eta = 2 / (lam[0] + lam[-1])             # the best fixed step
    theta_star = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
    theta = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for k in range(max_steps):
        if np.linalg.norm(theta - theta_star) <= tol * np.linalg.norm(theta_star):
            return lam[-1] / lam[0], k
        theta = theta - eta * (-2 / len(y) * X.T @ (y - X @ theta))
    return lam[-1] / lam[0], f"over {max_steps}"

for name, X in (("unscaled", X_centered), ("standardized", X_std)):
    kappa, k = gd_steps(X, y_c)
    print(f"{name:13}  condition number {kappa:9.1f}   steps {k}")
```

```output
unscaled       condition number    1809.4   steps 12500
standardized   condition number       1.3   steps 7
```

Only the scaling differs between the two runs, and it takes the step count from 12,500 down to 7.

This code compares full-batch gradient descent with mini-batch SGD on 1,000 made-up examples with 5 features. Both use the same learning rate and the same three passes over the data. Mini-batch SGD and its noise are developed in [Advanced Optimization](2.6-DL5-advanced-optimization-methods.md).

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n, d = 1000, 5
X = rng.standard_normal((n, d))
y = X @ np.arange(1.0, d + 1) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)
best = np.mean((y - X @ np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]) ** 2)

def mse(theta):
    return np.mean((y - X @ theta) ** 2)

for B in (1000, 32):
    theta = np.zeros(d)
    eta = 0.05
    updates = 0
    for epoch in range(3):
        order = rng.permutation(n)
        for start in range(0, n, B):
            idx = order[start:start + B]
            g = -2 / len(idx) * X[idx].T @ (y[idx] - X[idx] @ theta)
            theta -= eta * g
            updates += 1
    print(
        f"batch size {B:4}: {updates:3} updates in 3 epochs, "
        f"MSE {mse(theta):.4f}  (best possible {best:.4f})"
    )
```

```output
batch size 1000:   3 updates in 3 epochs, MSE 30.0006  (best possible 0.2477)
batch size   32:  96 updates in 3 epochs, MSE 0.2483  (best possible 0.2477)
```

With the full batch, three epochs mean three updates, and the MSE is still 30. Batches of 32 make 96 updates in the same three passes and get within 0.001 of the best possible MSE.

<Warning>

If the loss grows, oscillates wildly or turns into `nan`, the step is past
$2/\lambda_{\max}$ in some direction. Lower $\eta$ by a factor of 10 first, and
standardize the features, before you look for errors in the gradient.

</Warning>

<Optional title="Why η = 1/λ_max is safe: the descent lemma">

Suppose no direction anywhere has curvature above $\lambda_{\max}$. By Taylor's theorem, $f(\mathbf{y})$ equals the tangent-plane value $f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x})$ plus $\tfrac12 \lVert\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x}\rVert^2$ times the curvature along the segment at some point between $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$. That curvature is at most $\lambda_{\max}$, so $f$ lies below a parabola with curvature $\lambda_{\max}$ at every point:

$$
f(\mathbf{y}) \le f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x}) + \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{2}\lVert\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x}\rVert^2 .
$$

Minimizing the right side over $\mathbf{y}$ gives $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x} - \tfrac{1}{\lambda_{\max}}\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$, a gradient step with $\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max}$. Substituting it back,

$$
f\big(\mathbf{x} - \tfrac{1}{\lambda_{\max}}\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\big) \le f(\mathbf{x}) - \frac{1}{2\lambda_{\max}}\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\rVert^2 ,
$$

so every step lowers the loss by at least $\tfrac{1}{2\lambda_{\max}}\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2$ until the gradient is zero.

</Optional>
