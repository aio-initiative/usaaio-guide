# 2.30 · TF10 · 0. Setup

> **Goal:** The next-token task, the history and the architecture.
> **Stage:** Round 2, Core contest depth.

## The problem

### The task

A **language model** reads a sequence of tokens $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_t$ and predicts the next token, $x_{t+1}$. It assigns a probability to every token in its vocabulary as the possible next token, written

$$
p(x_{t+1} \mid x_1, \dots, x_t).
$$

For "the cat sat", "on" should get a high probability as $x_4$. Generating text is equivalent to repeated prediction: the predicted token is appended, and the model runs again, until it predicts the end token `<end>` or reaches a length limit.

Row four: 'the cat sat on the mat' predicts x7 = the end token.">
Generation from "the cat sat". Each prediction becomes the last token of the next input.
</Figure>

### What makes it hard

The token that decides the next one can be far back. In

> The students who failed the exam ___

the next word should be "were", because the subject, $x_2$ = "students", is plural, even though the nearest noun, $x_6$ = "exam", suggests "was".

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-agreement.svg" alt="The tokens 'The students who failed the exam' and a blank, labelled x1 to x7. A solid arrow runs from the blank back to 'students', labelled 'subject, plural: were'. A short dashed arrow runs from the blank to 'exam', labelled 'nearest noun, singular: was'.">
The token that decides $x_7$ is five positions back. The nearest noun points the wrong way.
</Figure>

So the model has to reach tokens far back, and judge from their meaning which one matters. Models that lean on recent words get this wrong: Linzen et al. (2016) found recurrent networks made more of these mistakes the more nouns like "exam" came in between.

## History

Each generation of language model fixed the main weakness of the one before. For example, a 3-gram model predicting the word after "The students who failed the exam" sees only "the exam", so it cannot know the subject is plural.

| Period | Approach | Key idea | What held it back |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1980s to 2000s | n-gram models | Predict the next word from counts of the previous few words in a large corpus | Only a few words of context, and most longer word sequences never appear in any corpus |
| 2000 | Neural language models | Learn a vector for each word, so similar words share what is learned (Bengio et al.) | Still a fixed window of previous words |
| 2010 to 2016 | Recurrent networks (RNN, LSTM) | Read word by word, carrying a memory of everything read so far | The memory fades over distance, and words must be processed one after another |
| 2014 | Attention | Let the model look back at every earlier word and choose which ones to use (Bahdanau et al.) | Still built on top of a recurrent network |
| 2017 | Transformer | Attention alone, with no recurrence: every word looks at every other word directly, and all positions are computed at once (Vaswani et al.) | Cost grows with the square of the length |
| 2018 to 2020 | GPT, GPT-2, GPT-3 | A decoder-only Transformer trained only to predict the next token, on more and more text | Needs very large amounts of data and compute |

The key change came in 2017, when Vaswani et al. dropped the recurrent network and kept only attention. Every position could then be processed at once, so models could be trained on far more text. Three years later the same design, scaled up, was GPT-3, and Kaplan et al. (2020) showed that such models keep improving predictably as they grow.

## The intuition behind the Transformer

### Token vectors

Each token becomes a vector, and every layer adjusts those vectors a little. By the last layer, the vector at the end of the text holds enough about everything before it to predict the next token. In "the cat sat", the vector at "sat" picks up who sat, which helps predict "on".

### Attention and feed-forward

Each block has two parts. In attention, each token gathers information from the tokens before it. This is how "sat" finds "cat". In the feed-forward network, each token then processes what it gathered on its own, for example turning "the cat sat" into a sense that a place, such as "on the mat", comes next. Stacking blocks repeats this, so later layers can build on what earlier ones found.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-exchange-think.svg" alt="Three columns for x1 = the, x2 = cat and x3 = sat. In the attention layer, arrows run from each token's vector to its own next vector and to those of later tokens. The arrow from cat to sat is drawn heavier. In the feed-forward layer, each token's vector goes straight up through its own FFN box, with no arrows between columns. A label at the top reads: one block. Stacking repeats it N times.">
One block. Attention moves information between tokens. The feed-forward network works on each token separately.
</Figure>

### Learned relationships

A convolutional network assumes that nearby inputs are related, and a recurrent network favors recent words, so both lean toward "exam" in "The students who failed the exam ___". A Transformer assumes neither. It learns from data which tokens matter to which, such as a verb and its subject.

### Next-token prediction

The model is trained only to predict the next token, but doing that well takes grammar, facts and more:

| Text | To predict the next token, the model needs |
|---|---|
| The students who failed the exam ___ | Grammar: the subject is plural |
| The capital of France is ___ | A fact |
| If $x = 3$, then $2x =$ ___ | Arithmetic |
| She opened her umbrella because it was ___ | Everyday knowledge |

None of these are taught directly. They are learned because they make the predictions better.

### Costs

- **Length.** The score matrix has an entry for every pair of tokens, so the work grows with the square of the sequence length: 1,000 tokens need a million scores per head, and 2,000 need four million (Step 5).
- **Order.** Attention on its own cannot tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog", so position information has to be added (Step 3).
- **Data.** A model that assumes little has to learn everything from data: GPT-3 was trained on about 300 billion tokens.

## Architecture

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-steps.svg" alt="A Transformer drawn bottom to top. The text 'the cat sat' goes through 1. Tokenize, 2. Embed and 3. Add position, then into a dashed frame marked 'one block, times N (11. Stack)' that holds 4 to 8. Masked multi-head attention, 9. Add and norm, 10. Feed-forward and 9. Add and norm, with dashed residual lines on the left. Above the frame is 12. Predict, which outputs the next word 'on'. A dashed line on the right, labelled 13. Generate, carries the word back to the input.">
A decoder-only Transformer, labelled with the step that covers each component.
</Figure>

Text enters at the bottom and the predicted next token leaves at the top. The dashed frame is one **block**. Production models stack many of them. The dashed lines on the left carry each part's input around it (Step 9), and the dashed loop on the right is generation (Step 13).

This is a **decoder-only** Transformer, the design used by GPT models. The original 2017 model also had an encoder, because it was built for translation. The diagram follows the original paper in normalizing after each part. Step 9 covers the variant GPT-2 uses.

The steps that follow explore this architecture one part at a time, in the order the data flows through it.
