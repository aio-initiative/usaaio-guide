## The problem

A model works with numbers, not text. Before anything else, the text has to be cut into pieces and each piece replaced by a number.

The model has a fixed list of the pieces it knows, its **vocabulary**. **Tokenization** cuts the input into those pieces, called **tokens**, and replaces each with its place in the list, its ID. For example, with a vocabulary in which "the" is entry 1, "cat" entry 2 and "sat" entry 3, the text "the cat sat" becomes the IDs 1, 2, 3.

The hard part is choosing the pieces. Three goals affect how text should be tokenized:

- **Any text.** A word that was not in the training text, such as "lowest", must still come out as IDs the model knows.
- **Few tokens.** Attention compares every token with every token, itself included, so its work grows with the square of the count. A text cut into 11 tokens needs 11 × 11 = 121 comparisons where 3 tokens need 9.
- **A vocabulary of reasonable size.** Every entry needs its own row in the embedding table of Step 2. The smallest GPT-2's 50,257 entries cost 38.6 million weights.

Large pieces give few tokens but need a huge vocabulary and still miss words. Small pieces cover every text but give many tokens.

## Earlier approaches

### Words

One token per word gives short sequences, but the vocabulary is huge, any word missing from it becomes an unknown `<unk>` token, and related forms such as "run" and "running" share nothing. A vocabulary that saw "Transformer" but not "Transformers" loses the second word entirely.

### Characters

One token per character never meets an unknown word, but for typical English the text becomes about five times longer, and the model has to learn spelling before meaning. The words of "the cat sat" are shorter than average, so it grows less: 11 tokens (t, h, e, space, c, a, t, space, s, a, t) instead of 3, about three and two-thirds times.

| Unit | Vocabulary size | Sequence length | Unknown inputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Word | Hundreds of thousands, and still incomplete | Shortest | Yes |
| Character | A few hundred | About 5 times the word count | No |
| Subword | Chosen in advance, 50,257 for GPT-2 | Close to the word count | No |

## Byte-pair encoding

**Subword** tokenization keeps common words whole and splits rare words into common pieces. **Byte-pair encoding** (BPE, Sennrich et al., 2016) finds those pieces by starting from single characters and repeatedly joining the two pieces that appear next to each other most often.

### An example

Suppose the training text contains only four words, each many times:

| Word | Times it appears | Written as characters |
|---|---|---|
| low | 5 | l o w |
| lower | 2 | l o w e r |
| newest | 6 | n e w e s t |
| widest | 3 | w i d e s t |

To begin, the vocabulary is just the 10 characters that appear: d, e, i, l, n, o, r, s, t and w.

**Round 1.** Count every pair of neighbouring pieces, counting a word as many times as it appears:

| Pair | Found in | Count |
|---|---|---|
| e s | newest (6 times), widest (3 times) | 9 |
| s t | newest (6), widest (3) | 9 |
| w e | newest (6), lower (2) | 8 |
| l o | low (5), lower (2) | 7 |
| o w | low (5), lower (2) | 7 |
| n e, e w | newest (6) | 6 each |
| w i, i d, d e | widest (3) | 3 each |
| e r | lower (2) | 2 |

"e s" is the most common pair (it ties with "s t", and the first one found wins), so "es" becomes a new piece and every "e s" is joined.

**Later rounds.** Count again and join the new most common pair. Six rounds change the four words like this:

| Round | Joins | low | lower | newest | widest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start | | l o w | l o w e r | n e w e s t | w i d e s t |
| 1 | e + s → es | l o w | l o w e r | n e w es t | w i d es t |
| 2 | es + t → est | l o w | l o w e r | n e w est | w i d est |
| 3 | l + o → lo | lo w | lo w e r | n e w est | w i d est |
| 4 | lo + w → low | low | low e r | n e w est | w i d est |
| 5 | n + e → ne | low | low e r | ne w est | w i d est |
| 6 | ne + w → new | low | low e r | new est | w i d est |

The vocabulary now has 16 pieces. The common word "low" is a single token, while the rarer "widest" is still split up. GPT-2 does the same with 50,000 rounds, starting from the 256 possible bytes plus one end-of-text token, for a vocabulary of 50,257.

### Tokenizing a new word

To tokenize new text, split it into characters and apply the joins in order. "lowest" never appeared in the training text, but it can still be built:

| After | lowest |
|---|---|
| Start | l o w e s t |
| Join 1, e + s | l o w es t |
| Join 2, es + t | l o w est |
| Join 3, l + o | lo w est |
| Join 4, lo + w | low est |

"lowest" becomes "low" and "est", sharing its pieces with words the model already knows. Since every character is in the vocabulary, no word is ever unknown. At worst it is spelled out letter by letter.

## Running example

From here on, each step follows one sentence, "the cat sat", through a tiny model whose numbers can be checked by hand. Its vocabulary is eight entries, seven whole words plus an end-of-text marker, so tokenizing is a simple lookup:

| Token | the | cat | sat |
|---|---|---|---|
| ID | 1 | 2 | 3 |

These are the tokens $x_1$, $x_2$ and $x_3$ of Step 0. The target, "on", has ID 4.

```python
vocab = ["<end>", "the", "cat", "sat", "on", "mat", "dog", "ran"]
token_id = {token: i for i, token in enumerate(vocab)}

ids = [token_id[w] for w in "the cat sat".split()]
print(ids)  # [1, 2, 3]
```

## Common questions

**Why does the running example use whole words?** So that every token can be read. Its vocabulary has only 8 entries, and a real tokenizer would split text into subword pieces as in the "lowest" example above. Nothing in the later steps depends on which kind of token is used.

**Does the model see the letters of a token?** No. After this step the model has only the IDs. "cat" is 2 and "sat" is 3, and nothing tells it that both end in "at". Anything it knows about spelling it has to learn from how tokens are used. So if a model misspells a word or cannot count its letters, suspect the tokenization before the transformer.

**How many tokens is a text?** For English and GPT-style tokenizers, a token is about 4 characters, or three-quarters of a word, on average. A 750-word essay is roughly 1,000 tokens.

**Is the vocabulary fixed?** Yes. It is built once, before training, and never changes afterwards, because every entry has its own row in the embedding table of Step 2.

[Step 2](2.32-TF12-transformer-embed.md) turns these three IDs into vectors. WordPiece and Unigram, the subword methods used by BERT and T5, are covered in [Tokenization and Word Embeddings](3.4-NL1-natural-language-processing-tokenization-embeddings.md).
