## Overview

[PCA](2.17-ML12-principal-component-analysis.md) is linear: every output coordinate is a weighted sum of input coordinates, so it can rotate the data and crop it, and nothing else. Two concentric rings have no linear projection that separates them, because no direction has one ring on each side. **t-SNE** and **UMAP** give up on preserving variance and preserve neighborhoods instead. Points that are close in the data should be close in the picture, and everything else is negotiable.

## Example: one point, two neighbors

Point $i$ has two neighbors, $j$ at distance 1 and $k$ at distance 2. Every other point is far enough away to ignore. Four questions take it through t-SNE:

- **(a)** With bandwidth $\sigma_i = 1$, what are the neighbor probabilities $p_{j \mid i}$ and $p_{k \mid i}$?
- **(b)** What are their entropy in bits and the perplexity, and would a larger or a smaller $\sigma_i$ bring the perplexity closer to 2?
- **(c)** The embedding places $j$ and $k$ at the same distances, 1 and 2, from $i$. What are the Student-t similarities $q$, normalized over the two pairs?
- **(d)** What is this point's contribution to the objective, $\sum p \log (p / q)$, in nats, and what would it be if the embedding used the same Gaussian as the data?

<NotebookCard id="nonlinear-dimensionality-reduction" />

The four parts are the four formulas of t-SNE, and the last one shows why the kernels are deliberately mismatched. The explanation answers each in turn.

## Explanation

### Similarities, not distances

t-SNE converts distances into a probability distribution over neighbors. For each point $i$, the chance of picking $j$ as its neighbor is a Gaussian in the distance, normalized over all candidates:

$$
p_{j\mid i} = \frac{\exp\!\big(-\lVert x_i - x_j \rVert^2 / 2\sigma_i^2\big)}
{\sum_{k \ne i} \exp\!\big(-\lVert x_i - x_k \rVert^2 / 2\sigma_i^2\big)} .
$$

$\sigma_i$ carries a subscript: each point gets its own bandwidth. In a dense region a small $\sigma$ is needed to tell neighbors apart, and in a sparse region the same $\sigma$ would put all the probability on the single nearest point. For the example, with $\sigma_i = 1$, the unnormalized values are $e^{-1/2} = 0.6065$ and $e^{-2} = 0.1353$, so $p_{j \mid i} = 0.8176$ and $p_{k \mid i} = 0.1824$. That is part (a).

### Perplexity

You do not set $\sigma_i$ directly. You set the **perplexity**, and t-SNE finds the $\sigma_i$ for each point that achieves it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Perp}(P_i) &= 2^{H(P_i)}, \\
H(P_i) &= -\sum_j p_{j\mid i} \log_2 p_{j\mid i} .
\end{aligned}
$$

That is 2 raised to the [entropy](3.1-PS4-information-theory-core.md) of the neighbor distribution, a smooth effective neighbor count. A distribution spread evenly over 30 points has perplexity 30, and one concentrated on a single neighbor has perplexity 1. For the example, $H = -(0.8176 \log_2 0.8176 + 0.1824 \log_2 0.1824) = 0.6854$ bits and the perplexity is $2^{0.6854} = 1.608$: a little more than one effective neighbor. A larger $\sigma_i$ flattens the distribution and raises it, $\sigma_i = 2$ gives 1.966, and a smaller one lowers it, $\sigma_i = 0.5$ gives 1.017. That is part (b). Perplexity always rises with $\sigma_i$, so a binary search finds the bandwidth for each point. The reference implementation does this, and the implementation below reproduces it. Typical values are 5 to 50, larger for larger datasets, and always below the number of points.

### The crowding problem and the Student-t kernel

Now place the points in two dimensions. The obstacle is geometric. In $\mathbb{R}^d$ at most $d + 1$ points can be mutually equidistant, so ten equidistant points need $\mathbb{R}^9$, and in the plane the limit is three. Real data is not laid out that evenly, but the pressure is the same: a point in high dimensions has many neighbors at moderate distance, and the plane has nowhere to put them all. With a Gaussian in the plane as well, the moderately distant points would be crushed together, the **crowding problem**. The implementation measures it. Ten exactly equidistant points projected onto a random plane come out with a longest distance a median 13.5 times the shortest over 1000 random planes (the best of those 1000 manages 3.9x), and even a regular decagon leaves a factor of 3.24.

t-SNE's fix is a **Student-t distribution with one degree of freedom** in the low-dimensional space:

$$
q_{ij} = \frac{\big(1 + \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert^2\big)^{-1}}
{\sum_{k \ne l} \big(1 + \lVert y_k - y_l \rVert^2\big)^{-1}} .
$$

Its tails are far heavier than a Gaussian's, so reaching a given low similarity takes a much larger distance, and moderately distant points get pushed apart in the embedding, not piled up.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/kernel-tails.svg" alt="Similarity against distance on a log axis from 1 down to 1e-8. The Gaussian falls to 1e-8 by distance 6. The Student-t stays above 1e-2 the whole way.">
The two kernels on a log axis. At distance 4 the Student-t gives 175 times the Gaussian's similarity, at distance 8 a factor of $10^{12}$.
</Figure>

For the example, the unnormalized similarities at distances 1 and 2 are $1/2$ and $1/5$, so $q = (0.7143, 0.2857)$. That is part (c).

### The objective

Symmetrize the conditionals, $p_{ij} = (p_{j\mid i} + p_{i\mid j})/2n$ for $n$ points, so that each pair has a single similarity and all the $p_{ij}$ sum to 1. Then minimize the **KL divergence** between the two distributions by gradient descent on the positions $y_i$:

$$
C = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \Vert Q) = \sum_{i \ne j} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}} .
$$

The example has only point $i$'s conditionals, so it uses them in place of $p_{ij}$. Its two terms are $0.8176 \ln (0.8176 / 0.7143) = 0.1104$ and $0.1824 \ln (0.1824 / 0.2857) = -0.0818$, total 0.0286 nats. With a Gaussian in the plane, $q$ at the same distances would equal $p$ and the cost would be 0. That is part (d), and it shows what the mismatch does. The near neighbor has less similarity in the plane than in the data, so the gradient pulls it closer. The far one has more, so it is pushed away. Summed over a whole dataset, that outward push on the moderately distant points is what relieves the crowding.

This is the **forward** KL, with $P$ in the first argument, and that choice decides how the plots behave. It punishes one kind of mistake much more than the other. If $p_{ij}$ is large and $q_{ij}$ small, the term is huge: separating true neighbors is punished severely. If $p_{ij}$ is small, the term is near zero whatever $q_{ij}$ is: placing distant points anywhere costs almost nothing. So the objective is almost entirely about local structure and says nearly nothing about global structure. That is what the loss asks for, not a flaw in the implementation.

Two details of the optimization also shape the picture. The descent starts from an initial layout, and since the loss barely constrains distant pairs, much of the global arrangement is inherited from that start. For the first 250 iterations, scikit-learn also multiplies every $p_{ij}$ by 12. This **early exaggeration** pulls true neighbors into tight groups with empty space between them, so whole groups can still move past one another before the layout settles.

### How to read the plots

- **Cluster sizes mean nothing.** t-SNE expands dense clusters and contracts sparse ones, because each point's $\sigma_i$ is set by its own local density.
- **Distances between clusters mean nothing.** Those are the near-zero terms in the loss. Two clusters far apart on screen may be adjacent in the data.
- **Perplexity changes the picture.** Sometimes completely. Look at several values. Structure that survives all of them is more trustworthy.
- **Random clouds produce apparent clusters.** Run it on pure noise and you still see blobs.
- **It is a visualization, not a feature transform.** t-SNE learns no mapping: the output coordinates are free parameters optimized directly, so scikit-learn's `TSNE` has no `transform` method. New points can be placed by optimizing their coordinates against the frozen embedding, which openTSNE does, but that is another optimization per batch. Keep PCA for features.
- **Prepare the input.** Standardize the columns, and with thousands of them reduce to about 50 with PCA first. Fix `random_state`, since every run lands differently. `init="pca"`, the default, starts from the PCA layout and keeps more of the global arrangement.
- **Score it instead of eyeballing it.** **Trustworthiness** counts the points that are among a point's $k$ nearest neighbors in the embedding but were not among its $k$ nearest in the data, each weighted by how far down the data's neighbor ranking it really sat. A score of 1 means no false neighbors, and the notebook below implements it to compare PCA, t-SNE and a random layout.

### UMAP

UMAP reaches a similar place from different mathematics, and the practical differences are what matter. It is faster and scales to far larger datasets. It optimizes a cross-entropy with a repulsive term for every pair, where t-SNE is nearly blind to distant pairs. Its between-cluster distances often carry a little more meaning than those of a t-SNE started at random, though still not enough to measure. That edge comes from where UMAP starts, a spectral layout of the neighbor graph, and not from its loss. Kobak and Linderman (2021) showed that t-SNE started from PCA keeps the global structure just as well, and that from a random start UMAP keeps it as poorly as t-SNE. It ships a `transform` for new points. Its knobs are `n_neighbors`, the analog of perplexity (default 15), and `min_dist`, how tightly points may pack (default 0.1). A small `n_neighbors` shows fine local detail and a large one the broad layout. A small `min_dist` gives tight clumps that suit clustering, and a larger one spreads points out. The reading advice is unchanged: cluster sizes and distances between clusters remain unreliable.

How it builds the picture, in five steps:

1. Find each point's $k$ nearest neighbors.
2. Give neighbor $j$ of point $i$ the weight $\exp(-(d_{ij} - \rho_i)/\sigma_i)$, with $\rho_i$ the distance to the nearest neighbor. The nearest neighbor always gets weight 1, so no point is stranded. $\sigma_i$ is chosen so that the weights sum to $\log_2 k$, which adapts to local density the way the perplexity search does.
3. Symmetrize the directed weights as a fuzzy union, $w_{ij} + w_{ji} - w_{ij} w_{ji}$.
4. In the plane, give a pair the similarity $v_{ij} = 1 / (1 + a \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert^{2b})$, a heavy-tailed curve like the Student-t, with $(a, b)$ fitted so that the curve stays near 1 out to `min_dist`.
5. Move the points to minimize the cross-entropy between the two sets of weights:

$$
\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}\log\frac{w_{ij}}{v_{ij}} + (1 - w_{ij})\log\frac{1 - w_{ij}}{1 - v_{ij}} .
$$

The first term pulls neighbors together. The second pushes apart pairs that sit close in the plane without being neighbors in the data, and it is estimated from a few random pairs per edge, the negative sampling of [word2vec](3.4-NL1-natural-language-processing-tokenization-embeddings.md). The implementation computes one point's weights, a fuzzy union, and the $(a, b)$ pairs for three values of `min_dist`.

### Kernel PCA

t-SNE and UMAP build a neighbor graph and optimize a layout. **Kernel PCA** takes a different route, and it is the one to reach for when the answer has to be reproducible or applied to new data. It is PCA run in a feature space you never construct: map each point through some $\phi$ into a space where the structure is linear and do ordinary PCA there. PCA needs only inner products, and a kernel $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \phi(\mathbf{x})^\top \phi(\mathbf{x}')$ supplies them. The recipe is to build the $n \times n$ kernel matrix, center it in feature space, and take its top eigenvectors. The implementation shows it on two concentric rings: linear PCA sits at chance, and a single RBF kernel PCA component separates the rings perfectly, for $\gamma$ between about 2 and 5. Kernel PCA does not remove the need to pick a length scale. It moves that choice into $\gamma$.

Centering needs a trick, because the mean has to be subtracted in feature space and $\phi$ is never formed. The kernel matrix is centered instead. With $\mathbf{1}$ the $n \times n$ matrix whose entries are all $1/n$,

$$
\tilde K = K - \mathbf{1}K - K\mathbf{1} + \mathbf{1}K\mathbf{1}
$$

is the matrix of inner products of the centered features $\phi(\mathbf{x}_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_j \phi(\mathbf{x}_j)$. Multiplying by $\mathbf{1}$ on the left replaces each entry by its column mean, and on the right by its row mean. So $\tilde K$ is $K$ with each entry's column mean and row mean subtracted and the mean of all of $K$ added back.

Take a unit eigenvector $\mathbf{a}$ of $\tilde K$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$. The training points' scores on that component are the entries of $\sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathbf{a}$. A new point's score is $\tilde{\mathbf{k}}^\top \mathbf{a} / \sqrt{\lambda}$, where $\tilde{\mathbf{k}}$ is its row of kernel values with the training points, centered with the training statistics. To center it, subtract the column means of $K$ and the row's own mean, then add back the mean of all of $K$. The two formulas agree, since a training point's centered row is a row of $\tilde K$, and $\tilde K \mathbf{a} / \sqrt{\lambda} = \sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathbf{a}$. The implementation checks both with a linear kernel, where kernel PCA has to reproduce ordinary PCA.

### Choosing between them

| | linear PCA | kernel PCA | t-SNE | UMAP |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| same result every run | yes | yes | no | no |
| transforms new points | yes | yes | not in scikit-learn | yes |
| between-cluster distances | meaningful | partly | loosely, from a PCA start | loosely |
| main knob | none | kernel and $\gamma$ | perplexity | `n_neighbors`, `min_dist` |
| scaling in $n$ | cheap | $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ | slow | fast |
| reach for it to | compress, denoise, take a first look | build nonlinear features for a model | see local cluster structure | see structure at scale |

The division that matters is feature extraction against visualization, not linear against nonlinear. PCA and kernel PCA define a mapping, so a fresh point can be pushed through the same transform. That lets them sit inside a `Pipeline` and be cross-validated honestly. t-SNE and UMAP optimize the positions of the points they were given. Fitting either on the full dataset and then reporting cross-validated accuracy downstream is a leak, because the embedding has seen every fold. The [feature engineering module](1.13-ML1-data-processing-features.md) measures leaks of that kind. To feed a model, use PCA, or kernel PCA when the structure is curved. To look at data, start with UMAP for its speed, and use t-SNE when local cluster structure is the whole question. Kernel PCA eigendecomposes an $n \times n$ matrix at $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ cost, so of the four it is the one that stops scaling first.

## Implementation

### The example in NumPy

The four parts of the example, plus the perplexity at two other bandwidths:

```python
import numpy as np

d = np.array([1.0, 2.0])                                   # distances from point i to its two neighbors
sigma = 1.0
p = np.exp(-d ** 2 / (2 * sigma ** 2))
p = p / p.sum()                                            # p_{j|i} and p_{k|i}
entropy = -(p * np.log2(p)).sum()
print(f"p = {np.round(p, 4)}, entropy {entropy:.4f} bits, perplexity {2 ** entropy:.4f}")
for s in (0.5, 2.0):
    ps = np.exp(-d ** 2 / (2 * s ** 2))
    ps = ps / ps.sum()
    print(f"sigma = {s}: p = {np.round(ps, 4)}, perplexity {2 ** (-(ps * np.log2(ps)).sum()):.4f}")

q = 1 / (1 + d ** 2)                                       # Student-t similarities at the same distances
q = q / q.sum()
print(f"q = {np.round(q, 4)}")
print(f"KL terms {np.round(p * np.log(p / q), 4)}, total {(p * np.log(p / q)).sum():.4f} nats")
qg = np.exp(-d ** 2 / 2)                                   # a Gaussian in the plane would reproduce p exactly
qg = qg / qg.sum()
print(f"with a Gaussian in the plane: KL {(p * np.log(p / qg)).sum():.4f}")
```

```output
sigma = 2.0: p = [0.5927 0.4073], perplexity 1.9657
q = [0.7143 0.2857]
KL terms [ 0.1104 -0.0818], total 0.0286 nats
with a Gaussian in the plane: KL 0.0000
```

### Perplexity by binary search

Perplexity is a smooth neighbor count, and the binary search hits it exactly:

```python
def conditional_p(dists_sq, sigma):
    """Gaussian similarities from one point to all others, normalized."""
    w = np.exp(-dists_sq / (2 * sigma**2))
    return w / w.sum()

def perplexity_of(p):
    nz = p > 0
    H = -np.sum(p[nz] * np.log2(p[nz]))          # Shannon entropy, in bits
    return 2**H

def find_sigma(dists_sq, target, lo=1e-6, hi=1e3, iters=60):
    """Binary search sigma so the neighbor distribution hits the wanted perplexity."""
    for _ in range(iters):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if perplexity_of(conditional_p(dists_sq, mid)) > target:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(200, 10))
d0 = np.sum((X[0] - X[1:]) ** 2, axis=1)         # squared distances from point 0

print(f"{'target':>8}{'sigma found':>14}{'achieved':>11}")
for target in [2, 5, 30, 50]:
    s = find_sigma(d0, target)
    print(f"{target:>8}{s:>14.4f}{perplexity_of(conditional_p(d0, s)):>11.4f}")
```

```output
  target   sigma found   achieved
       2        0.6891     2.0000
       5        0.8186     5.0000
      30        1.0658    30.0000
      50        1.1776    50.0000
```

Larger perplexity needs a wider Gaussian, and $\sigma$ less than doubles to go from 2 effective neighbors to 50. That steep relationship is one reason perplexity is the exposed knob, not $\sigma$. The other is that one perplexity value gives every point its own $\sigma_i$, matched to its local density.

### The crowding problem, measured

Ten points that are exactly equidistant in $\mathbb{R}^9$, projected onto a plane:

```python
def simplex(d):
    """d+1 mutually equidistant points in R^d.

    The d+1 basis vectors of R^(d+1) are mutually equidistant and lie on the
    d-dimensional hyperplane sum(x) = 1. Rotating that hyperplane onto R^d
    preserves every distance, so the result is a regular simplex.
    """
    E = np.eye(d + 1)
    E = E - E.mean(axis=0)
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(E, full_matrices=False)
    return E @ Vt[:d].T

def pairwise(P):
    D = np.sqrt(((P[:, None, :] - P[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1))
    return D[np.triu_indices(len(P), 1)]

for d in [2, 3, 5, 9]:
    dist = pairwise(simplex(d))
    print(f"  R^{d}: {d+1} points, {len(dist):>2} distances, "
          f"min={dist.min():.4f} max={dist.max():.4f} spread={np.ptp(dist):.1e}")

E = np.eye(10)                    # ten equidistant points: the simplex before rotating
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
ratios = []
for _ in range(1000):
    Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(10, 2)))     # an orthonormal basis of a random plane
    flat = pairwise(E @ Q)
    ratios.append(flat.max() / flat.min())
print(f"  projected onto 1000 random planes: median ratio {np.median(ratios):.1f}x, "
      f"best {min(ratios):.1f}x")

angles = 2 * np.pi * np.arange(10) / 10
flat = pairwise(np.c_[np.cos(angles), np.sin(angles)])
print(f"  laid out as a regular decagon    : ratio {flat.max() / flat.min():.2f}x")
```

```output
  R^2: 3 points,  3 distances, min=1.4142 max=1.4142 spread=2.2e-16
  R^3: 4 points,  6 distances, min=1.4142 max=1.4142 spread=2.2e-16
  R^5: 6 points, 15 distances, min=1.4142 max=1.4142 spread=8.9e-16
  R^9: 10 points, 45 distances, min=1.4142 max=1.4142 spread=6.7e-16
  projected onto 1000 random planes: median ratio 13.5x, best 3.9x
  laid out as a regular decagon    : ratio 3.24x
```

All 45 distances are identical to machine precision in $\mathbb{R}^9$ and lose that the moment they reach a plane. PCA picks no better plane here, because a regular simplex has the same variance in every direction. A regular decagon gets the ratio down to $1/\sin 18^\circ = 3.24$, and no layout reaches 1, since at most three points in a plane can be mutually equidistant. That leftover distortion is the pressure the heavy tails relieve.

### UMAP's weights by hand

One point's fuzzy neighborhood, a fuzzy union, and the $(a, b)$ pair behind `min_dist`:

```python
from scipy.optimize import brentq, curve_fit

# 1. One point's fuzzy neighborhood: distances to its k = 5 nearest neighbors.
d = np.array([0.8, 1.0, 1.1, 1.6, 2.4])
k = len(d)
rho = d.min()                                      # the nearest neighbor is always fully connected
excess = lambda sigma: np.exp(-(d - rho) / sigma).sum() - np.log2(k)
sigma = brentq(excess, 1e-6, 100.0)                # the bandwidth that makes the weights sum to log2 k
w = np.exp(-(d - rho) / sigma)
print(f"rho = {rho}, sigma = {sigma:.4f}, weights {np.round(w, 4)}, sum {w.sum():.4f} = log2({k})")

# 2. The graph is directed until the two directions are combined as a fuzzy union.
w_ij, w_ji = 0.6, 0.5
print(f"fuzzy union of 0.6 and 0.5: {w_ij + w_ji - w_ij * w_ji:.2f}")

# 3. The low-dimensional similarity 1 / (1 + a d^(2b)), with (a, b) fitted to min_dist.
def ab_for(min_dist, spread=1.0):
    x = np.linspace(0, 3 * spread, 300)
    target = np.where(x < min_dist, 1.0, np.exp(-(x - min_dist) / spread))
    curve = lambda x, a, b: 1.0 / (1.0 + a * x ** (2 * b))
    (a, b), _ = curve_fit(curve, x, target)
    return a, b

for md in (0.0, 0.1, 0.5):
    a, b = ab_for(md)
    print(f"min_dist {md}: a = {a:.3f}, b = {b:.3f}, similarity at distance 0.5 = {1 / (1 + a * 0.5 ** (2 * b)):.3f}")
```

```output
rho = 0.8, sigma = 0.4369, weights [1.     0.6327 0.5033 0.1603 0.0257], sum 2.3219 = log2(5)
fuzzy union of 0.6 and 0.5: 0.80
min_dist 0.0: a = 1.933, b = 0.790, similarity at distance 0.5 = 0.608
min_dist 0.1: a = 1.577, b = 0.895, similarity at distance 0.5 = 0.687
min_dist 0.5: a = 0.583, b = 1.334, similarity at distance 0.5 = 0.916
```

The nearest neighbor gets weight exactly 1 and the rest fall off from there. The middle row, $a = 1.577$ and $b = 0.895$, is the pair umap-learn uses by default, since its default `min_dist` is 0.1. Raising `min_dist` to 0.5 keeps two points half a unit apart at similarity 0.916, nearly the same place, so the optimization has no reason to pull them closer and clusters come out looser.

### Kernel PCA on two rings

```python
from sklearn.datasets import make_circles
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA, KernelPCA
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

X, y = make_circles(n_samples=400, factor=0.3, noise=0.05, random_state=0)

def separable(features):
    """In-sample accuracy of a linear classifier on these features."""
    f = np.asarray(features).reshape(len(X), -1)
    return LogisticRegression().fit(f, y).score(f, y)

print(f"raw coordinates            : {separable(X):.3f}")
print(f"linear PCA, 2 components   : {separable(PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(X)):.3f}")
print("\nRBF kernel PCA, first component alone:")
for g in (0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20):
    comp1 = KernelPCA(n_components=2, kernel="rbf", gamma=g).fit_transform(X)[:, 0]
    print(f"  gamma {g:<5} {separable(comp1):.3f}")
```

```output
raw coordinates            : 0.497
linear PCA, 2 components   : 0.500

RBF kernel PCA, first component alone:
  gamma 0.5   0.500
  gamma 1     0.510
  gamma 2     1.000
  gamma 5     1.000
  gamma 10    0.755
  gamma 20    0.740
```

Linear PCA sits at chance even with both components. It can only rotate, and no rotation makes a ring linearly separable. At $\gamma = 0.5$ the kernel is too wide and every pair of points looks similar. At $\gamma = 20$ it is too narrow and each point resembles only its immediate neighborhood, so the component stops describing the ring as a whole. The window that works is neither guessable from the data nor stable across datasets.

### Centering the kernel matrix

A linear kernel makes the centering checkable. Its feature map is the identity, so kernel PCA has to reproduce ordinary PCA, on the training points and on new ones. The data sits far from the origin on purpose:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Z = rng.normal(size=(60, 3)) @ np.array([[2.0, 0.0, 0.0], [0.6, 1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 0.3, 0.2]]) + 5.0
Z_new = rng.normal(size=(5, 3)) + 5.0                # data deliberately far from the origin
n, one = len(Z), np.full((len(Z), len(Z)), 1 / len(Z))

K = Z @ Z.T                                          # a linear kernel, so the right answer is plain PCA
Kc = K - one @ K - K @ one + one @ K @ one           # centered in feature space
lam, A = np.linalg.eigh(Kc)
lam, A = lam[::-1][:2], A[:, ::-1][:, :2]            # top two eigenpairs
scores = A * np.sqrt(lam)                            # training points: eigenvector times sqrt(eigenvalue)

pca = PCA(n_components=2).fit(Z)
print(f"centered kernel PCA equals PCA on training points: {np.allclose(np.abs(scores), np.abs(pca.transform(Z)))}")

one_new = np.full((len(Z_new), n), 1 / n)
K_new = Z_new @ Z.T                                  # kernel between new points and training points
K_new_c = K_new - one_new @ K - K_new @ one + one_new @ K @ one   # centered with TRAINING statistics
print(f"and on new points, centered the same way        : {np.allclose(np.abs(K_new_c @ A / np.sqrt(lam)), np.abs(pca.transform(Z_new)))}")

lam_raw, A_raw = np.linalg.eigh(K)                   # skip the centering
top = A_raw[:, -1]
mean_dir = Z @ Z.mean(axis=0) / np.linalg.norm(Z @ Z.mean(axis=0))
print(f"uncentered: top component vs the mean direction, |cosine| = {abs(top @ mean_dir):.4f}")
```

```output
centered kernel PCA equals PCA on training points: True
and on new points, centered the same way        : True
uncentered: top component vs the mean direction, |cosine| = 1.0000
```

Both match PCA, up to the sign of each component, which is arbitrary. Without the centering, the top component is not a direction of variance at all. For data this far from the origin, it points at the mean. scikit-learn's `KernelPCA` centers the training kernel and new points' rows for you.
