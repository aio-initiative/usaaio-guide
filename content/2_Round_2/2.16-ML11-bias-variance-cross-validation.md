## Overview

A model flexible enough to find the pattern is flexible enough to fit the noise. The **bias-variance decomposition** splits a model's average test error into three named pieces and says which one a change in the model moves. **Cross-validation** is how that error is estimated from the data in hand, without touching the test set.

Both are Round 1 material. The fundamentals set in [2026 Round 1](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-1-problems.pdf), Problem 1, asks about bias and variance and what regularization buys. [2026 Round 2, Day 2](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-2-day-2-problems.pdf), Problem 4 gave 200 labeled images, and the first decision was how many to hold out for validation. Every tabular problem ends with a score, and the score is only as honest as the protocol behind it.

## Example: which degree?

Fifty points come from $y = \sin(2\pi x) + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.3^2)$ and $x$ uniform on $[0, 1]$. Polynomials of degree 1, 3, 5 and 9 are fit to them by least squares. Which degree has the highest bias, which the highest variance, and why? The training error falls with every extra degree, so why can it not choose the degree, and what can? And if a fresh test point carries noise of variance 0.09, what is the lowest expected squared error any model can reach on it?

## Explanation

### A fit depends on its training set

Suppose the data come from

$$
y = f(x) + \varepsilon .
$$

$f$ is the true pattern, which we do not know. $\varepsilon$ is noise: it is zero on average, it has variance $\sigma^2$, and no model can predict it. A training set is one random sample of such points. A different sample gives a different fitted model $\hat{f}$. The figure does exactly that, fitting the same kind of model to 25 fresh training sets.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/bias-variance-fits.svg" alt="Three panels, one per polynomial degree. In each, 25 thin fitted curves from 25 different training sets, their average as a bold curve, and the true sine as a dashed curve. Degree 1 gives nearly identical straight lines far from the sine. Degree 3 gives curves close to the sine and to each other. Degree 9 gives curves that swing widely around it.">
The same kind of model fit to 25 training sets of 50 points. Thin lines are the fits, the bold line is their average, and the dashed line is the truth. Degree 1 is consistent and wrong. Degree 9 is right on average and all over the place.
</Figure>

The two failures in the picture have names. **Bias** is how far the average fit sits from the truth. Degree 1 cannot bend, so every one of its fits misses in the same way. **Variance** is how much the fit moves from one training set to the next. Degree 9 has ten free coefficients to a cubic's four, so it has room to chase each sample's noise. Degree 3 has little of either.

### The decomposition

Fix one test input $x$. Three numbers matter there:

- $f$, the true value.
- $\hat{f}$, the model's prediction. It changes with the training set.
- $\bar{f}$, the average of $\hat{f}$ over all training sets.

A new observation at $x$ is $y = f + \varepsilon$. The expected squared error of the prediction, averaged over training sets and over the noise, splits into three parts:

$$
\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f})^2\big]
= \underbrace{\big(f - \bar{f}\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
+ \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{f} - \bar{f})^2\big]}_{\text{variance}}
+ \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}\;}
$$

The bias term is how far the average fit is from the truth. The variance term is how far one fit typically lands from the average fit. The noise term is the part of $y$ that nobody can predict.

The proof is two expansions of a square.

1. Separate the noise. Since $y = f + \varepsilon$, $(y - \hat{f})^2 = (f - \hat{f})^2 + 2\varepsilon(f - \hat{f}) + \varepsilon^2$. The middle term averages to zero, because the noise has mean zero and has nothing to do with the training set. The last term averages to $\sigma^2$.
2. Split what is left around the average fit: $(f - \hat{f})^2 = (f - \bar{f})^2 + 2(f - \bar{f})(\bar{f} - \hat{f}) + (\bar{f} - \hat{f})^2$. The middle term averages to zero again, because $\hat{f}$ averages to $\bar{f}$. The first term is the squared bias and the last averages to the variance.

A more flexible model has less bias, because it can get closer to $f$. It has more variance, because it reacts more to the particular sample. The noise is a property of the data, so no model gets below it. For the example that floor is 0.09. Test error is the sum of the three. As the model gets more flexible it traces a U, lowest where neither bias nor variance dominates. The U-shaped curve is the right mental model for the models in this lesson, but it is not the whole story. Very overparameterized models (networks with far more parameters than data points) often show a *second* descent in test error past the interpolation threshold, where the classical account predicts disaster. This is called double descent.

**Regularization** makes the same trade with a different setting. A ridge penalty pulls the weights toward zero. That adds bias, and in exchange the fit moves less from one training set to the next. A lightly penalized flexible model often beats both the unpenalized fit and a stiffer model. That is the answer the Round 1 question about regularization wants, and the implementation measures it on the degree-9 polynomial. Early stopping and dropout make the same trade in a neural network. **Data augmentation** is often the strongest of all, since it adds genuine information about the invariances the task should respect: a rotated cat is still a cat, and telling the model so is worth more than any penalty.

### Underfitting and overfitting

| | Bias | Variance | Training error | Validation error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Underfitting | high | low | high | high, close to training |
| Overfitting | low | high | low | high, far above training |

The gap between training and validation error is the signal, not the level. An underfit model predicts the same wrong thing on every training set, so both errors are high and close. An overfit model chases its training points and predicts something different off them. Training error cannot choose the degree, because it only falls as the model grows: a degree-9 polynomial through 50 points fits them better than a cubic does, noise included. What can choose is an error measured on data the model did not see.

<ProblemCard id="bv-noise-floor-from-repeats" />

### The split

Hold data back before fitting anything. A **training set** fits the model. A **validation set** chooses the degree, the regularization strength, the number of neighbors: any setting the training error cannot choose. Such a setting is called a **hyperparameter**. A **test set** is touched once, at the end, for the number you report. A test score that was consulted while choosing is a validation score with a misleading name. For classification, split so that each part keeps the class proportions, `stratify=y` in `train_test_split` and `StratifiedKFold` for folds, or a rare class can land entirely on one side. With a 1% positive class and a 100-row fold, the expected positive count is 1, and a random split gives the fold no positives at all with probability $0.99^{100} \approx 0.37$, where its positive-class recall and F1 are $0/0$.

The one rule that goes with the split is **no leakage**: nothing computed from validation or test rows may influence the fit. Standardizing with the mean of the whole dataset, choosing features by looking at every row, and imputing from all the data all break it. Each leaks a little of the held-out rows into training and makes the estimate optimistic. [Feature Engineering](1.13-ML1-data-processing-features.md) has the pipeline that keeps preprocessing inside the training rows.

<ProblemCard id="bv-copies-across-the-split" />

### k-fold cross-validation

One validation split wastes the held-out rows, which never train the model, and its estimate depends on which rows landed there. **$k$-fold cross-validation** fixes both. Shuffle the rows once and cut them into $k$ near-equal folds. For each fold, fit on the other $k - 1$ and score on it. Average the $k$ scores. Every row is scored exactly once and trains the model $k - 1$ times, so the estimate uses all the data and is steadier than one split. $k = 5$ or $k = 10$ is usual. $k = n$ is **leave-one-out**: every row is its own fold. Each fit uses almost all the data, and it costs $n$ fits.

For least squares the $n$ fits collapse into one. The fitted values are $\hat{y} = Hy$, where $H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ is the projection matrix of [Linear Algebra I](1.7-MA1-linear-algebra-i-vectors-matrices-systems-transformations.md), here called the **hat matrix**. Its diagonal entry $h_{ii}$ is the **leverage** of row $i$, and the leave-one-out error at row $i$ is the ordinary residual $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$ scaled up to $e_i / (1 - h_{ii})$. The same shortcut holds for ridge with $H_\lambda = X(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top$, and one singular value decomposition of $X$ gives $H_\lambda$ for every $\lambda$.

<ProblemCard id="bv-loo-of-the-mean" />

Choosing a hyperparameter by cross-validation is a search: score every candidate on the same folds, keep the best, then refit it on all the training rows. `GridSearchCV` does that for a grid of candidates, and the test set is still untouched when it finishes.

Two cautions. The leakage rule applies inside each fold: scalers and feature selection are re-fit on the training folds alone. And shuffling is only right when the order of the rows carries no information. A time series must be split forward in time, and repeated measurements of one subject must stay on one side of the cut, which `GroupKFold` enforces. Otherwise neighbors of every test row sit in training and the score flatters the model. [Time Series](2.20-ML15-time-series.md) has that split.

### More data

The two terms respond to data differently. Variance falls as $n$ grows, because the fit averages over more points. Bias does not, because a line is still a line. So the best amount of flexibility depends on how much data there is. With more data a more flexible model wins, and the implementation ends by measuring that. Error plotted against $n$ is a **learning curve**, and it is how to tell whether more data would help.

## Implementation

### The decomposition, measured

The example, with the expectation over training sets replaced by an average over 300 of them. Each fit is scored on a fixed grid of test inputs:

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda x: np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)
grid = np.linspace(0, 1, 101)                        # the test inputs
noise = 0.3

def fit_many(degree, trials=300, n=50, seed=0):
    """Predictions on the grid from `trials` polynomials, each fit to a fresh training set."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    preds = []
    for _ in range(trials):
        x = rng.uniform(0, 1, n)
        y = f(x) + rng.normal(0, noise, n)
        preds.append(np.polyval(np.polyfit(x, y, degree), grid))
    return np.array(preds)                           # (trials, 101)

print(f"{'degree':>6} {'bias^2':>8} {'variance':>9} {'noise':>6} {'total':>7}")
for degree in (1, 3, 5, 9):
    P = fit_many(degree)
    bias2 = np.mean((P.mean(axis=0) - f(grid)) ** 2)   # average fit against the truth
    var = np.mean(P.var(axis=0))                       # spread of the fits
    print(f"{degree:>6} {bias2:>8.4f} {var:>9.4f} {noise ** 2:>6.2f} {bias2 + var + noise ** 2:>7.4f}")
```

```output
degree   bias^2  variance  noise   total
     1   0.2036    0.0134   0.09  0.3069
     3   0.0049    0.0089   0.09  0.1037
     5   0.0001    0.0161   0.09  0.1062
     9   0.0008    0.2068   0.09  0.2976
```

Bias$^2$ falls from 0.20 at degree 1 to 0.0001 at degree 5 and 0.0008 at degree 9, and variance climbs from 0.01 to 0.21. Degree 3 has the lowest total and degree 5 is close behind. The total is a prediction, and it can be checked by scoring the same 300 fits against fresh noisy targets:

```python
P = fit_many(3)
fresh = f(grid) + np.random.default_rng(5).normal(0, noise, P.shape)
print(f"measured error of 300 degree-3 fits on fresh noisy targets: {np.mean((P - fresh) ** 2):.4f}")
```

```output
measured error of 300 degree-3 fits on fresh noisy targets: 0.1035
```

### Choosing the degree with cross-validation

Now one training set of 50 points, the situation a contest puts you in, and no access to the truth. The loop is the whole algorithm:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.uniform(0, 1, 50)
y = f(x) + rng.normal(0, noise, 50)

def kfold_mse(x, y, degree, k=5, seed=0):
    idx = np.random.default_rng(seed).permutation(len(x))    # shuffle once
    folds = np.array_split(idx, k)                            # k near-equal chunks
    scores = []
    for i in range(k):
        val = folds[i]
        train = np.concatenate([folds[j] for j in range(k) if j != i])
        coef = np.polyfit(x[train], y[train], degree)        # fit on k - 1 folds
        scores.append(np.mean((np.polyval(coef, x[val]) - y[val]) ** 2))
    return np.array(scores)

print(f"{'degree':>6} {'train MSE':>10} {'CV MSE':>8} {'fold std':>9}")
for degree in range(1, 10):
    train_mse = np.mean((np.polyval(np.polyfit(x, y, degree), x) - y) ** 2)
    scores = kfold_mse(x, y, degree)
    print(f"{degree:>6} {train_mse:>10.4f} {scores.mean():>8.4f} {scores.std():>9.4f}")
```

```output
degree  train MSE   CV MSE  fold std
     1     0.3042   0.3322    0.1230
     2     0.3039   0.3392    0.1250
     3     0.0952   0.1185    0.0404
     4     0.0936   0.1233    0.0425
     5     0.0881   0.1178    0.0277
     6     0.0880   0.1284    0.0209
     7     0.0869   0.1418    0.0330
     8     0.0849   0.1500    0.0257
     9     0.0802   0.1417    0.0201
```

Training error falls at every degree and would pick 9. Cross-validation is lowest at degree 5, with degrees 3 and 4 within a fraction of one fold's standard deviation of it. That is the honest reading of a CV table: a plateau of acceptable choices, not a single winner. The 300-set measurement above agrees. Degrees 3 and 5 are within 0.003 of each other there, and 9 is nearly three times worse. Report the mean and the spread together, and prefer the simplest model on the plateau.

The same loop in scikit-learn, to the same digits. These are not the hand-rolled loop's folds, so the degree-3 score moves from 0.1185 to 0.1117:

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

kf = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
model = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(3), LinearRegression())
sk = -cross_val_score(model, x[:, None], y, cv=kf, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")
ours = [np.mean((np.polyval(np.polyfit(x[train], y[train], 3), x[val]) - y[val]) ** 2)
        for train, val in kf.split(x)]
print(f"scikit-learn {sk.mean():.4f}  the loop {np.mean(ours):.4f}")
```

```output
scikit-learn 0.1117  the loop 0.1117
```

`cross_val_score` re-fits the whole pipeline inside every fold, transformer and all, so preprocessing stays leak-free with one line.

### Searching the grid

The search over the degree in one call, then a grid over the degree and a ridge penalty together:

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

search = GridSearchCV(make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(), LinearRegression()),
                      {"polynomialfeatures__degree": range(1, 10)},
                      cv=kf, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error").fit(x[:, None], y)
print(f"degree alone: best {search.best_params_['polynomialfeatures__degree']}, CV MSE {-search.best_score_:.4f}")

params = {"polynomialfeatures__degree": [3, 5, 9], "ridge__alpha": [1e-4, 1e-2, 1.0]}
search = GridSearchCV(make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(), Ridge()), params,
                      cv=kf, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error").fit(x[:, None], y)
print(f"degree and penalty: best {search.best_params_}, CV MSE {-search.best_score_:.4f}")
print(f"{'degree':>6}{'no penalty':>12}" + "".join(f"{a:>10}" for a in params["ridge__alpha"]))
for d in params["polynomialfeatures__degree"]:
    plain = -cross_val_score(make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(d), LinearRegression()),
                             x[:, None], y, cv=kf, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error").mean()
    row = [-m for prm, m in zip(search.cv_results_["params"], search.cv_results_["mean_test_score"])
           if prm["polynomialfeatures__degree"] == d]
    print(f"{d:>6}{plain:>12.4f}" + "".join(f"{v:>10.4f}" for v in row))
```

```output
degree alone: best 5, CV MSE 0.1110
degree and penalty: best {'polynomialfeatures__degree': 9, 'ridge__alpha': 0.0001}, CV MSE 0.1086
degree  no penalty    0.0001      0.01       1.0
     3      0.1117    0.1116    0.2095    0.3222
     5      0.1110    0.1114    0.1487    0.3020
     9      0.1372    0.1086    0.1536    0.2598
```

Degree alone picks 5 on these folds. With the penalty in the grid, the best cell is degree 9 at $\alpha = 10^{-4}$. The unpenalized degree-9 error of 0.1372 falls to 0.1086, below every unpenalized degree: the penalty removed the variance that made degree 9 lose. At $\alpha = 1$ every degree is over-regularized and the error sits far above the best cell. $10^{-4}$ is the smallest penalty in the grid, and for degree 9 the error rises again when the penalty is removed, from 0.1086 to 0.1372, so a finer grid between 0 and $10^{-2}$ could still do a little better. Here it does, barely: $\alpha = 3 \times 10^{-4}$ scores 0.1078, a gain of 0.0008 over the best grid cell. The three best cells, degree 9 at $\alpha = 10^{-4}$ and $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and degree 5 with no penalty, sit within 0.0032 of each other, the plateau again, and the search ends with the test set untouched.

### More data

The totals from the decomposition for degree 3 and degree 9, as the training set grows:

```python
print(f"{'n':>5} {'degree 3':>9} {'degree 9':>9}")
for n in (50, 200, 1000):
    totals = []
    for degree in (3, 9):
        P = fit_many(degree, n=n)
        totals.append(np.mean((P.mean(axis=0) - f(grid)) ** 2) + np.mean(P.var(axis=0)) + noise ** 2)
    print(f"{n:>5} {totals[0]:>9.4f} {totals[1]:>9.4f}")
```

```output
    n  degree 3  degree 9
   50    0.1037    0.2976
  200    0.0968    0.0956
 1000    0.0952    0.0910
```

At 50 points degree 9 is nearly three times worse than degree 3. At 200 it is slightly better, and at 1,000 clearly so: its variance has been averaged away and its lower bias is what remains. The noise floor of 0.09 is where both are heading.
