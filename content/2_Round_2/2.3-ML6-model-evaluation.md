## Overview

A classifier that never flags anything can score 95% accuracy and be useless. On balanced data accuracy is enough. On imbalanced data it mostly reports the class ratio, and imbalance is the normal case, because the class that matters, the disease or the fraud, is the rare one. The **confusion matrix**, **precision** and **recall**, **F1** and **AUC** are the vocabulary for saying what a classifier gets wrong.

Both rounds use that vocabulary. [2026 Round 1](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-1-problems.pdf), Problem 1 asks for F1 as a definition, and Problem 9 graded a kNN model by macro-F1 on a hidden test set. [2026 Round 2, Day 2](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-2-day-2-problems.pdf), Problem 4 graded a three-class image model by macro-F1, so the class handled worst counted as much as the class handled best.

## Example: a screening test

A screening test is run on 1,000 patients, of whom 50 have the disease. It flags 130 patients: 40 of them are sick and 90 are false alarms. It misses the other 10 sick patients. A second test declares everyone healthy. Which of the two is the better screen?

<NotebookCard id="classification-metrics" />

Every number a grader can compute from the first test's predictions comes from four counts: 40 sick patients flagged, 90 healthy patients flagged, 10 sick patients missed and 860 healthy patients passed. The explanation builds each metric from them.

## Explanation

### The confusion matrix

Each prediction on a two-class problem is one of four things. A **true positive** is flagged and positive. A **false positive** is flagged and negative. A **false negative** is positive and not flagged. A **true negative** is negative and not flagged. The four counts in a $2 \times 2$ table are the confusion matrix. For the screen:


|                      | Predicted sick | Predicted healthy |
| -------------------- | -------------- | ----------------- |
| **Actually sick**    | TP = 40        | FN = 10           |
| **Actually healthy** | FP = 90        | TN = 860          |


**Accuracy** is the diagonal over everything, $(40 + 860) / 1000 = 0.90$. The test that declares everyone healthy scores $950 / 1000 = 0.95$, a higher accuracy, while catching no one. When a classifier predicts the majority class, its accuracy is exactly that class's share of the data.

<Warning>

scikit-learn's `confusion_matrix` puts true labels on rows and predictions
on columns, with classes sorted ascending, so for 0/1 labels `C[0, 0]` is
the true negatives and `C[1, 1]` the true positives. Check before reading
numbers off it.

</Warning>

Unpacking the matrix with `.ravel()` hands back the counts in the order `tn, fp, fn, tp`, so the line is `tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y_true, y_pred).ravel()`. Writing `tp, fp, fn, tn` in that unpack silently swaps the true positive and true negative counts, so precision, recall, F1 and specificity come out wrong while accuracy is unchanged, which is why the bug is easy to miss.

### Precision and recall

**Precision** asks what fraction of the flags were right. **Recall** asks what fraction of the positives were caught:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FP} = \frac{40}{130} = 0.308, \\[4pt]
\text{recall} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FN} = \frac{40}{50} = 0.80 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The screen catches 80% of the sick patients, and when it flags someone it is right less than a third of the time. Which number matters depends on which error costs more. A missed cancer costs a life and a false alarm costs a follow-up test, so screening pushes recall. A real email lost to the spam folder is the failure that matters there, so a spam filter pushes precision.

<ProblemCard id="cm-precision-for-the-other-class" />

The two trade off through the **threshold**. A classifier outputs a score, and everything above $t$ is flagged. Lower $t$ and more is flagged: recall can only rise, precision usually falls. Raise $t$ and the reverse. Neither number means much alone.

<ProblemCard id="cm-flags-a-lower-threshold-adds" />

### F1

The **F1 score** is the harmonic mean of the two:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_1 &= \frac{2 \cdot \text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}} \\[4pt]
&= \frac{TP}{TP + \tfrac12(FP + FN)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

For the screen $F_1 = 40 / (40 + 50) = 0.444$. A harmonic mean is pulled toward the smaller of its two numbers. A test that flags everyone has recall 1 and precision $50 / 1000 = 0.05$. Their arithmetic mean is 0.525 and their F1 is $2(0.05)(1) / 1.05 = 0.095$. A good F1 cannot be bought by maxing one of the two and abandoning the other.

For the everyone-healthy test, recall is $0 / 50 = 0$ and F1 is 0. Its 0.95 accuracy says nothing about it as a screen, and the first test, with recall 0.80, is the better one.

<ProblemCard id="cm-which-fix-raises-f1" />

Two relatives. **Specificity**, $TN / (TN + FP)$, is the recall of the negative class, and **balanced accuracy** averages it with recall, so the everyone-healthy test scores 0.5 on it instead of 0.95. $F_\beta$ weights recall $\beta^2$ times as much as precision, $F_\beta = (1 + \beta^2) PR / (\beta^2 P + R)$, and $F_2$ is the usual choice when a miss costs more than a false alarm. $F_{0.5}$ is the counterpart when a false alarm costs more, weighting precision four times as much as recall.

### ROC and AUC

Every metric so far describes one threshold. The **ROC curve** shows all of them. As $t$ sweeps from high to low, plot the true positive rate, which is recall, against the false positive rate $FP / (FP + TN)$. At $t = \infty$ nothing is flagged, the point $(0, 0)$. At $t = -\infty$ everything is, the point $(1, 1)$. In between the curve rises toward the corner $(0, 1)$, and a model that scores at random traces the diagonal. The screen at its current threshold is one point of its curve, $(90 / 950, 0.80) = (0.095, 0.80)$.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/roc-curve.svg" alt="An ROC curve rising above the random diagonal, with the area under it shaded, the operating point of the default threshold marked with a dot, and the perfect corner marked.">
One model's ROC curve. The dot is where the default threshold of 0.5 puts it, and the shaded area is the AUC.
</Figure>

The **AUC**, the area under that curve, is 1 for a perfect ranker and 0.5 for a random one. It is also the probability that a randomly chosen positive scores higher than a randomly chosen negative, a statement about ranking that involves no threshold at all. It needs the scores: hard 0/1 predictions leave one point on the curve, and the area under that is the balanced accuracy.

<ProblemCard id="cm-ranking-a-week-of-claims" />

Under heavy imbalance ROC flatters a model, because the false positive rate divides by the huge number of negatives and barely moves while thousands of false alarms pile up. The **precision-recall curve** divides by the number of flags instead and is the honest picture when positives are rare. Its area is the **average precision**, and a random ranker scores the positive rate on it, not 0.5.

### Are the probabilities trustworthy?

Every metric so far scores the flags or the ranking, not the numbers themselves. A **reliability diagram** checks the numbers: it groups predictions into bins, and for each bin compares the average predicted probability to the actual fraction of positives. Points on the diagonal mean the probabilities can be taken at face value. The [logistic regression](1.27-ML8-linear-logistic-regression.md) lesson calls this calibration and shows a model that fails it.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/ml6-calibration.svg" alt="A reliability diagram with predicted probability on the x-axis and observed frequency on the y-axis, both from 0 to 1. A dashed diagonal runs from the lower left to the upper right. A blue curve labelled calibrated stays close to the diagonal. A terracotta curve labelled overconfident starts above the diagonal at low probabilities, crosses it near 0.5, and falls below it at high probabilities.">
Two models on the same examples. The calibrated one tracks the diagonal, and the overconfident one twists away from it at both ends.
</Figure>

Two common calibration methods are Platt scaling and isotonic regression.

| Method | Main Idea | Tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Platt scaling | Fit a small logistic regression on the model scores | Simple and stable, but assumes a sigmoid-shaped correction |
| Isotonic regression | Fit a flexible non-decreasing calibration curve | More flexible, but needs more calibration data |

Calibration should be learned on held-out calibration data, not the training set and not the final test set.

### More than two classes

With $K$ classes the confusion matrix is $K \times K$, true classes on rows and predictions on columns. Class $k$'s recall is its diagonal entry over its row sum, and its precision is the same entry over its column sum. Treat each class in turn as positive against the rest, compute its precision, recall and F1, then average. The **macro** average weights the $K$ classes equally, so a rare class counts as much as a common one and a model that ignores it is punished. Graders pick it for that reason. The **micro** average pools the counts first, and in single-label classification it equals accuracy, since every wrong prediction is one false positive and one false negative. The **weighted** average weights each class by its number of rows. `classification_report` prints the per-class table, the accuracy, and the macro and weighted averages.

<ProblemCard id="cm-ignored-rare-class" />

### Optimize the metric the grader computes

The metric is part of the problem statement, and three things follow. Choose between models by the graded metric on the validation split, because the best model by accuracy and the best by macro-F1 are often different. Tune the threshold on the validation split, because 0.5 maximizes nothing in particular, and on imbalanced data the F1-best threshold is usually well below it. Weight the rare classes with `class_weight="balanced"`, which scales each class's loss by the inverse of its frequency. The implementation measures all three.

## Implementation

### Every metric from four counts

The screen's four counts, and every two-class metric computed from them. Change the counts:

<EmbeddedPyEditor>
TP, FP, FN, TN = 40, 90, 10, 860                  # change the four counts
accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + FP + FN + TN)
precision = TP / (TP + FP)
recall = TP / (TP + FN)
specificity = TN / (TN + FP)
f1 = 2 * precision * recall / (precision + recall)
f2 = 5 * precision * recall / (4 * precision + recall)
print(f"accuracy {accuracy:.3f}  precision {precision:.3f}  recall {recall:.3f}  specificity {specificity:.3f}")
print(f"F1 {f1:.3f}  F2 {f2:.3f}  balanced accuracy {(recall + specificity) / 2:.3f}")
</EmbeddedPyEditor>

### A real confusion matrix

The breast-cancer logistic model from the regression module, scored on its test rows:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, classification_report, roc_auc_score

data = load_breast_cancer()
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    data.data, data.target, test_size=0.25, random_state=0, stratify=data.target)
model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression()).fit(X_train, y_train)
pred = model.predict(X_test)
scores = model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]

print(confusion_matrix(y_test, pred))
print(classification_report(y_test, pred, target_names=data.target_names, digits=3))
print(f"AUC {roc_auc_score(y_test, scores):.3f}")
```

```output
[[50  3]
 [ 3 87]]
              precision    recall  f1-score   support

   malignant      0.943     0.943     0.943        53
      benign      0.967     0.967     0.967        90

    accuracy                          0.958       143
   macro avg      0.955     0.955     0.955       143
weighted avg      0.958     0.958     0.958       143

AUC 0.995
```

The model makes three false positives and three false negatives out of 143. The macro average, 0.955, is a touch below the accuracy, 0.958, because the smaller class, malignant, scores lower. The AUC of 0.995 says the ranking is nearly perfect.

### When accuracy and macro-F1 disagree

A synthetic problem with 10% positives, where the two metrics pick different models:

```python
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.metrics import f1_score

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=4000, n_features=10, weights=[0.9, 0.1],
                           class_sep=0.8, flip_y=0.03, random_state=0)
Xtr, Xte, ytr, yte = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.5, random_state=0, stratify=y)
print(f"{'model':<22} {'accuracy':>8} {'macro-F1':>8} {'flagged':>8}")
for name, clf in (("all negative", None),
                  ("logistic", LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)),
                  ("logistic, balanced", LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000, class_weight="balanced"))):
    p = np.zeros_like(yte) if clf is None else clf.fit(Xtr, ytr).predict(Xte)
    print(f"{name:<22} {np.mean(p == yte):>8.3f} {f1_score(yte, p, average='macro'):>8.3f} {p.sum():>8}")
print(f"positives in the test half: {yte.sum()}")
```

```output
model                  accuracy macro-F1  flagged
all negative              0.889    0.471        0
logistic                  0.902    0.639       69
logistic, balanced        0.791    0.665      549
positives in the test half: 222
```

The all-negative model has the second-best accuracy and the worst macro-F1. The plain logistic model flags 69 rows against 222 actual positives, and `class_weight="balanced"` flags 549, trading eleven points of accuracy for the best macro-F1 of the three. A grader computing macro-F1 wants the third model. One computing accuracy wants the second.

### Tuning the threshold

The same scores, with the threshold chosen on a validation split and the result reported on a separate test split:

```python
from sklearn.metrics import average_precision_score

Xval, Xte2, yval, yte2 = train_test_split(Xte, yte, test_size=0.5, random_state=0, stratify=yte)
clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(Xtr, ytr)
val_scores, test_scores = clf.predict_proba(Xval)[:, 1], clf.predict_proba(Xte2)[:, 1]

thresholds = np.linspace(0.05, 0.95, 91)
val_f1 = [f1_score(yval, val_scores >= t) for t in thresholds]
best = thresholds[int(np.argmax(val_f1))]
print(f"F1-best threshold on validation: {best:.2f}")
for t in (0.5, best):
    print(f"threshold {t:.2f}: test F1 {f1_score(yte2, test_scores >= t):.3f}  test accuracy {np.mean((test_scores >= t) == yte2):.3f}")
print(f"ROC AUC {roc_auc_score(yte2, test_scores):.3f}  average precision {average_precision_score(yte2, test_scores):.3f}  positive rate {yte2.mean():.3f}")
```

```output
F1-best threshold on validation: 0.23
threshold 0.50: test F1 0.329  test accuracy 0.902
threshold 0.23: test F1 0.482  test accuracy 0.871
ROC AUC 0.853  average precision 0.473  positive rate 0.111
```

The F1-best threshold, 0.23, sits well below 0.5. It raises the test F1 from 0.329 to 0.482 and costs three points of accuracy. The validation rows chose it and the test rows measured it, which is the only order that keeps the reported number honest. The last line scores the ranking: a ROC AUC of 0.853 against a random ranker's 0.5, and an average precision of 0.473 against a random ranker's 0.111.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/threshold-curves.svg" alt="F1 and accuracy on the validation split against the decision threshold from 0.05 to 0.95. Accuracy stays near 0.9 across the range. F1 rises to a peak at threshold 0.23 and falls steadily after, well below its peak by 0.5. Dashed lines mark 0.23 and 0.5.">
The two metrics on the validation split at every threshold. Accuracy barely moves, because the majority class is right whatever the threshold. F1 peaks at 0.23 and has lost nearly a third of its value by 0.5.
</Figure>
