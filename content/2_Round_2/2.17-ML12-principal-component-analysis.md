## Overview

Real datasets rarely fill the space they are stored in. The 1,797 digit images in scikit-learn have 64 pixels each, and 21 directions in that 64-dimensional space carry 90% of their variance. **Principal component analysis** finds the directions of greatest variance, in order, and keeps the first few. The result is a low-dimensional summary that can be plotted, compressed, denoised, or handed to a model in place of the raw columns.

The contest asks for a PCA class built from scratch. [2026 Round 1](https://www.usaaio.org/attachments/past-problems/2026/usaaio-2026-round-1-problems.pdf), Problem 2 asked for the three operations such a class does: normalize the data (subtract the column means), project it onto the components, and compute the residuals. The multiple-choice questions want the one-glance facts: the components are the top eigenvectors of the covariance matrix and the fraction of variance explained is a ratio of eigenvalues.

## Example: four points, two directions

The data is the four points $(0,0)$, $(2,2)$, $(3,1)$ and $(1,3)$. Compute the mean, the centered points and the sample covariance matrix, dividing by $n - 1$. Find the eigenvalues and unit eigenvectors of the covariance matrix and the fraction of variance each component explains. Compute each point's score on the first component, reconstruct each point from that score alone, and compare the total squared residual with $(n - 1)\lambda_2$.

The four points are chosen so that every number comes out exact. The explanation does the four steps in order, and each step is one of the operations a PCA class performs.

## Explanation

### Center first

Stack the $n$ points as the rows of $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ and subtract the column means $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, giving the centered matrix $X_c$. The sample covariance matrix is

$$
\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} X_c^\top X_c \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d},
$$

symmetric, with the variance of each column on its diagonal and the covariance of each pair of columns off it. For the example the mean is $(1.5, 1.5)$, the centered points are $(-1.5,-1.5)$, $(0.5,0.5)$, $(1.5,-0.5)$ and $(-0.5,1.5)$, and

$$
\Sigma = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 5/3 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & 5/3 \end{bmatrix} .
$$

PCA is about variance around the mean. Skipping the centering replaces $\Sigma$ with the second-moment matrix $X^\top X/(n-1)$, whose top eigenvector tends to point at the mean and not along the spread. The implementation shows it. Shift the four points by $(10, 0)$ and skip the centering, and the top direction becomes $(0.99, 0.13)$, aimed at the new mean $(11.5, 1.5)$. The centered data gives $(1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$.

### The direction of most variance

Project a centered point onto a unit vector $\mathbf{w}$ and the coordinate is $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$. Across the data the variance of that coordinate is

$$
\frac{1}{n-1} \sum_i (\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i)^2
= \mathbf{w}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{w} .
$$

PCA asks for the $\mathbf{w}$ of length 1 that makes it largest. A maximum under a constraint is found with a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$, from [Convex Optimization](2.5-DL5-advanced-optimization.md). The constraint is $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w} = 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L} &= \mathbf{w}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{w} - \lambda (\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w} - 1), \\
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \mathcal{L} &= 2 \Sigma \mathbf{w} - 2 \lambda \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\Sigma \mathbf{w} = \lambda \mathbf{w} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last equation says that $\mathbf{w}$ is an **eigenvector** of $\Sigma$: a direction that $\Sigma$ only stretches, by the factor $\lambda$, its **eigenvalue**. At an eigenvector the variance is $\mathbf{w}^\top \Sigma \mathbf{w} = \lambda \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w} = \lambda$. So the first principal component is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue, and the variance along it is that eigenvalue. The second is the top eigenvector orthogonal to the first, and so on. Because $\Sigma$ is symmetric, it has an orthonormal eigenbasis, and its eigenvalues are real. Each eigenvalue is the variance of the data along its eigenvector, so none is negative. They add up to the total variance, $\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)$, so the fraction of variance a component explains is $\lambda_j / \sum_i \lambda_i$.

For the example, $\Sigma$ has eigenvalues 2 and $4/3$, since $\Sigma (1, 1)^\top = (2, 2)^\top$ and $\Sigma (1, -1)^\top = (4/3, -4/3)^\top$, with unit eigenvectors $\mathbf{w}_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$ and $\mathbf{w}_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt{2}$. They sum to $10/3 = 5/3 + 5/3$, the total variance, and the fractions explained are 0.6 and 0.4.

### Scores, reconstruction, residuals

The **scores** are the coordinates of the centered points in the new axes, $Z = X_c W$, where the columns of $W$ are the components kept. Because those columns are orthonormal eigenvectors of $\Sigma$, $\operatorname{Cov}(Z) = W^\top \Sigma W$ is diagonal, so scores on different components are uncorrelated. Keeping $k$ of $d$ components and going back, $\hat{X} = Z W^\top + \boldsymbol{\mu}$, gives the **reconstruction**, the projection of each point onto the $k$-dimensional subspace through the mean. The **residuals** are $X - \hat{X}$. For the example the scores on $\mathbf{w}_1$ are $(x + y)/\sqrt{2}$ of the centered points: $-3/\sqrt{2}$, $1/\sqrt{2}$, $1/\sqrt{2}$, $1/\sqrt{2}$, or −2.121 and three times 0.707. Reconstructing from those alone returns $(0,0)$ and $(2,2)$ exactly, since both lie on the line through the mean in direction $(1,1)$. It maps both $(3,1)$ and $(1,3)$ to $(2,2)$, with residuals $(1,-1)$ and $(-1,1)$. The total squared residual is 4, equal to $(n-1)\lambda_2 = 3 \cdot 4/3$, and the same holds in general. The squared reconstruction error of keeping $k$ components is $(n-1)$ times the sum of the eigenvalues thrown away, so the fraction of variance kept is also the fraction of the data that survives the round trip.

Dividing each score by $\sqrt{\lambda_j}$ gives every component unit variance. That is **whitening**, `PCA(whiten=True)`, and distance-based models downstream, such as k-means or an RBF SVM, sometimes want their inputs that way.

### How many components

The cumulative fraction of variance explained says how much of the data $k$ components keep. On the digits, 10 components keep 0.738, 21 keep 0.90 and 29 keep 0.95 of the variance in 64 pixels.

The three ways to choose $k$: a target fraction such as 0.95, which `PCA(n_components=0.95)` takes directly, the point where the curve flattens, or the accuracy of whatever uses the scores. The implementation measures the last on the digits: 5-nearest neighbors scores 0.967 on 10 components and 0.980 on all 64. Ten numbers per image in place of sixty-four cost 1.3 points.

### Scale

Variance depends on the units. A column in millimeters has a million times the variance of the same column in meters, and PCA on the raw columns follows the variance. On the breast cancer data the raw column `worst area` has variance 323,598 and the smallest column $7 \times 10^{-6}$. The first raw component therefore explains 0.982 of the variance and is made of the two area columns, loadings 0.85 and 0.52. After standardizing every column, the first component explains 0.443 and its largest loadings are 0.26, spread over the concavity measurements. `PCA` centers the data itself and never scales it, so standardize first unless the columns share a unit and their scale is meant to count.

### PCA by the SVD

The singular value decomposition of the centered data, $X_c = U S V^\top$ with $S$ diagonal, gives

$$
\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} X_c^\top X_c = V \left( \frac{S^2}{n-1} \right) V^\top ,
$$

an eigendecomposition of $\Sigma$. The columns of $V$ are the components and $s_j^2 / (n-1)$ are the eigenvalues, so PCA never needs to form $\Sigma$ at all. Libraries use the SVD because forming $X_c^\top X_c$ amplifies rounding error. One thing to expect from any implementation: a component's sign is arbitrary, since $-\mathbf{w}$ is an eigenvector whenever $\mathbf{w}$ is. The class below reproduces scikit-learn's components on the digits to within $10^{-14}$ once signs are ignored, and three of its first ten come out flipped. `TruncatedSVD` is the same decomposition without the centering, for sparse matrices such as word counts.

### What PCA cannot do

Every component is a straight axis, and the scores are a rotation followed by a crop, so PCA only finds linear structure. No projection separates two concentric rings, which is where the [nonlinear module](2.18-ML13-nonlinear-dimensionality-reduction.md) starts. Its headline methods are t-SNE and **UMAP**, and both give up on preserving variance and preserve neighborhoods instead. **UMAP** builds a weighted graph of each point's nearest neighbors and then optimizes a layout whose neighborhood graph matches that graph, so *who is near whom* survives and *how far apart* two groups end up does not. Its knobs `n_neighbors` and `min_dist` set how local "near" is and how tightly points may pack, so cluster sizes and distances between clusters in the picture come from those knobs rather than from the data. PCA is the opposite trade: faithful about global variance, unable to unfold a curved manifold. PCA also never sees the labels. The direction of greatest variance can be the one direction that carries no label information, and a classifier given only the top components never sees the direction it needed.

## Implementation

### The example in NumPy

Center, covariance, eigendecomposition, scores, reconstruction, residual:

```python
import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)

X = np.array([[0, 0], [2, 2], [3, 1], [1, 3]], dtype=float)
mu = X.mean(axis=0)
Xc = X - mu                                                  # 1. center
Sigma = Xc.T @ Xc / (len(X) - 1)                             # 2. covariance
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(Sigma)                           # 3. eigendecomposition, ascending order
order = np.argsort(vals)[::-1]
vals, vecs = vals[order], vecs[:, order]
print("mean", mu)
print("covariance\n", Sigma)
print("eigenvalues", vals, " variance explained", vals / vals.sum())
print("eigenvectors as columns\n", vecs)

z = Xc @ vecs[:, 0]                                          # 4. scores on the first component
recon = np.outer(z, vecs[:, 0]) + mu                         # 5. back to the original coordinates
print("PC1 scores", z)
print("reconstruction from PC1\n", recon)
print("residuals\n", np.round(X - recon, 4) + 0.0)
print("total squared residual", ((X - recon) ** 2).sum(), " (n - 1) * lambda_2 =", 3 * vals[1])

shifted = X + np.array([10.0, 0.0])                          # skip the centering and the top direction aims at the mean
_, v = np.linalg.eigh(shifted.T @ shifted / 3)
top = v[:, -1] * np.sign(v[0, -1])
print("top direction without centering", top, " mean of the shifted data", shifted.mean(axis=0))
```

`np.linalg.eigh` is for symmetric matrices, returns eigenvalues in ascending order, and returns orthonormal eigenvectors as columns. The sort puts the largest first. Forget it and the first component is the direction of least variance, not the widest.

Change the points or the number of components kept:

<EmbeddedPyEditor packages="numpy">
import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)

X = np.array([[0, 0], [2, 2], [3, 1], [1, 3]], dtype=float)   # change the points
k = 1                                                         # components to keep

mu = X.mean(axis=0)
Xc = X - mu
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(Xc.T @ Xc / (len(X) - 1))
order = np.argsort(vals)[::-1]
vals, vecs = vals[order], vecs[:, order]
Z = Xc @ vecs[:, :k]
recon = Z @ vecs[:, :k].T + mu
print("eigenvalues", vals, " variance explained", vals / vals.sum())
print("components as columns\n", vecs)
print("scores\n", Z)
print("total squared residual", round(float(((X - recon) ** 2).sum()), 4),
      " (n - 1) x discarded eigenvalues", round(float((len(X) - 1) * vals[k:].sum()), 4))

### A PCA class

This is that class. `fit` takes the SVD of the centered data, `transform` produces scores, `inverse_transform` reconstructs, and the explained variance comes from the singular values:

```python
class PCAScratch:
    def __init__(self, n_components):
        self.k = n_components

    def fit(self, X):
        self.mean_ = X.mean(axis=0)
        U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X - self.mean_, full_matrices=False)
        variance = s ** 2 / (len(X) - 1)                     # the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix
        self.components_ = Vt[:self.k]                       # one component per row, like scikit-learn
        self.explained_variance_ = variance[:self.k]
        self.explained_variance_ratio_ = variance[:self.k] / variance.sum()
        return self

    def transform(self, X):
        return (X - self.mean_) @ self.components_.T

    def inverse_transform(self, Z):
        return Z @ self.components_ + self.mean_

from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)
ours, theirs = PCAScratch(10).fit(X), PCA(10).fit(X)
print("digits:", X.shape)
print("components, ignoring sign:", np.abs(np.abs(ours.components_) - np.abs(theirs.components_)).max())
print("explained variance ratio: ", np.abs(ours.explained_variance_ratio_ - theirs.explained_variance_ratio_).max())
print("scores, ignoring sign:    ", np.abs(np.abs(ours.transform(X)) - np.abs(theirs.transform(X))).max())
print("sign agreement per component:", np.sign((ours.components_ * theirs.components_).sum(axis=1)))
print("variance explained by the first ten:", np.round(ours.explained_variance_ratio_, 3))
```

### How many components

The cumulative variance on the digits, then a check. The mean squared reconstruction error per image equals the discarded eigenvalues, up to the factor $(n-1)/n$ between the sample covariance and a plain mean of squares:

```python
full = PCAScratch(64).fit(X)
cumulative = np.cumsum(full.explained_variance_ratio_)
for k in [1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 64]:
    print(f"k = {k:>2}: cumulative variance {cumulative[k - 1]:.3f}")
print("components for 90%:", int(np.searchsorted(cumulative, 0.90)) + 1, " for 95%:", int(np.searchsorted(cumulative, 0.95)) + 1)

Z = full.transform(X)
for k in [1, 5, 10, 20]:
    X_hat = Z[:, :k] @ full.components_[:k] + full.mean_
    error = ((X - X_hat) ** 2).sum(axis=1).mean()
    discarded = full.explained_variance_[k:].sum() * (len(X) - 1) / len(X)
    print(f"k = {k:>2}: reconstruction error per image {error:8.3f}   discarded variance {discarded:8.3f}")
```

### PCA before a classifier

The digits split into training and test rows, PCA fitted on the training rows only, and 5-nearest neighbors on the scores:

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0, stratify=y)
for k in [2, 5, 10, 20, 64]:
    p = PCAScratch(k).fit(X_train)                           # fitted on the training rows only
    acc = KNeighborsClassifier(5).fit(p.transform(X_train), y_train).score(p.transform(X_test), y_test)
    print(f"k = {k:>2}: 5-NN test accuracy {acc:.3f}")
```

```output
k =  2: 5-NN test accuracy 0.618
k =  5: 5-NN test accuracy 0.913
k = 10: 5-NN test accuracy 0.967
k = 20: 5-NN test accuracy 0.976
k = 64: 5-NN test accuracy 0.980
```

Two components are a picture, not a classifier. Ten score 0.967 against 0.980 for all sixty-four, and PCA is fitted on the training rows for the same reason a scaler is: its mean and components are learned parameters.
