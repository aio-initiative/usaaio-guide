# 2.34 · TF14 · 4. Q, K, V

> **Goal:** Queries, keys and values from each token's vector.
> **Stage:** Round 2, Core contest depth.

## The problem

After Step 3, every token has a vector that says what it is and where it is. But each vector was made from its own token alone. "sat" knows nothing yet about "cat".

To predict what follows "the cat sat", the model has to combine them. Each token has to pull in information from the earlier tokens that matter to it. For "sat", that is "cat", which says who sat, and not "the".

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-problem-qkv.svg" alt="Three boxes: the, cat and sat. A heavy arrow runs from cat to sat, labelled 3. hands over information. Under sat: 1. says what it is looking for. Under cat: 2. shows what it offers. Under the: not what sat is looking for.">
What has to happen for "sat" to receive information from "cat".
</Figure>

For this to work, a token needs three things:

1. **A way to say what it is looking for.** "sat" is looking for a subject.
2. **A way to show what it offers**, so that others can find it. "cat" should show that it can be a subject, so that "sat" finds it and not "the".
3. **Something to hand over when it is picked.** Once found, "cat" should hand over which animal it is, which need not be the same information that made it a match.

One vector per token cannot do all three well, as the earlier approaches show.

## Earlier approaches

### Comparing the vectors directly

The simplest comparison is the dot product of the two tokens' vectors from Step 3: multiply matching numbers and add. It has two problems.

It works in both directions equally. For "sat", (0.909, −0.416, 1.020, 2.000), and "cat", (0.841, 1.540, 1.010, 1.000):

$$
0.909 \times 0.841 + (-0.416) \times 1.540 + 1.020 \times 1.010 + 2.000 \times 1.000 = 3.154.
$$

"cat" against "sat" is the same sum in a different order, so it is also 3.154. "sat" could not look for "cat" without "cat" looking just as much for "sat".

A token also tends to match itself. The dot product of a vector with itself is the square of its length, and no vector of the same length or shorter can score higher. In the running example, "sat" scores itself 6.040, "cat" 3.154 and "the" 2.493, so most of its attention would go to itself.

Only a longer vector can beat it. (1, 1, 1, 1) scores itself 4, but it scores (2, 2, 2, 2) at 8. This happens once in the running example: "the" has the shortest vector, and it scores "cat" at 3.381 and itself at 3.000. So the plain dot product favours the token itself and tokens with large vectors, whether or not they are relevant.

### A small network for every pair

Bahdanau et al. (2014) scored each pair with a small learned network. The two vectors go in together and one score comes out:

$$
\text{score}(a, b) = v \cdot \tanh(W_1 a + W_2 b).
$$

For example, to score "sat" against "cat" with a width of 4 and 8 hidden values:

1. Multiply the vector of "sat" by $W_1$ and the vector of "cat" by $W_2$, and add the results. That gives 8 hidden values.
2. Pass each through tanh.
3. Multiply the 8 results by the 8 weights of $v$ and add, which gives one score.

Because $W_1$ and $W_2$ are different, "sat" against "cat" and "cat" against "sat" need not give the same score, which fixes the first problem above. But the tanh in the middle means the work cannot be shared between pairs. Each pair needs its own pass through the network:

| Text | Pairs of a token with itself or an earlier token | Passes |
|---|---|---|
| "the cat sat" | 1 + 2 + 3 | 6 |
| 100 tokens | 1 + 2 + … + 100 | 5,050 |
| 1,000 tokens | 1 + 2 + … + 1,000 | 500,500 |

That is about half a million passes for a 1,000-token text. The dot product of Step 5 gets all of those scores from one matrix multiplication.

## Queries, keys and values

A Transformer turns each token's vector into three new vectors, each made with its own table of learned weights.

**The three vectors.** For a token whose vector is $h$, its row of $H$:

$$
\text{query} = h \, W_Q, \qquad \text{key} = h \, W_K, \qquad \text{value} = h \, W_V.
$$

The **query** says what the token is looking for, the **key** says what it offers, and the **value** is what it hands over when it is picked. It is like a library search: the query is what you type, the keys are the catalogue entries, and the values are the books.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-qkv.svg" alt="The vector of sat from Step 3, (0.909, -0.416, 1.020, 2.000), with three arrows leading out of it through the weight tables W Q, W K and W V. Each arrow ends in four numbers split into two for head 1 and two for head 2. The query is (1.020, 2.000) and (-0.416, 2.000). The key is (-0.416, 1.020) and (-0.416, 2.000). The value is (0.909, -0.416) and (1.020, 2.000).">
The three vectors made from the vector of "sat" in the running example, each split between the two heads.
</Figure>

**Why three and not one.** Because queries and keys come from different weights, looking is not symmetric. In head 1 of the running example, the query of "sat" against the key of "cat" gives a dot product of 3.591, but the query of "cat" against the key of "sat" gives only 0.600. So the verb looks for its subject without the subject looking back just as hard. Comparing the token vectors directly could not do this: $a \cdot b$ and $b \cdot a$ are always equal.

**Heads.** The numbers of each vector are split into groups, one per **attention head**. With a width of 4 and 2 heads, each head gets 2 numbers of the query, 2 of the key and 2 of the value. GPT-2 small splits 768 numbers into 12 heads of 64. Step 8 explains why.

## Running example

### The weights

In the running example, each number of a query, key or value simply copies one of the token's four numbers from $H$. Trained weights mix all four.

| | Head 1, first number | Head 1, second number | Head 2, first number | Head 2, second number |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Query copies | Dim 3 | Dim 4 | Dim 2 | Dim 4 |
| Key copies | Dim 2 | Dim 3 | Dim 2 | Dim 4 |
| Value copies | Dim 1 | Dim 2 | Dim 3 | Dim 4 |

### One token, then every token

For example, "sat" is (0.909, −0.416, 1.020, 2.000), so its head 1 query is (1.020, 2.000) and its head 1 key is (−0.416, 1.020). For every token:

| Token | Query, head 1 | Key, head 1 | Value, head 1 | Query, head 2 | Key, head 2 | Value, head 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| the | (0.000, 1.000) | (1.000, 0.000) | (1.000, 1.000) | (1.000, 1.000) | (1.000, 1.000) | (0.000, 1.000) |
| cat | (1.010, 1.000) | (1.540, 1.010) | (0.841, 1.540) | (1.540, 1.000) | (1.540, 1.000) | (1.010, 1.000) |
| sat | (1.020, 2.000) | (−0.416, 1.020) | (0.909, −0.416) | (−0.416, 2.000) | (−0.416, 2.000) | (1.020, 2.000) |

Head 2's queries and keys are identical. Step 8 shows what that does.

<ProblemCard id="tf-the-key-on-matches" />

```python
import numpy as np

H = np.array([[1.000, 1.000, 0.000, 1.000],
              [0.841, 1.540, 1.010, 1.000],
              [0.909, -0.416, 1.020, 2.000]])

# Each column copies one dimension of H; columns 1-2 are head 1, 3-4 head 2.
W_Q = np.zeros((4, 4)); W_Q[2, 0] = W_Q[3, 1] = W_Q[1, 2] = W_Q[3, 3] = 1
W_K = np.zeros((4, 4)); W_K[1, 0] = W_K[2, 1] = W_K[1, 2] = W_K[3, 3] = 1
W_V = np.eye(4)

Q, K, V = H @ W_Q, H @ W_K, H @ W_V
Q1, K1, V1 = Q[:, :2], K[:, :2], V[:, :2]      # head 1
Q2, K2, V2 = Q[:, 2:], K[:, 2:], V[:, 2:]      # head 2
```

## Common questions

**Do the query, key and value depend on the position?** Yes. They are computed from $H$, which holds the token and its position together. "cat" at position 1 and "cat" at position 5 would have different queries, keys and values.

**Why is the value separate from the key?** What a token is found by need not be what it passes on. A key can say "I am a noun that can be a subject", while the value carries which noun it is. With one vector for both, the model could only pass on what it matched on.

**Why are head 2's queries and keys identical?** They were set that way on purpose, to show what goes wrong. When the query and key weights are the same, a token tends to match itself, which is the problem of comparing vectors directly. Step 8 shows the result.

**Do real weights copy single dimensions?** No. Each number of a trained query is a mix of all $d$ numbers of the token vector. The copying here only keeps the arithmetic short.
