## Q, K, V

### The problem

After part 1, every token has a vector that says what it is and where it is. But each vector was made from its own token alone. "sat" knows nothing yet about "cat".

To predict what follows "the cat sat", the model has to combine them. Each token has to pull in information from the earlier tokens that matter to it. For "sat", that is "cat", which says who sat, and not "the".

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-problem-qkv.svg" alt="Three boxes: the, cat and sat. A heavy arrow runs from cat to sat, labelled 3. hands over information. Under sat: 1. says what it is looking for. Under cat: 2. shows what it offers. Under the: not what sat is looking for.">
What has to happen for "sat" to receive information from "cat".
</Figure>

For this to work, a token needs three things:

1. **A way to say what it is looking for.** "sat" is looking for a subject.
2. **A way to show what it offers**, so that others can find it. "cat" should show that it can be a subject, so that "sat" finds it and not "the".
3. **Something to hand over when it is picked.** Once found, "cat" should hand over which animal it is, which need not be the same information that made it a match.

One vector per token cannot do all three well, as the earlier approaches show.

### Earlier approaches

#### Comparing the vectors directly

The simplest comparison is the dot product of the two tokens' vectors from part 1: multiply matching numbers and add. It has two problems.

It works in both directions equally. For "sat", (0.909, −0.416, 1.020, 2.000), and "cat", (0.841, 1.540, 1.010, 1.000):

$$
0.909 \times 0.841 + (-0.416) \times 1.540 + 1.020 \times 1.010 + 2.000 \times 1.000 = 3.154.
$$

"cat" against "sat" is the same sum in a different order, so it is also 3.154. "sat" could not look for "cat" without "cat" looking just as much for "sat".

A token also tends to match itself. The dot product of a vector with itself is the square of its length, and no vector of the same length or shorter can score higher. In the running example, "sat" scores itself 6.040, "cat" 3.154 and "the" 2.493, so most of its attention would go to itself.

Only a longer vector can beat it. (1, 1, 1, 1) scores itself 4, but it scores (2, 2, 2, 2) at 8. This happens once in the running example: "the" has the shortest vector, and it scores "cat" at 3.381 and itself at 3.000. So the plain dot product favours the token itself and tokens with large vectors, whether or not they are relevant.

#### A small network for every pair

Bahdanau et al. (2014) scored each pair with a small learned network. The two vectors go in together and one score comes out:

$$
\text{score}(a, b) = v \cdot \tanh(W_1 a + W_2 b).
$$

For example, to score "sat" against "cat" with a width of 4 and 8 hidden values:

1. Multiply the vector of "sat" by $W_1$ and the vector of "cat" by $W_2$, and add the results. That gives 8 hidden values.
2. Pass each through tanh.
3. Multiply the 8 results by the 8 weights of $v$ and add, which gives one score.

Because $W_1$ and $W_2$ are different, "sat" against "cat" and "cat" against "sat" need not give the same score, which fixes the first problem above. But the tanh in the middle means the work cannot be shared between pairs. Each pair needs its own pass through the network:

| Text | Pairs of a token with itself or an earlier token | Passes |
|---|---|---|
| "the cat sat" | 1 + 2 + 3 | 6 |
| 100 tokens | 1 + 2 + … + 100 | 5,050 |
| 1,000 tokens | 1 + 2 + … + 1,000 | 500,500 |

That is about half a million passes for a 1,000-token text. The dot product in [Scores](#lesson-scores) gets all of those scores from one matrix multiplication.

### Queries, keys and values

A Transformer turns each token's vector into three new vectors, each made with its own table of learned weights.

**The three vectors.** For a token whose vector is $h$, its row of $H$:

$$
\text{query} = h \, W_Q, \qquad \text{key} = h \, W_K, \qquad \text{value} = h \, W_V.
$$

The **query** says what the token is looking for, the **key** says what it offers, and the **value** is what it hands over when it is picked. It is like a library search: the query is what you type, the keys are the catalogue entries, and the values are the books.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-qkv.svg" alt="The vector of sat from part 1, (0.909, -0.416, 1.020, 2.000), with three arrows leading out of it through the weight tables W Q, W K and W V. Each arrow ends in four numbers split into two for head 1 and two for head 2. The query is (1.020, 2.000) and (-0.416, 2.000). The key is (-0.416, 1.020) and (-0.416, 2.000). The value is (0.909, -0.416) and (1.020, 2.000).">
The three vectors made from the vector of "sat" in the running example, each split between the two heads.
</Figure>

**Why three and not one.** Because queries and keys come from different weights, looking is not symmetric. In head 1 of the running example, the query of "sat" against the key of "cat" gives a dot product of 3.591, but the query of "cat" against the key of "sat" gives only 0.600. So the verb looks for its subject without the subject looking back just as hard. Comparing the token vectors directly could not do this: $a \cdot b$ and $b \cdot a$ are always equal.

**Heads.** The numbers of each vector are split into groups, one per **attention head**. With a width of 4 and 2 heads, each head gets 2 numbers of the query, 2 of the key and 2 of the value. GPT-2 small splits 768 numbers into 12 heads of 64. [Multi-head](2.37-TF17-transformer-weighted-sum.md#lesson-multi-head) explains why.

### Running example

#### The weights

In the running example, each number of a query, key or value simply copies one of the token's four numbers from $H$. Trained weights mix all four.

| | Head 1, first number | Head 1, second number | Head 2, first number | Head 2, second number |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Query copies | Dim 3 | Dim 4 | Dim 2 | Dim 4 |
| Key copies | Dim 2 | Dim 3 | Dim 2 | Dim 4 |
| Value copies | Dim 1 | Dim 2 | Dim 3 | Dim 4 |

#### One token, then every token

For example, "sat" is (0.909, −0.416, 1.020, 2.000), so its head 1 query is (1.020, 2.000) and its head 1 key is (−0.416, 1.020). For every token:

| Token | Query, head 1 | Key, head 1 | Value, head 1 | Query, head 2 | Key, head 2 | Value, head 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| the | (0.000, 1.000) | (1.000, 0.000) | (1.000, 1.000) | (1.000, 1.000) | (1.000, 1.000) | (0.000, 1.000) |
| cat | (1.010, 1.000) | (1.540, 1.010) | (0.841, 1.540) | (1.540, 1.000) | (1.540, 1.000) | (1.010, 1.000) |
| sat | (1.020, 2.000) | (−0.416, 1.020) | (0.909, −0.416) | (−0.416, 2.000) | (−0.416, 2.000) | (1.020, 2.000) |

Head 2's queries and keys are identical. [Multi-head](2.37-TF17-transformer-weighted-sum.md#lesson-multi-head) shows what that does.

<ProblemCard id="tf-the-key-on-matches" />

```python
import numpy as np

H = np.array([[1.000, 1.000, 0.000, 1.000],
              [0.841, 1.540, 1.010, 1.000],
              [0.909, -0.416, 1.020, 2.000]])

# Each column copies one dimension of H; columns 1-2 are head 1, 3-4 head 2.
W_Q = np.zeros((4, 4)); W_Q[2, 0] = W_Q[3, 1] = W_Q[1, 2] = W_Q[3, 3] = 1
W_K = np.zeros((4, 4)); W_K[1, 0] = W_K[2, 1] = W_K[1, 2] = W_K[3, 3] = 1
W_V = np.eye(4)

Q, K, V = H @ W_Q, H @ W_K, H @ W_V
Q1, K1, V1 = Q[:, :2], K[:, :2], V[:, :2]      # head 1
Q2, K2, V2 = Q[:, 2:], K[:, 2:], V[:, 2:]      # head 2
```

### Common questions

**Do the query, key and value depend on the position?** Yes. They are computed from $H$, which holds the token and its position together. "cat" at position 1 and "cat" at position 5 would have different queries, keys and values.

**Why is the value separate from the key?** What a token is found by need not be what it passes on. A key can say "I am a noun that can be a subject", while the value carries which noun it is. With one vector for both, the model could only pass on what it matched on.

**Why are head 2's queries and keys identical?** They were set that way on purpose, to show what goes wrong. When the query and key weights are the same, a token tends to match itself, which is the problem of comparing vectors directly. [Multi-head](2.37-TF17-transformer-weighted-sum.md#lesson-multi-head) shows the result.

**Do real weights copy single dimensions?** No. Each number of a trained query is a mix of all $d$ numbers of the token vector. The copying here only keeps the arithmetic short.

The next section, [Scores](#lesson-scores), compares every query with every key.

## Scores

### The problem

After [Q, K, V](#lesson-q-k-v), every token has a query, which says what it is looking for, and a key, which says what it offers. Nothing has been compared yet.

"sat" needs to find out which token matters most to it. To do that, the model compares the query of "sat" with the key of every token and gives each comparison one number, a **score**. A high score means the key is a good match for the query.

| Query of "sat" compared with | Score |
|---|---|
| Key of "the" | ? |
| Key of "cat" | ? |
| Key of "sat" | ? |

This step fills in those numbers, for "sat" and for every other token. The way of scoring has to meet three needs:

- **Cheap.** There is a score for every pair of tokens: 3 × 3 = 9 for "the cat sat", and 1,000 × 1,000 = 1,000,000 per head for a 1,000-token text.
- **All at once.** A GPU is fast when it does one large calculation, not a million small ones.
- **Not too large.** [Mask and softmax](#lesson-mask-and-softmax) turns the scores into weights, and that works badly when the scores are far apart.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-problem-scores.svg" alt="A plot of scores per head against the number of tokens in the text, both on logarithmic scales. The line rises steeply: 9 scores for the 3 tokens of &quot;the cat sat&quot;, and 1,000,000 scores for 1,000 tokens.">
The number of scores grows with the square of the length. A text 10 times longer needs 100 times as many.
</Figure>

### Earlier approaches

#### A small network for every pair

Scoring each pair with its own network works but is slow. A dot product gives every score at once from a single matrix multiplication, which is what GPUs do best.

#### The plain dot product

The dot product of a query and a key is cheap, but its size depends on how many numbers the query and key have. That count is the **head size**: 2 in the running example and 64 in GPT-2.

**More numbers, bigger scores.** A dot product adds one product for every number. With more numbers there are more products to add, so the total tends to be larger. For queries and keys whose numbers are about 1 in size, with either sign, the dot product is typically about the square root of the head size:

| Head size | Typical size of a dot product |
|---|---|
| 2 | 1.4 |
| 16 | 4 |
| 64 | 8 |

**Why big scores are a problem.** [Mask and softmax](#lesson-mask-and-softmax) turns each row of scores into weights that add up to 1, and what matters there is the gap between scores. A small gap shares the weight. A large gap gives everything to one token:

| Two scores | Gap | Weights |
|---|---|---|
| 2 and 0 | 2 | 0.88 and 0.12 |
| 16 and 0 | 16 | 0.9999999 and 0.0000001 |

With a head size of 64, gaps like 16 are common. The token then ignores all but one of the others. Training also stalls, because a small change to the scores no longer changes the weights, so there is nothing to tell the model which way to adjust.

### Scaled dot-product scores

**One score.** The fix is to divide the dot product by its typical size, the square root of the head size. The score of one token against another is the dot product of the first token's query with the second token's key, divided by that:

$$
\text{score} = \frac{\text{query} \cdot \text{key}}{\sqrt{\text{head size}}}.
$$

With a head size of 64 the divisor is 8, so the scores 16 and 0 from the table become 2 and 0, and the weights go back to 0.88 and 0.12. Scores stay around 1 in size whatever the head size.

For example, in head 1 the query of "sat" is (1.020, 2.000) and the key of "cat" is (1.540, 1.010). The dot product is 1.020 × 1.540 + 2.000 × 1.010 = 3.591. The head size is 2, so the divisor is $\sqrt{2} = 1.4142$ and the score is 3.591 ÷ 1.4142 = 2.539.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-scores.svg" alt="The query of sat, (1.020, 2.000), on the left, with an arrow to each of three keys on the right. The key of the, (1.000, 0.000), gets a score of 0.721. The key of cat, (1.540, 1.010), gets the highest score, 2.539, and its arrow is drawn heavier. The key of sat, (-0.416, 1.020), gets 1.142.">
The query of "sat" in head 1 scored against every key. "cat" scores highest.
</Figure>

#### The divisor

**Why a square root.** Suppose each of the products in a dot product is +1 or −1, with equal chance. Positive and negative products partly cancel, so the total is usually much smaller than the head size. With a head size of 4 there are 16 equally likely cases:

| Total | −4 | −2 | 0 | 2 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cases out of 16 | 1 | 4 | 6 | 4 | 1 |

The average of the squared totals is (16 + 4 × 4 + 0 + 4 × 4 + 16) ÷ 16 = 4, so the typical size of the total is $\sqrt{4} = 2$, not 4.

**Any head size.** Squaring a total of $n$ products $x_1, \dots, x_n$ gives $n$ squares and $n(n - 1)$ cross terms, each the product of two different $x$ values:

$$
(x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n)^2 = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots + x_n^2 + (\text{cross terms such as } x_1 x_2).
$$

Every square is 1. A cross term is +1 or −1 with equal chance, so the cross terms average 0, and the squared total averages $n$. For a head size of 4 that is 4 squares and 12 cross terms, and the average of 4 matches the table. The total is therefore typically $\sqrt{n}$, and quadrupling the head size only doubles the scores. The argument needs only that the products are independent of each other, each averaging 0 with a square that averages 1, which is roughly true of queries and keys whose numbers start out random around 0 with a typical size of 1.

**Dividing by the square root.** Dividing every score by $\sqrt{n}$ divides every squared score by $n$, so the squared score averages $n \div n = 1$ for every head size. For the running example's head size of 2, the squared total averages 2, and the divisor is $\sqrt{2} = 1.414$.

**Why not divide by the head size.** That would shrink the scores too far. Take the scores 16 and 0 again, with a head size of 64:

| Divide by | Scores | Weights |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing | 16 and 0 | 0.9999999 and 0.0000001 |
| $\sqrt{64} = 8$ | 2 and 0 | 0.88 and 0.12 |
| 64 | 0.25 and 0 | 0.56 and 0.44 |

Without a divisor one token takes everything. Dividing by 64 makes the two tokens almost equal, so the token could hardly prefer one over the other. Dividing by 8 leaves a clear preference that can still change.

**What the divisor does not do.** It is a fixed number, the same for every pair, and it is not learned. Dividing every score by the same number keeps their order, so the best match is still the best match. Only the gaps between scores change, and with them how sharply the weights favor the top token.

**In real models.** The divisor is set by the head size alone: 8 for GPT-2's heads of 64 numbers and $\sqrt{2} = 1.414$ in the running example. It is right when the numbers in the queries and keys are about 1 in size, which is roughly true at the start of training. After that the model can make its scores larger or smaller by changing its weights, so the divisor mainly gives training a good starting point.

That matters because a one-hot softmax is fatal for *training*, not for the forward pass. The softmax Jacobian is $\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top$, and when $p$ is one-hot, every entry is $\approx0$: $p_i(1-p_i) = 1\cdot0 = 0$ for the winner and $\approx0$ for everyone else. **The gradient vanishes**, and attention stops learning which positions to look at. The scaling keeps the softmax in its responsive region, but it does not guarantee nonzero or healthy gradients.

<ProblemCard id="tf-a-head-whose-queries-are-its-keys" />

#### Every score at once

Stack the queries as the rows of $Q$ and the keys as the rows of $K$. One matrix multiplication then gives every pair:

$$
S = \frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{\text{head size}}}.
$$

Row $i$, column $j$ of $S$ is the score of token $i$'s query against token $j$'s key. For 3 tokens, $S$ is a 3 × 3 table.

<ProblemCard id="tf-which-key-a-query-picks" />

### Running example

#### One row of scores

The query of "sat" in head 1 is (1.020, 2.000). Comparing it with each key:

| Key of | Key | Dot product with (1.020, 2.000) | Divided by 1.4142 |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | (1.000, 0.000) | 1.020 × 1.000 + 2.000 × 0.000 = 1.020 | 0.721 |
| cat | (1.540, 1.010) | 1.020 × 1.540 + 2.000 × 1.010 = 3.591 | 2.539 |
| sat | (−0.416, 1.020) | 1.020 × (−0.416) + 2.000 × 1.020 = 1.615 | 1.142 |

"sat" matches "cat" best: head 1 links the verb to its subject. (The vectors hold more decimals than are shown, so redoing a step from the rounded numbers can differ in the last digit: this dot product comes to 1.616, and head 2's diagonal scores of 2.385 and 2.950 come to 2.384 and 2.951.)

#### Every score in head 1

Doing the same for every row gives head 1's full table of scores:

| Query of | the | cat | sat |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | 0.000 | 0.714 | 0.721 |
| cat | 0.714 | 1.814 | 0.424 |
| sat | 0.721 | **2.539** | 1.142 |

#### Every score in head 2

| Query of | the | cat | sat |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | 1.414 | 1.796 | 1.120 |
| cat | 1.796 | 2.385 | 0.961 |
| sat | 1.120 | 0.961 | **2.950** |

In head 2 the queries and keys are the same, so a token tends to match itself: "cat" and "sat" both score themselves highest. "the" is the exception. Its vector is the shortest, and it scores "cat" at 1.796, above its own 1.414. Scores against later tokens, such as this one, are removed by the mask in [Mask and softmax](#lesson-mask-and-softmax), so "the" still ends up attending only to itself.

```python
import numpy as np

Q1 = np.array([[0.000, 1.000], [1.010, 1.000], [1.020, 2.000]])
K1 = np.array([[1.000, 0.000], [1.540, 1.010], [-0.416, 1.020]])
head_size = 2

S1 = Q1 @ K1.T / np.sqrt(head_size)   # every pair in one multiplication
print(S1.round(3))
```

### Common questions

**Can a score be negative?** Yes. A query of (1, 0) and a key of (−1, 0) have a dot product of −1 and a score of −0.707. A negative score only means a poor match, and the softmax (see [Mask and softmax](#lesson-mask-and-softmax)) turns it into a small weight.

**Is a score a probability?** No. A score has no fixed range and means something only next to the other scores in its row. [Mask and softmax](#lesson-mask-and-softmax) turns each row into weights that add up to 1.

**Is the table symmetric?** No. In head 1, "sat" scores "cat" at 2.539, but "cat" scores "sat" at only 0.424, because queries and keys come from different weights. Head 2's table is symmetric, because its queries and keys are the same.

**Does a token score itself?** Yes. The diagonal of the table is each token against itself: "sat" scores itself 1.142 in head 1.

The next section, [Mask and softmax](#lesson-mask-and-softmax), turns each row of scores into weights.

## Mask and softmax

### The problem

After [Scores](#lesson-scores), each head has a table of scores, one row per token. Two things stand between those scores and usable weights.

**Looking ahead.** In training, the model reads a whole text, and every position predicts its next token in the same run. For "the cat sat on", "the" has to predict "cat", "cat" has to predict "sat", and "sat" has to predict "on". The token to be predicted is right there in the input, one place to the right. If "the" could look at "cat", it would copy the answer and learn nothing. So a position must not see the tokens after it.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-problem-mask.svg" alt="Three rows, one for each position. Position 1 may see the, while cat and sat are drawn dashed, and it must predict cat. Position 2 may see the and cat, while sat is dashed, and it must predict sat. Position 3 may see the, cat and sat, and it must predict on. A note says: solid, may be seen, and dashed, must stay hidden.">
What each position may look at in training. Its target is always the first token it must not see.
</Figure>

**Scores are not weights.** [Weighted sum](2.37-TF17-transformer-weighted-sum.md#lesson-weighted-sum) averages the values of the tokens, and an average needs weights that are never negative and add up to 1. Scores are neither. The row of "sat" in head 1 is 0.721, 2.539 and 1.142, which adds up to 4.402, and a score can be negative. Something has to turn each row into proper weights, while keeping the highest score the highest weight.

### Earlier approaches

#### One prefix at a time

Running the model separately on "the", "the cat", "the cat sat" avoids looking ahead but takes one run per token.

#### Picking the best token

Putting all the weight on the highest score gives valid weights, but small changes to the scores then change nothing, so the model cannot be trained. Scores of 2.0 and 1.9 give weights of 1 and 0, and they stay 1 and 0 until the second score overtakes the first.

### Causal mask and softmax

**The mask.** The **causal mask** hides every score for a later token by setting it to $-\infty$. In the row for "cat", the score against "sat" is hidden, and in the row for "the", both later scores are.

**The softmax.** The **softmax** turns each row of scores $s_1, s_2, \dots$ into weights. Raise $e$ to the power of each score, then divide by the row total:

$$
\text{weight}_i = \frac{e^{s_i}}{e^{s_1} + e^{s_2} + \dots}.
$$

For example, the scores 1, 2 and 3 give $e^1 = 2.718$, $e^2 = 7.389$ and $e^3 = 20.086$, which add up to 30.193. The weights are 2.718 ÷ 30.193 = 0.090, 0.245 and 0.665. A hidden score gives $e^{-\infty} = 0$, so its weight is exactly 0.

<Figure src="assets/module-media/transformer-mask-softmax.svg" alt="Three tables with rows and columns the, cat, sat. The first holds the scores from the Scores section. In the second, after the mask, the three scores above the diagonal are marked hidden. In the third, after the softmax, each row holds weights that add up to 1: 1.000 for the, then 0.250 and 0.750 for cat, then 0.115, 0.709 and 0.175 for sat, with 0 wherever a score was hidden.">
Head 1 of the running example. The mask hides the later tokens, and the softmax turns each row into weights that add up to 1.
</Figure>

**What the softmax keeps.** The weights are positive and add up to 1, and a higher score always gets a higher weight. Only the differences between scores matter: 1, 2, 3 and 11, 12, 13 give the same weights. A score that is higher by 1 gets a weight $e \approx 2.72$ times larger. The weights also change smoothly when the scores change, which is what training needs.

**How fast a weight moves.** Training needs to know how much each weight changes when a score changes, its slope. When one score rises by a small amount, its own weight rises by weight × (1 − weight) times that amount, and every other weight falls by the product of the two weights. For "sat" in head 1, per unit rise in the score of "cat":

| Weight of | the | cat | sat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Change | −0.115 × 0.709 = −0.082 | 0.709 × (1 − 0.709) = 0.206 | −0.175 × 0.709 = −0.124 |

The three changes add up to 0, so the weights still add up to 1. The slope is largest when a weight is near 0.5 and vanishes near 0 or 1. With the scores 2 and 0 from [Scores](#lesson-scores), the top weight 0.88 has a slope of 0.88 × 0.12, about 0.1. With 16 and 0 it is 0.9999999 × 0.0000001, about 0.0000001, so training can barely move the weights, which is why [Scores](#lesson-scores) divides the scores. [Calculus and Gradients](1.9-MA3-calculus-gradients.md) derives these slopes.

**One run for every position.** With the mask in place, the model makes a prediction at every position in one run, and none of them can see its own answer. On "the cat sat" that is 3 predictions from 1 run in place of 3 runs.

**Checking the mask.** A hidden score gets a weight of exactly 0, so the token behind it has no effect at all. Changing "sat" to "ran" shows it. In head 2, the score of "the" against the third token changes from 1.120 to 0.413, but it is hidden either way, so "the" keeps the weights 1, 0 and 0. Every number at the first two positions comes out the same to the last digit, all the way to the predictions in part 5, and only the third row changes. Without the mask, as in BERT, "the" would put 0.232 of its head 2 weight on "sat", and changing the third token would change every row.

**Padding.** Texts in a batch have different lengths, so the shorter ones are filled up with a pad token, and a second mask hides the pads as keys, columns of the table. Padding "the cat" with `<end>` to the length of "the cat sat" adds a pad at the third position. The causal mask already hides that pad from "the" and "cat". Pads at the start, or a model with no causal mask such as [BERT and Masked Language Modeling](2.10-TF2-transformer-architectures-bert-mlm.md), need the padding mask.

<ProblemCard id="tf-a-pad-at-the-start" />

**A row with nothing left.** Every row needs at least one visible score. If all of them are hidden, every term is $e^{-\infty} = 0$, and 0 ÷ 0 is stored as NaN, "not a number". The causal mask never does this, because each token can see itself. Hiding a pad as a query as well as a key does:

| Pad's row in head 1 | the | cat | pad |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pad hidden as a key | 0.498 | 0.502 | 0 |
| Pad hidden as a key and a query | NaN | NaN | NaN |

One NaN spreads through training to every weight of the model in a single step. So pads are hidden as keys only, and code often uses a large negative number such as $-10^9$ in place of $-\infty$. That works only because each row's maximum is subtracted first, as the running example's code does: an all-hidden row becomes all zeros, and the softmax gives equal weights instead of NaN. The positions whose target is a pad are left out of the loss.

### Running example

#### The mask

The scores of head 1 from [Scores](#lesson-scores), with the later tokens hidden:

| Query of | the | cat | sat |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | 0.000 | hidden | hidden |
| cat | 0.714 | 1.814 | hidden |
| sat | 0.721 | 2.539 | 1.142 |

#### The softmax

Row by row:

| Row | $e$ to the power of each score | Row total | Weights |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | $e^{0.000} = 1.000$ | 1.000 | 1.000, 0, 0 |
| cat | $e^{0.714} = 2.042$, $e^{1.814} = 6.135$ | 8.177 | 0.250, 0.750, 0 |
| sat | $e^{0.721} = 2.056$, $e^{2.539} = 12.667$, $e^{1.142} = 3.133$ | 17.856 | 0.115, 0.709, 0.175 |

"sat" puts 12.667 ÷ 17.856 = 70.9% of head 1's attention on "cat". The weights of head 1:

| Weights of | the | cat | sat |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | 1.000 | 0 | 0 |
| cat | 0.250 | 0.750 | 0 |
| sat | 0.115 | **0.709** | 0.175 |

#### Head 2

| Row | $e$ to the power of each score | Row total | Weights |
|---|---|---|---|
| the | $e^{1.414} = 4.112$ | 4.112 | 1.000, 0, 0 |
| cat | $e^{1.796} = 6.025$, $e^{2.385} = 10.859$ | 16.884 | 0.357, 0.643, 0 |
| sat | $e^{1.120} = 3.065$, $e^{0.961} = 2.614$, $e^{2.950} = 19.106$ | 24.785 | 0.124, 0.105, 0.771 |

For "sat", the two heads attend to different tokens:

| Head | the | cat | sat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.115 | **0.709** | 0.175 |
| 2 | 0.124 | 0.105 | **0.771** |

Head 1 looks back at the subject. Head 2 stays on the current token.

```python
import numpy as np

S1 = np.array([[0.000, 0.714, 0.721],
               [0.714, 1.814, 0.424],
               [0.721, 2.539, 1.142]])
T = S1.shape[0]

mask = np.triu(np.ones((T, T), dtype=bool), k=1)   # True for later tokens
S1 = np.where(mask, -np.inf, S1)
S1 = S1 - S1.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)            # subtract each row's max
A1 = np.exp(S1) / np.exp(S1).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
print(A1.round(3))
```

### Common questions

**Why set hidden scores to −∞ and not to 0?** A score of 0 is not "no attention". $e^0 = 1$, so it would still get a weight. If the row of "the" were 0, 0, 0, the softmax would give 0.333 to each token, including the two it must not see. Only $-\infty$ gives a weight of exactly 0.

**Combining masks.** Masks compose by addition, since they are all additive biases on the logits: a causal mask plus a padding mask plus a relative-position bias is one sum.

**Why does "the" put all its weight on itself?** It is the first token, so it is the only one it can see. Its row has one score, and a softmax of one number is always 1.

**Is the mask used when generating?** Yes, the model always runs the same way. When generating, the later tokens do not exist yet, so the mask matters most in training, where the whole text is present and each position must not see its own answer.

**Why $e$ and not another base?** Any base above 1 gives valid weights. Changing the base is the same as multiplying every score by a constant, which the model can learn to do itself. $e$ is the convention, and it makes the derivative simple.
